# Evidence note: childbirth, family formation and teacher exit and return

## 1. Stinebrickner, properly characterised

The paper currently leans on Stinebrickner (2002) alone. Both of his core papers use the National Longitudinal Study of the High School Class of 1972 (NLS-72), following certified teachers from that cohort through the late 1970s and 1980s. The cohort is old, but the contribution is methodological as well as descriptive, and the two papers do different jobs. Recommended characterisation:

### 1.1 Stinebrickner (2001), Journal of Labor Economics: the structural model

- Estimates a dynamic, discrete-choice (structural) model of annual labour supply for certified elementary and secondary teachers: in each period the individual chooses between teaching, non-teaching work and non-participation, with forward-looking behaviour and, unusually for the time, serial correlation in the teacher wage process. [verified via IDEAS/RePEc abstract; article page at University of Chicago Press was paywalled]
- Findings: teaching participation falls substantially with time since certification; previously unavailable marital-status and fertility variables provide "new insight" into that decline, i.e. family formation is a central driver of the participation decline, not just wages; higher-ability (higher test score) certified teachers participate less; the model is used to simulate the participation response of the overall sample and of different teacher types to two kinds of wage increases, finding meaningful but differential wage responsiveness.
- Framing for the paper: the model separates the roles of wages and family formation in the exit decision, so the paper can cite it for the mechanism (fertility shocks reduce the value of market work relative to home time, and wage rises only partially offset this) rather than for cohort-specific magnitudes.

### 1.2 Stinebrickner (2002), Journal of Human Resources: exits and destinations

- Examines the timing of exits from teaching and the reasons for exit, and compares teachers with non-teachers. [verified via IDEAS/RePEc abstract; JSTOR full text paywalled]
- Key findings: approximately 67 per cent of exiting female teachers leave the labour force altogether rather than switch occupations; the presence of a newborn child is "the single most important determinant of exits" for women; studies that control only for the total number of children (rather than the timing of births) will understate the role of fertility in quit decisions. The paper also examines return rates of departing teachers.
- Honest caveat for the text: the NLS-72 cohort finished secondary school in 1972; the labour market, parental leave rights and childcare context have changed substantially, and at least one recent US study (Moyer 2022, section 4) finds that the old gendered return patterns no longer hold.

## 2. Recent evidence (2010s-2020s), by country

### 2.1 England (administrative School Workforce Census, official and quasi-official)

The strongest recent administrative evidence on maternity and teacher exit anywhere in the OECD.

- **DfE School Workforce Census (SWC) analysis of maternity returners** (figures produced by DfE from the SWC, publicised through the Maternity Teacher Paternity Teacher (MTPT) Project's written evidence to the House of Commons Education Committee): between 2016 and 2019 an average of 9,903 teachers began maternity leave each academic year; an average of 1,774 (17-20 per cent) left the profession within the first year after returning, more than double the leaving rate of all female teachers in comparable age brackets; an average of 3,835 left within four years of returning, a cumulative attrition rate of 38.6 per cent. [secondary: figures verified consistently across the parliamentary written evidence record and press reporting (Tes); the underlying DfE ad hoc analysis should be cited directly in the final draft]
- **Women in their thirties are the largest single group of leavers**: more than 9,000 women aged 30-39 left teaching in England in 2022/23, against roughly 3,400 men of similar age; nearly one in four of the ~40,000 leavers that year were women in this age band. Source data: SWC, as compiled in the Missing Mothers report (New Britain Project with the MTPT Project, 2024) and reported by the BBC. [secondary: SWC underlying data; report page verified]
- **NFER, Part-time Teaching and Flexible Working in Secondary Schools** (Sharp, Smith, Worth and Van den Brande, 2019): about one in six secondary teachers would like to reduce their hours; 31 per cent of those who wanted and could afford fewer hours had made no formal request because they expected refusal; NFER's wider Teacher Labour Market series (Worth and colleagues) documents an unmet demand for part-time work concentrated in secondary schools, that teachers who leave for other jobs raise their part-time rate by around 20 percentage points after leaving, and that flexible working is critical for retaining the large cohort of women approaching their mid-thirties. [verified from NFER publication pages]
- **DfE, "6,500 additional teachers" delivery plan (2025)**: explicitly identifies women aged 30-39 as the largest leaver group and responds with funded doubling of full-pay maternity leave for teachers from 4 to 8 weeks; reports 17,274 returners to state-funded schools, the highest in ten years, and a 9 per cent leaver rate. [verified from GOV.UK]
- **SWC official statistics (reporting year 2025)**: 38,600 FTE qualified teachers left the state-funded sector in 2024/25 (8.5 per cent, roughly 1 in 12), down from 40,700 in 2023/24; the SWC methodology explicitly classifies maternity-related career breaks within "out of service" teachers and counts returners annually. [verified from Explore Education Statistics]

### 2.2 United States

- **Brummet, Penner, Pharris-Ciurej and Porter (2025), "After School: An Examination of the Career Paths and Earnings of Former Teachers", Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis** (first published February 2024; also EdWorkingPaper 24-1003): novel linkage of school district administrative records to IRS tax records; most leavers stay in education-related work; roughly 20 per cent of leavers are non-employed post exit; post-exit non-employment and earnings declines are concentrated among voluntary female leavers with children present or a high-earning spouse, "suggesting a role for family in career decisions". This is the closest modern administrative analogue to Stinebrickner's 67 per cent labour-force-exit finding, and it is already in the paper's base. [abstract only; EdWorkingPaper PDF available]
- **NCES, Teacher Attrition and Mobility: Results from the 2021-22 Teacher Follow-up Survey** (Taie and Lewis, 2023, NCES 2024-039): among 2020/21 public school teachers who voluntarily left teaching, 15 per cent gave "other personal life reasons (e.g., health, pregnancy/childcare, caring for family)" as their most important reason, second only to retirement (16 per cent); 66 per cent of leavers who were working said work-life balance was better in their new position. Nationally representative official statistics, but survey-based and not childbirth-specific. [verified from source PDF]
- **Moyer (2022), "Has 'Who Comes Back' Changed? Teacher Reentry 2000-2019", Educational Researcher**: using NLSY97, only about one fifth of teachers who left between 2000 and 2019 subsequently returned, well below the one quarter to one half found for earlier cohorts, and reentry is no longer associated with gender or child-rearing status. Directly relevant: the gendered exit-and-return-around-childbirth pattern of the Stinebrickner era may be weakening in the US. [verified via ERIC record]
- **NCTQ, State of the States: paid parental leave (2025/26)**: only 15 states plus DC guarantee paid parental leave for all teachers; RAND (2024 State of the American Teacher) finds 32 per cent of US teachers have access to paid parental leave versus 46 per cent of comparable working adults. Useful policy-context citations. [secondary/report pages]

### 2.3 Denmark and the Nordic register-data literature

- **Kleven, Landais and Søgaard (2019), "Children and Gender Inequality: Evidence from Denmark", AEJ: Applied**: Danish full-population administrative data 1980-2013; event studies around first birth show a long-run child penalty in earnings of about 20 per cent, operating through participation, hours and wage rates in roughly equal measure; after first birth women disproportionately move into the public sector and "family friendly" firms. [verified from source PDF]
- Implication for the paper: teaching is a destination occupation in this literature (public-sector, family-friendly), not a studied origin; Kleven and co-authors do not report child penalties for teachers specifically, and no teacher-specific child-penalty estimate was found in the cross-country follow-ups (Kleven, Landais, Posch, Steinhauer and Zweimüller 2019).
- **Madsen, Brekke and Fekjær (2023), Work, Employment and Society**: Norwegian administrative records 2003-2013 show childbirth raises women's risk of moving from male-dominated to female-dominated workplaces; again teaching is a destination rather than the studied origin. [abstract only]
- No Nordic register study estimating the effect of childbirth on exit from (and return to) the teaching profession specifically was found (see section 5).

### 2.4 Germany, Austria, Netherlands (official statistics on the part-time margin)

- **Germany, Destatis press release (August 2025)**: in school year 2023/24, 43.1 per cent of the roughly 739,500 teachers at general schools worked part-time; 50.7 per cent of female teachers versus 22.6 per cent of male teachers; the rate has risen each year (40.6 per cent in 2021/22, 42.3 per cent in 2022/23). Women are 73 per cent of the teaching workforce. In Germany the childbirth adjustment happens overwhelmingly on the hours margin (Teilzeit, often within civil-servant status) rather than through occupational exit. [verified from Destatis press pages]
- **Netherlands, CBS**: in primary education only 26 per cent of female teachers worked full-time in 2017 (64 per cent of male teachers), with the share of "large part-time" jobs (20-35 hours) growing since 2003; the Dutch case is the extreme version of adjustment on hours rather than exit. [verified from CBS longread]
- Austria: no teacher-specific childbirth study found; Austrian Social Security Database work on parental leave reforms (Lalive and Zweimüller lineage) covers all employees, not teachers separately.

### 2.5 Australia

- **AITSL, Australian Teacher Workforce Data (ATWD), National Trends: Teacher Workforce (2025 and 2026 editions)**: women are 78 per cent of the teacher workforce; registration-based attrition is low (about 1.25 per cent per year among early-career registrants); 77 per cent of casual relief teachers are women, and 32 per cent of the casual relief workforce are women aged 30-49, with nearly a third of casual relief teachers working effectively full-time hours 2021-2023, consistent with mothers stepping into casual and part-time arrangements rather than leaving education outright. [verified from AITSL pages; interpretation partly via AARE research commentary, April 2026]
- Peer-reviewed: a 2025 Asia Pacific Journal of Education article on why Australian teachers leave and their post-teaching pathways exists (Tandfonline, paywalled) and should be pulled through the library before citing for gender-specific claims. [abstract only, access blocked]

## 3. Evidence on return after career breaks

- **England**: the SWC records returners annually; 17,274 teachers returned to state-funded schools in the latest year (highest in a decade, DfE 2025 delivery plan), and returners have risen since 2021/22 (SWC 2025 release). The MTPT/DfE figures imply that the great majority of maternity-leave takers do come back (roughly 9,900 leaves per year against roughly 1,770 first-year post-return leavers), but that returners then leave at elevated rates: 17-20 per cent in the first year back, about double the comparable female rate, and 38.6 per cent cumulatively within four years. Part-time return protects retention: industry analysis of 150,000 teacher records (The Key Group, 2025) finds 32 per cent of part-time maternity returners left within four years versus 45 per cent of full-time returners; grey literature, use only as corroboration. [mixed verification, as flagged above]
- **United States**: Grissom and Reininger (2012, Education Finance and Policy 7(4): 425-454), using NLSY79 survival analysis, established that a substantial share of exiting teachers eventually return and that family characteristics, including young children at home, delay women's reentry. Elsayed and Roch (2023, Educational Policy 37(2): 279-307), using the Beginning Teacher Longitudinal Study and discrete-time hazard models, find women are 10-12 percentage points more likely than men to return, and note former teachers may account for over a third of entering teachers in some estimates. Against this, Moyer (2022) finds only about one fifth of 2000-2019 leavers returned, with no gender or child-rearing association, so the "she'll be back after the children start school" assumption should not be asserted for recent US cohorts. [abstract-level verification for all three]
- No robust estimate of the distribution of break lengths before return around childbirth specifically was found for any country; the England SWC could support one but no published analysis does so.

## 4. What can honestly be said about the gap

Checked carefully (searches across child-penalty, teacher-attrition and register-data literatures, plus England, Nordic, German, Dutch, Australian and US official sources, August 2026):

1. It remains true that **no published study provides quasi-experimental (event-study) estimates of the effect of childbirth on exit from and return to the teaching profession using administrative register data** in any OECD country. The child-penalty literature (Kleven and co-authors and successors) estimates penalties for all women and documents post-birth moves into public-sector and family-friendly employment, but reports no teacher-specific occupational-exit estimates.
2. However, the paper can no longer claim there is no recent administrative evidence at all. Descriptive administrative evidence now exists: DfE School Workforce Census analyses of maternity returners in England (attrition of 17-20 per cent in the first year back and 38.6 per cent within four years, 2016-2019), and Brummet et al.'s (2025) US school-district-to-IRS linkage showing post-exit non-employment concentrated among voluntary female leavers with children.
3. Suggested wording for the paper: "Descriptive administrative evidence from England and the United States confirms that exits and post-exit non-employment cluster around motherhood, but no study yet exploits register data to estimate the causal effect of childbirth on teachers' exit and subsequent return, in the manner of the child-penalty literature."
4. Additional nuance worth a sentence: recent US survey evidence (Moyer 2022) suggests the gendered return pattern of Stinebrickner's cohort has weakened, and continental European register statistics (Germany, Netherlands) show the childbirth adjustment increasingly operating through part-time hours within teaching rather than exit, so the phenomenon is institution-dependent.
