# Hunting the half-remembered "UCL utility-function meta-analysis" paper

Remembered elements: (a) researcher "from UCL"; (b) built a **utility function** for teachers'
occupational decisions; (c) did a "kind of meta-analysis" comparing coefficients/estimates
**across many existing studies**; (d) concluded that **money/pay matters most**.

Verdict up front: the memory almost certainly points at **Sam Sims (UCL Institute of
Education / CEPEO)** and, most likely, blends **two closely related 2025 papers** by his team
plus (possibly) an older Sims blog for EPI. No single paper matches all four elements exactly;
the two 2025 papers between them match everything.

---

## 1. Best matches

### 1a. Sims, Lowes-Belk & Routledge (2025/2026) — the cross-study synthesis

**Citation:** Sims, S., Lowes-Belk, H., & Routledge, C. (2025). Why do people choose to enter
and exit the teaching profession? An interdisciplinary quantitative synthesis. *British
Educational Research Journal* (article 10.1002/berj.70027; online 2025, issue-paginated 2026).
Open access (CC-BY). https://doi.org/10.1002/berj.70027

**Affiliation:** All three authors at UCL — Sam Sims is at the UCL Institute of Education /
Centre for Education Policy and Equalising Opportunities (CEPEO). Funded by Ambition Institute.

**Method — matches "utility function" + "meta-analysis of coefficients across studies":**
- Adapts Cassar & Meier's (2018, *JEP*) interdisciplinary model of job value into an explicit
  utility model of the decision to teach: *"The value of a (teaching) job depends… positively
  on income… positively on meaning… negatively on costs"*, with income ← salary + pension;
  meaning ← autonomy, mastery, relatedness; costs ← hours, flexibility, leave.
- Tests and refines this model via a **systematic review of 12 previously published job-choice
  (discrete-choice/conjoint) survey experiments from eight countries** — six with serving
  teachers (exit margin), six with non-teachers/potential entrants (entry margin) — comparing
  the estimated effects of each attribute across the studies, frequently expressed in a common
  **salary-equivalent / willingness-to-pay metric**. From the abstract: *"This paper provides a
  conceptual synthesis, distilling what we know into a single interdisciplinary model capturing
  influences on the decision (not) to teach. We then test and refine this model using a
  systematic review of survey experiments on job preferences among both teachers and potential
  teachers. Our final model shows good fit with the data, sheds light on the relative importance
  of different influences on job choice…"*

**Actual headline conclusion — more nuanced than "money matters most":**
All eight attributes in the model (salary, pensions, autonomy, mastery, relatedness, hours,
flexibility, leave) are supported by the data. Pay clearly matters — but the distinctive
finding is about the *structure* of compensation and about non-pay attributes being worth
pay-rise-sized amounts. From the authors' own summary (Ambition Institute, 2025):
- *"Evidence from the US suggests teachers place five times more weight on an immediate
  increase in salary, rather than the equivalent increase in annual pension income upon
  retirement (Johnston, 2021). This suggests that salary increases funded by reduced employer
  pension contributions could significantly reduce teacher shortages."*
- *"Evidence from the US suggests people place equal weight on an extra ten days of paid leave
  and a 7% pay rise (Maestas et al., 2023)."*
- People *"place equal weight on (are indifferent between) increasing pay from 'average' to
  'slightly above average' as they do on gaining access to job-specific training"* (Valet et
  al., 2021) — i.e. *"people were willing to trade salary for access to high-quality training."*
- *"Professional development, flexible working and additional paid leave emerged as stronger
  motivators than previously recognised"*; *"reforms in these areas could be as valuable as
  pay rises."*
- Policy framing: *"While our findings corroborate the importance of intrinsic factors, they
  also clearly demonstrate the importance of extrinsic incentives, such as pay and benefits."*

So: extrinsic rewards (pay, hours, leave) come out very strong relative to what the
self-report literature suggested, and the paper monetises everything against salary — but its
punchline is "several low-cash-cost levers are worth as much as pay rises", not "pay dominates".

### 1b. Sims & Routledge (2025) — the "money matters most" finding

**Citation:** Sims, S., & Routledge, C. (2025). Understanding the decision (not) to become a
teacher: evidence from survey experiments with undergraduates in the UK and US. CEPEO Working
Paper No. 25-15, UCL Centre for Education Policy and Equalising Opportunities, November 2025.
https://repec-cepeo.ucl.ac.uk/cepeow/cepeowp25-15.pdf (RePEc: ucl:cepeow:25-15; funded by
the Gatsby Foundation; summarised by FutureEd as "Why Undergraduates (Don't) Choose to Teach").

**Affiliation:** Both authors UCL (CEPEO).

**Method — matches "utility function", not "across many studies":** Writes down the utility
function explicitly (their Equation 1):
*"Utility = Income (salary) + Meaning (mission, autonomy, competence, connection) − Costs
(hours, flexibility)"*, following Cassar & Meier (2018), then estimates attribute weights and
willingness-to-pay from two **new** conjoint survey experiments with thousands of UK and US
undergraduates (WTP computed with Hole's `wtp` Stata module). It is new data, not a synthesis
of existing coefficients.

**Actual headline conclusion — this is the "money matters most" result:**
- Abstract: *"Contrary to existing research, which is largely based on self-reports, we found
  that extrinsic rewards have the most influence on job choices, even among those who are
  considering teaching."*
- Highlights: *"In stark contrast with existing research, we found that differences in
  extrinsic rewards were consistently more important determinants of undergraduates' job
  preferences than altruistic or intrinsic motives… Undergraduates place particular weight on
  salary and hours, with plausible variations in these job attributes changing the probability
  of respondents selecting a job by 9-15 percentage points."*
- Altruism exists but is small in money terms: respondents would give up only about £1.2k
  (UK) / $1.5k (US) of salary for a job with "significant" rather than small social impact.
- Policy line: *"Policymakers looking to reduce teacher shortages should improve pay and
  reduce workload for teachers."*

### 1c. (Possible contaminant of the memory) Sims (2018) EPI blog

Sims, S. (2018, 9 Oct). *Is pay more important than workload for teacher retention?*
Education Policy Institute blog. Written while Sims was at UCL IOE. It explicitly weighs the
evidence across existing studies and concludes pay matters more: the outside-option pay gap
*"explains (in a statistical sense) almost a third (30 per cent) of the differences in
retention by degree subject"*, while for workload *"the evidence is far weaker than for pay."*
No formal utility function, no common-metric conversion — but it is a UCL researcher comparing
studies and concluding money matters most, and it may be part of what the OECD author recalls.
(Note this predates Sims's later well-known work showing working conditions matter a lot.)

**How the memory maps:** UCL ✓ (all three); utility function ✓ (1a conceptually, 1b as an
explicit equation); comparing coefficients across many existing studies ✓ (1a: 12 experiments,
8 countries, common WTP metric); "money matters most" ~ (1b says extrinsic rewards, led by
salary and hours, dominate; 1a is deliberately more balanced — pay matters and is the numeraire
everything is priced in, but PD/flexibility/leave are "as valuable as pay rises"). The most
likely referent of the memory as stated is the **BERJ synthesis (1a)** with the conclusion
partly imported from **1b** (the two were produced by the same team in the same year and are
often presented together, e.g. in the Ambition Institute review "Why do people enter and exit
teaching?", https://www.ambition.org.uk/why-people-enter-and-exit-teaching/).

## 2. Near-misses and why they don't fit

- **Joshua Fullard (Essex ISER, now Warwick Business School)** — *Labour market expectations
  and occupational choice: evidence from teaching* (SSRN 4384928 / ISER) and related
  discrete-choice-experiment work on UK teachers. Utility/DCE framework ✓, willingness-to-pay ✓,
  but single-study (not cross-study), not UCL, and the conclusion is the **opposite**: he finds
  *"non-pecuniary factors are the most cost-effective method of reducing teacher attrition"*
  (and that preventing cuts to amenities beats adding benefits).
- **Andrew Johnston (2021/2024)** — *Preferences, Selection, and the Structure of Teacher Pay*
  (AEJ: Applied; NBER w33088). A landmark single discrete-choice experiment with US teachers,
  including the salary-vs-pension "5x" result the Sims synthesis quotes. Utility/WTP ✓,
  cross-study ✗, UCL ✗ (UC Merced).
- **Nguyen, Pham, Crouch & Springer (2020)** — *The correlates of teacher turnover: an updated
  and expanded meta-analysis of the literature* (Educational Research Review). Genuine
  meta-analysis of ~120 studies ✓, but US-based authors (Kansas State/Vanderbilt), no utility
  function, and its emphasis is on school organisational factors (discipline, administrative
  support) at least as much as pay.
- **See, Morris, Gorard et al. (2020)** — Durham University reviews of interventions for
  teacher recruitment/retention; conclude financial incentives are among the better-evidenced
  levers (mainly for recruitment). Cross-study ✓, "money works" ✓-ish, but Durham not London,
  and no utility function or common-metric conversion.
- **Peter Dolton (& van der Klaauw 1995 EJ, 1999 ReStat; Handbook of the Economics of
  Education ch. 'Teacher supply', 2006)** — structural occupational-choice/utility models of UK
  teacher supply in which **relative pay strongly drives entry and exit**. Utility function ✓,
  money matters ✓, but these are structural estimates on one dataset plus a literature review,
  not a coefficient-level meta-analysis, and Dolton's affiliations are Newcastle/Royal
  Holloway/Sussex/NIESR, not UCL.
- **Sims & Jerrim (2020)** — *TALIS 2018: teacher working conditions, turnover and attrition*
  (DfE report). UCL ✓, but single-dataset descriptive/regression work, and its thrust is that
  working conditions matter.
- **Jack Worth et al. (NFER)** — teacher labour market annual reports and pay-vs-retention
  modelling (e.g. pay rises' predicted retention effects); policy modelling rather than a
  utility-function meta-analysis; NFER not UCL.
- **EPI pay reviews (Zuccollo et al.)** — cost-effectiveness comparisons of pay policies;
  again not a cross-study utility synthesis.
- **Sims & Benhenda (2022)** — CEPEO WP 22-04, effect of £2,000 retention payments for
  shortage-subject teachers (23% reduction in leaving). UCL ✓ and "money works" ✓, but a single
  quasi-experimental evaluation, not a synthesis.

## 3. Honest caveat

No single paper matches all four remembered elements. In particular, no UCL paper that
synthesises coefficients across many studies concludes flatly that "money matters most":
the Sims-Lowes-Belk-Routledge synthesis (the best fit for the method) deliberately concludes
that several non-pay levers are *as valuable as* pay rises, while the flat "extrinsic
rewards/salary matter most" conclusion comes from the companion Sims & Routledge paper, which
is based on new survey experiments (entry margin only) rather than a cross-study comparison.
If the OECD author insists the paper concluded pay dominates *retention* specifically, the
2018 Sims EPI blog (1c) or Dolton's structural work are the fallback candidates. I could not
access the BERJ full text directly (Wiley 403s automated access; the paper is CC-BY via
https://doi.org/10.1002/berj.70027) — quotes for 1a are taken from the authors' own published
summary (Ambition Institute, 2025) and the indexed abstract, and quotes for 1b from the CEPEO
working-paper PDF.
