## CLAIM A — "The pattern Stinebrickner documented has changed"

Benchmark being contrasted: Stinebrickner (2002, JHR 37(1): 192–216, NLS-72 cohort, US 1970s–80s): roughly two thirds (~67%) of exiting **female** teachers left the labour force altogether rather than switch occupations; a newborn child was "the single most important determinant of exits" for women. (Characterisation as in `maternity_evidence.md`; abstract-level verification.)

### A.1 Best modern figure 1 — Brummet, Penner, Pharris-Ciurej and Porter (2024), US tax-record study

- Citation (Crossref-verified): Brummet, Q., Penner, E.K., Pharris-Ciurej, N. and Porter, S.R. (2024) 'After School: An Examination of the Career Paths and Earnings of Former Teachers', *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, 47(2), pp. 503–531 (published online 26 February 2024; in the 2025 volume). https://doi.org/10.3102/01623737241227906. CC-BY (license registered with Crossref, start date 2024-02-26).
- Data: novel linkage of school district administrative records to IRS tax records; leavers followed up to 8 years after exit, anywhere in the US.
- **Abstract, verbatim** [abstract, publisher metadata via Semantic Scholar/Crossref APIs, 2026-09-16]:
  > "The majority of leavers remain in education and mean earnings are slightly below pre-exit earnings even 8 years later. … Roughly 20% of leavers are unemployed, and the bottom quartile of employed leavers earn less than $20,000 annually. … Post-exit employment and earnings declines are associated with the presence of children or a high-earning spouse for voluntary female leavers, suggesting a role for family-conscious policies for teacher retention."
- **Article body** [indexed article text — SAGE returned 403 to every fetch route tried in this session (HTML, PDF, full-XML text-mining endpoint); no green OA copy exists (Unpaywall/OpenAlex/CES working papers checked). These sentences were recovered from the search index of the SAGE full text and MUST be re-verified against the article before print]:
  > "One year after exiting the school district, roughly 81% of nonretiring leavers are employed," varying by leave reason and gender; "male voluntary leavers have the highest employment rates and female voluntary leavers have the lowest"; "nearly 46% of all leavers mov[e] to another K–12 district."
- Printable contrast: where Stinebrickner found ~two thirds of exiting female teachers leaving employment altogether, the modern IRS-linked evidence shows roughly **80% of non-retiring leavers employed one year after exit** (equivalently, ~20% non-employed), with the family gradient surviving only as a *margin*: non-employment and earnings losses concentrate among voluntary female leavers with children at home or a high-earning spouse.

### A.2 Best modern figure 2 — NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey 2012–13, Table 6 [verified from source]

- Source: Goldring, R., Taie, S. and Riddles, M. (2014) *Teacher Attrition and Mobility: Results From the 2012–13 Teacher Follow-up Survey. First Look* (NCES 2014-077). Washington, DC: NCES. https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2014/2014077.pdf — Table 6, p. 12 ("Total number and percentage distribution of public school teacher leavers, by their current occupational and industry status: 2012–13").
- Verbatim distribution of ALL public school leavers (N = 259,400):

| Current status | % |
|---|---|
| Working for a school/district in K–12 education, but not as a regular classroom teacher | 29.3 |
| Working in K–12 education but not in a school/district | 1.1 ! |
| Working in pre-K or postsecondary education | 2.2 ! |
| Working outside education, incl. military | 7.7 |
| Student at a college or university | 1.9 |
| **Caring for family members** | **9.4** |
| Retired | 38.3 |
| Unemployed | 5.8 |
| Disabled / Other | ‡ |

(! = CV 30–50%, interpret with caution; ‡ = reporting standards not met.)

- Derived (own calculation, state it as such if used): excluding the 38.3% who retired, **about two thirds (40.3/61.7 ≈ 65%) of non-retirement leavers were working a year after exit**, ~15% were out of the labour force caring for family members (9.4/61.7), and ~9% were unemployed. Not published by sex; but the US teaching force is ~77% female, so the aggregate is female-dominated.
- The 2021–22 TFS (NCES 2024-039M, Table A-8, p. A-16 [verified from source]) no longer disaggregates the non-working categories ("Other" 43.0% lumps retired/caring/unemployed together), so **2012–13 Table 6 is the latest TFS with a separate "caring for family members" destination share**.

### A.3 England — DfE/IFF Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders, wave 3 [verified from source]

- Source: IFF Research and IOE, UCL's Faculty of Education and Society (2025) *Working lives of teachers and leaders: wave 3. Research report*. London: Department for Education (June 2025), ch. 13, Figure 13.2 and surrounding text (pp. 119–120). https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68f11c49e8e4040c38a3cfbf/Working_lives_of_teachers_and_leaders_wave_3_-_research_report.pdf
- Verbatim (p. 119): "When asked what they were doing at the time of the survey, **42% of leavers were still working in education, 31% were not currently working at all (including 11% who had retired) and 28% [were] working outside of education**." (Leavers who left the English state sector 2023–24; n = 472.)
- Figure 13.2 detail (read from the chart's data labels; small-n caution): retired 11%, not currently working (other) 10%, maternity/paternity/adoption leave 4%, in education or training 3%, **undertaking caring responsibilities 2%**, volunteering 1%. Independent school 13%, state school non-teaching role 7%, supply teaching 4% (confirmed in text).
- So in England today, roughly **one in five non-retired leavers is out of work, and only ~6% of leavers are out of the labour market for explicitly family reasons (4% parental leave + 2% caring)** — nothing like a two-thirds withdrawal.

### A.4 Claim A verdict

**Supported.** Print against Stinebrickner's two-thirds either (i) Brummet et al.'s ~20% non-employed / ~81% of non-retiring leavers employed one year out (US, IRS records), or (ii) WLTL wave 3's 31% not working including 11% retired (England, survey). Caveats to respect in drafting: Stinebrickner's figure is for *female* leavers early in their careers (retirement negligible); the modern figures are for all leavers, so compare on the non-retirement margin. The gender-specific modern statement (female voluntary leavers have the *lowest* post-exit employment, driven by children/high-earning spouse) rests on Brummet et al.; the "children/high-earning spouse" clause is in the published abstract, the "lowest employment rates" sentence is currently verified only at [indexed article text] level.

## CLAIM B — "Family shocks (relative's illness, care responsibilities) push exits, the more so because teaching offers little flexibility"

### B.1 Survey figures where family/caring reasons rank among leaver motives

**United States, TFS 2021–22** [verified from source] — Taie, S. and Lewis, L. (2023) *Teacher Attrition and Mobility: Results From the 2021–22 Teacher Follow-up Survey to the National Teacher and Principal Survey. First Look* (NCES 2024-039). Full report with tables: https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2024/2024039M.pdf, Table A-7, p. A-14:

- Most important reason for voluntarily leaving K–12 teaching, all teacher leavers (public / private in parentheses):
  - "Because I decided to retire or receive retirement benefits from last year's school system" — **16.1%** (16.6 / 13.3)
  - "**Because of other personal life reasons (e.g., health, pregnancy/childcare, caring for family)**" — **15.0%** (13.5 / **24.4**)
  - "Because I decided to pursue a position other than that of a K–12 teacher" — 13.1% (13.6 / 10.3)
  - "Because I wanted or needed a higher salary" — 9.5% (9.2 / 11.2)
- So the health/pregnancy/childcare/caring-for-family category is the **top non-retirement reason** US leavers give, essentially tied with retirement. The category wording explicitly covers health and caring for family — exactly the "relative's illness / care responsibilities" content of the sentence.

**United States, TFS 2012–13** [verified from source] — NCES 2014-077, Table 5, p. 11: among public school leavers, 9.7% left involuntarily; among reasons rated most important, "**Personal life factors**" 38.4% — by far the largest category (career factors 13.0%, salary/benefits 6.8%!, school factors 6.3%). The report does not decompose the category in the tables; its questionnaire grouping includes pregnancy/child care, health, caring for family, moving. Occupational-status counterpart: 9.4% of all 2012–13 leavers were at home **caring for family members** (Table 6, above).

**Sweden, Statistics Sweden 2023** [verified from source previously; see `sweden_leavers_reasons.md`] — SCB, *Lärare utanför yrket 2022/2023*, Temarapporter 2023:6, Tabellbilaga Tab 15: "**Svårighet att kombinera arbete och familj**" (difficulty combining work and family) cited by **15%** of former teachers as a reason for no longer working as a teacher (women 17%, men 10%; multiple response). **Wording caution:** the Swedish item is about combining work and family, not about a relative's illness or care duties specifically — do not hang the "relative's illness" clause on the Swedish figure.

**England, WLTL wave 3** [verified from source] — Figure 13.1, p. 118: among those who left the English state school sector 2023–24 (n=520), "**Personal reasons**" were an important factor in the decision to leave for **44%**, and "**poor mental and/or physical health**" for **62%** (multiple response; workload 84%, stress/wellbeing 75% top the list). No separate "caring responsibilities" reason item exists in WLTL; caring appears only as a destination (2% of leavers, Figure 13.2).

### B.2 The "(in)flexibility of teaching" — teacher-specific evidence exists

**England, WLTL wave 3 (DfE) [verified from source]:**
- "**Lack of flexible working opportunities**" was cited as an important factor in the decision to leave by **39%** of 2023–24 leavers (Figure 13.1, p. 118) and by **47%** of serving teachers/leaders considering leaving (Table 12.2, p. 115).
- Leavers in work vs those still teaching (pp. 122–124): **62% of leavers in work had a flexible working arrangement vs 46% of those still in English state schools**; home/remote working **33% vs 4%**; late start/early finish at manager's discretion 26% vs 13%; average hours in last full week **37.4 vs 49.3**; 70% of leavers in work reported working at least 5 hours fewer than in their state-school role.
- Verbatim (p. 123): "As well as having fewer contracted hours than in their previous job in the English state school sector, leavers in work were more likely to have flexible working arrangements in place."

**United States, TFS 2021–22 [verified from source]** — NCES 2024-039M, Table A-9, pp. A-17–A-18: working leavers comparing their current occupation with teaching rated "**Ability to balance personal life and work**" better in the current position **65.6%** vs better in teaching 13.0% (not better or worse 21.4%); "Autonomy or control over own work" 60.3% vs 9.8%; "Manageability of workload" 58.2% vs 14.1%.

**England, NFER (2019)** [already verified in `maternity_evidence.md`] — Sharp, Smith, Worth and Van den Brande, *Part-time Teaching and Flexible Working in Secondary Schools* (NFER): ~1 in 6 secondary teachers would like to reduce their hours; **31%** of those who wanted (and could afford) fewer hours had made no formal request because they expected refusal.

**Goldin's "greedy work" framing:** no teacher-specific source found; Goldin's own work centres on pharmacy/law/MBA labour markets. **Do not cite Goldin for teaching specifically.** The inflexibility clause is better carried by DfE WLTL + TFS Table A-9, which are teacher-specific and primary.

### B.3 Claim B verdict

**Supported, with one wording adjustment.** (1) Family/care reasons demonstrably rank high among leaver motives: US TFS 2021–22 puts "other personal life reasons (health, pregnancy/childcare, caring for family)" at 15% of voluntary leavers — the top reason after (and statistically indistinguishable from) retirement; Sweden's work-family item is 15% (17% of women); England's "personal reasons" 44% (multiple response). (2) "Teaching offers little flexibility" is sourceable teacher-specifically: 39% of English leavers name lack of flexible working as an important reason for leaving, leavers' jobs are far more flexible than the schools they left (62% vs 46% flexible arrangements; 33% vs 4% remote), and two thirds of US working leavers say work-life balance is better in their new job. The only unsupported element is the *Swedish* wording as evidence of caring specifically — keep the Swedish figure tied to "combining work and family".

## Safe sentences (print-ready)

**Claim A (US, strongest):**
> The pattern Stinebrickner documented for the 1970s and 1980s — when around two thirds of exiting female teachers withdrew from the labour force altogether, most often around a birth — no longer describes today's leavers. In linked school-district and tax-record data for the United States, roughly 80 per cent of non-retiring leavers are employed one year after exit, and most remain in education; what survives of the old pattern is a margin, with post-exit non-employment concentrated among voluntary female leavers with children at home or a high-earning spouse (Stinebrickner, 2002; Brummet et al., 2024).

*(Before print: re-verify the "one year after exit / 81%" sentence against the EEPA article itself — this session could confirm the published abstract (20% non-employed; family gradient) but not fetch the body text. A safer variant using only the abstract:)*
> …roughly 20 per cent of leavers are not employed after exit, and post-exit non-employment is concentrated among voluntary female leavers with children or a high-earning spouse (Brummet et al., 2024).

**Claim A (England alternative):**
> Among teachers who left the English state sector in 2023/24, 42 per cent were still working in education, 28 per cent were working outside it, and 31 per cent were not working at all — including 11 per cent who had retired and only 6 per cent on parental leave or caring at home (IFF Research and IOE, UCL, 2025).

**Claim A (US survey alternative):**
> In the last US Teacher Follow-up Survey to publish full destinations, fewer than one in ten leavers (9.4 per cent) had left work to care for family members, while retirement accounted for 38 per cent and most of the rest had moved to other jobs, largely within education (Goldring, Taie and Riddles, 2014, Table 6).

**Claim B (reasons):**
> Personal-life shocks remain a leading stated reason for leaving: in the 2021/22 US Teacher Follow-up Survey, 15 per cent of voluntary leavers named "other personal life reasons", such as health, pregnancy and childcare, or caring for family, as their most important reason — second only to retirement (16 per cent) (Taie and Lewis, 2023, Table A-7); in Sweden, 15 per cent of former teachers (17 per cent of women) cited difficulty combining work and family among their reasons for leaving (Statistics Sweden, 2023).

**Claim B (inflexibility):**
> Teaching's inflexibility sharpens these pressures: 39 per cent of leavers from the English state sector cited the lack of flexible working opportunities as an important factor in their decision, and leavers' new jobs were markedly more flexible than the schools they left (62 per cent versus 46 per cent with flexible arrangements; 33 per cent versus 4 per cent working from home) (IFF Research and IOE, UCL, 2025). Likewise, two thirds (66 per cent) of US leavers in work said the ability to balance personal life and work was better in their new position, against 13 per cent who said it had been better in teaching (Taie and Lewis, 2023, Table A-9).
