# Turnover concentration in disadvantaged and rural schools — evidence verification

Verified 14 September 2026. Claim chain: **(1)** teacher turnover/attrition is higher in disadvantaged (high-poverty, low-achieving) and rural schools; **(2)** the exits are driven by working conditions in those schools (discipline climate, leadership, support), not by pupil characteristics per se; **(3)** this concentration coincides with where shortages/vacancies are most acute.

Verification method: bibliographic details via Crossref/Semantic Scholar; findings checked against full-text PDFs where obtainable (Carver-Thomas & Darling-Hammond EPAA PDF; Hanushek/Kain/Rivkin JHR PDF from hanushek.stanford.edu; CCSR report PDF; OECD TALIS 2024, PISA 2022 Vol. II and EAG 2025 full-report PDFs from the OECD content CDN), otherwise against abstracts and publisher/ERIC records. Quoted numbers below were read directly from the source unless flagged.

---

## US candidates

### Hanushek, Kain & Rivkin (2004) — VERIFIED. Use for claim (1).
- **What it shows** (Texas administrative panel, UTD Texas Schools Project): teacher transitions are "much more strongly related to characteristics of the students, particularly race and achievement, than to salary" (abstract). Concrete numbers verified in the JHR text: **almost 20% of teachers in bottom-quartile (achievement) schools leave each year**; a school with 10% more Black students would need roughly 10% higher salaries to neutralise the increased leaving probability; for less experienced female teachers the implied compensating salary differential for teaching in large urban districts' disadvantaged schools is **25–40%**.
- **Caveat for claim (2)**: the paper itself attributes mobility to *student characteristics* — it cannot separate pupils from the conditions that co-vary with pupil intake. Use it for (1), and let Johnson/Kraft/Papay and Simon/Johnson carry (2).
- **Reference**: Hanushek, E. A., J. F. Kain and S. G. Rivkin (2004), "Why Public Schools Lose Teachers", *Journal of Human Resources*, Vol. 39/2, pp. 326-354, https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.XXXIX.2.326. (Q1; also NBER WP 8599.)

### Boyd, Lankford, Loeb & Wyckoff (2005) — VERIFIED. Use for claim (1) + quality-drain nuance.
- **What it shows** (NYC, 1996–2002 administrative data): **27% of first-year teachers in the lowest student-achievement quartile of NYC schools do not return the following year, versus 15% in the highest-achieving quartile**; teachers with strong qualifications (certification-exam scores) are the most likely to leave low-performing schools, so sorting worsens over careers — of new hires in the lowest-achieving schools 28% scored in the bottom quartile of the general-knowledge certification exam, rising to 44% among those still there five years later (verified via Annenberg Institute publication record; AEA full text is paywalled).
- **Reference**: Boyd, D., H. Lankford, S. Loeb and J. Wyckoff (2005), "Explaining the Short Careers of High-Achieving Teachers in Schools with Low-Performing Students", *American Economic Review*, Vol. 95/2 (Papers and Proceedings), pp. 166-171, https://doi.org/10.1257/000282805774669628.

### Johnson, Kraft & Papay (2012) — VERIFIED. Core cite for claim (2).
- **What it shows** (Massachusetts: 25,000-teacher MassTeLLS working-conditions survey merged with demographic and achievement data): "measures of the school environment **explain away much of the apparent relationship between teacher satisfaction and student demographic characteristics**" (abstract, results section); conditions predict both satisfaction and career intentions, and the elements that matter most are *social* — collegial relationships, principal leadership, school culture — not facilities or resources. Favorable conditions also predict higher student achievement growth.
- **Reference**: Johnson, S. M., M. A. Kraft and J. P. Papay (2012), "How Context Matters in High-Need Schools: The Effects of Teachers' Working Conditions on Their Professional Satisfaction and Their Students' Achievement", *Teachers College Record*, Vol. 114/10, pp. 1-39, https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811211401004.

### Simon & Johnson (2015) — VERIFIED (already cited in draft; details confirmed).
- **What it shows**: review of six large-scale quantitative studies of turnover in high-poverty schools through an organisational lens; conclusion (abstract): "**teachers who leave high-poverty schools are not fleeing their students. Rather, they are fleeing the poor working conditions**" — above all school leadership, collegial relationships and elements of school culture — and, unlike student characteristics, these are amenable to policy.
- **Reference**: Simon, N. S. and S. M. Johnson (2015), "Teacher Turnover in High-Poverty Schools: What We Know and Can Do", *Teachers College Record*, Vol. 117/3, pp. 1-36, https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811511700305.

### Carver-Thomas & Darling-Hammond (2019) — VERIFIED. Use for claims (1) and (3).
- **What it shows** (nationally representative SASS/NTPS and TFS data): "**The turnover rate in Title I schools is nearly 50% greater than that in non-Title I schools (16% versus 11%)**"; mathematics/science teacher turnover is nearly 70% greater in Title I schools; in the top quartile of schools by share of students of color, turnover is about 70% higher than in the bottom quartile (raw), and **46% higher controlling for other school and teacher characteristics**; lack of administrative support is among the strongest predictors. Framing sentence (abstract): "Addressing teacher turnover is critical to stemming the country's continuing teacher shortages" — the explicit turnover-shortage link. All numbers read from the EPAA PDF.
- **Note**: EPAA is a solid open-access peer-reviewed journal (not Q1 in most rankings); the authors are LPI-affiliated, but this is the peer-reviewed article, not the LPI advocacy report (that is the 2017 LPI version, DOI 10.54300/454.278 — do not cite that one).
- **Reference**: Carver-Thomas, D. and L. Darling-Hammond (2019), "The trouble with teacher turnover: How teacher attrition affects students and schools", *Education Policy Analysis Archives*, Vol. 27/36, https://doi.org/10.14507/epaa.27.3699.

### Allensworth, Ponisciak & Mazzeo (2009) — VERIFIED. Supporting cite for claims (1)+(2).
- **What it shows** (all CPS teachers, 5 cohorts, plus 2007 survey of ~34,000 teachers): "**About 100 CPS schools suffer from chronically high rates of teacher turnover, losing a quarter or more of their teaching staff every year**"; the typical elementary school lost 51% of its 2002 teachers within four years; almost all chronically unstable schools serve predominantly African American student bodies, **but** workplace conditions — principal leadership, teacher collaboration/collegial relationships, perceptions of parents as partners, safety — strongly predict retention *controlling for* student composition, and largely account for the demographic gradient.
- **Status**: research-consortium report (University of Chicago), not journal peer review — high credibility, widely cited (ERIC ED505882). Use as companion to Simon & Johnson rather than a headline cite.
- **Reference**: Allensworth, E., S. Ponisciak and C. Mazzeo (2009), *The Schools Teachers Leave: Teacher Mobility in Chicago Public Schools*, Consortium on Chicago School Research, University of Chicago, Chicago, https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/schools-teachers-leave-teacher-mobility-chicago-public-schools (ERIC ED505882).

### Ronfeldt, Loeb & Wyckoff (2013) — VERIFIED (already cited; details confirmed). Consequences, not drivers.
- **What it shows** (850,000 NYC grade 4-5 student-year observations over 8 years; school-by-grade turnover with fixed effects): students in grade levels with higher turnover score lower in ELA and math, and "**these effects are particularly strong in schools with more low-performing and Black students**"; there is a disruptive effect beyond changes in teacher quality composition.
- **Fit**: this establishes why concentrated turnover *matters* (harm falls on the same disadvantaged schools); it is not evidence on the level or drivers of turnover. Keep it, but do not use it to document concentration itself.
- **Reference**: Ronfeldt, M., S. Loeb and J. Wyckoff (2013), "How Teacher Turnover Harms Student Achievement", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 50/1, pp. 4-36, https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831212463813.

## Non-US candidates

### Falch & Strøm (2005) — VERIFIED. Core non-US cite for claim (1).
- **What it shows** (Norway, matched employer-employee panel of certified teachers in primary/lower-secondary schools; wages completely centralised, so mobility isolates non-pecuniary job attributes): **the propensity to quit is significantly higher in schools with high shares of minority students and of students with special needs**; teacher composition and school size also matter; robust across specifications and sub-samples (abstract confirmed via ERIC EJ697400; bibliographic details via Crossref).
- **Fit**: with wages off the table by design, this is clean evidence that *school-level non-pecuniary attributes* drive quits — a bridge between claims (1) and (2).
- **Reference**: Falch, T. and B. Strøm (2005), "Teacher turnover and non-pecuniary factors", *Economics of Education Review*, Vol. 24/6, pp. 611-631, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2004.09.005.

### Karbownik (2020) — VERIFIED, with an important caveat. Causal non-US cite for claim (1); complicates claim (2).
- **What it shows** (Stockholm upper-secondary schools; 2000 admission reform that exogenously reshuffled pupils across schools; Swedish register data): "**a 10-percentile-point increase in student credentials decreases the probability of a separation by up to 10 percentage points**". Effects are found **mainly for mobility between schools rather than exits from the profession**, and teachers "react mostly to direct measures of student ability (grades from compulsory school) rather than to other correlated characteristics (immigrant status, parental income or paternal cognitive skills)". The data do not support the compensating-wage-differentials hypothesis. (Abstract verified via Semantic Scholar; published version confirmed via Crossref. The earlier version is IFAU Working Paper 2014:15 / SSRN 2876539.)
- **Caveat**: this is the strongest *causal* evidence that student composition itself moves teachers — so it supports concentration (1), but it cuts against a strong reading of "conditions, not pupils" (2): teachers responded to pupils' prior achievement specifically, not to demographic proxies. Prior achievement plausibly proxies day-to-day classroom conditions, but the paper cannot show that. Cite it honestly (see footnote below).
- **Reference**: Karbownik, K. (2020), "The effects of student composition on teacher turnover: Evidence from an admission reform", *Economics of Education Review*, Vol. 75, 101960, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2020.101960.

### Allen, Burgess & Mayo (2018) — VERIFIED, with caveat. England cite for claim (1); useful counterweight.
- **What it shows** (England, School Workforce Census-era administrative data): **a positive raw association between school disadvantage and teacher turnover**, which is substantially weakened once school, pupil and local labour-market characteristics are controlled; the remaining association is "largely accounted for by teacher characteristics, with the poorer schools hiring much younger teachers on average". The authors read this equilibrium as reflecting the lower attractiveness of disadvantaged schools (they must recruit from a younger, more mobile pool).
- **Fit**: supports (1) but shows the England gradient is flatter than the US one and partly compositional. Good for OECD-audience balance; do not oversell.
- **Reference**: Allen, R., S. Burgess and J. Mayo (2018), "The teacher labour market, teacher turnover and disadvantaged schools: new evidence for England", *Education Economics*, Vol. 26/1, pp. 4-23, https://doi.org/10.1080/09645292.2017.1366425.

## OECD cross-national sources (for claim 3)

### OECD PISA 2022 Results, Volume II (2023) — VERIFIED from full-report PDF. Best cross-national cite for claim (3).
- **What it shows** (principals' reports, ~80 education systems): "In **30 countries/economies, students attending socio-economically disadvantaged schools were exposed to more shortages of education staff than their peers in advantaged schools**" (largest gaps in Peru, Jordan, Australia, Colombia, Brunei, Uruguay, Panama, UAE); **only in Malta** were shortages greater in advantaged schools. In 36 systems shortages were more prevalent in public than private schools; and "**on average across OECD countries and in 16 countries/economies, shortages of education staff were more prevalent in rural schools than in urban schools**". Between 2018 and 2022 the share of students in schools whose principal reported instruction hindered by a lack of teaching staff rose in 58 countries/economies.
- **Where**: chapter 5, **Figure II.5.4** ("Shortage of education staff and school characteristics") and **Tables II.B1.5.2 and II.B1.5.4**; trend figure is **Figure II.5.3**.
- **Reference**: OECD (2023), *PISA 2022 Results (Volume II): Learning During – and From – Disruption*, PISA, OECD Publishing, Paris, https://doi.org/10.1787/a97db61c-en.

### OECD TALIS 2024 (2025) — VERIFIED from full-report PDF. Cross-national shortage levels; disadvantage gaps in some systems.
- **What it shows** (principals' reports, lower secondary): **23% of teachers work in schools where a shortage of qualified teachers limits the school's capacity to provide quality instruction** (OECD average; around half of teachers in Bahrain, Latvia, the Netherlands), up 2 percentage points since 2018. By school characteristics: shortages are more often reported in **publicly managed schools** (gaps above 30 pp in Australia, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia), and — in Australia, Denmark, Estonia and New Zealand — socio-economically **advantaged schools are less likely to report shortages, with gaps again exceeding 30 percentage points**. *Caveat the report itself makes*: "most education systems do not show large differences in perceptions based on the socio-economic intake of students" — the disadvantage gap is large only in a subset of systems (Australia is the standout).
- **Where**: **Figure 1.6** and **Table 1.17** (levels), **Table 1.18** (change since 2018), **Table 1.19** (by school characteristics, incl. location and socio-economic intake).
- **Reference**: OECD (2025), *Results from TALIS 2024: The State of Teaching*, TALIS, OECD Publishing, Paris, https://doi.org/10.1787/90df6235-en.

### OECD Education at a Glance 2025, Indicator D8 — VERIFIED from full-report PDF. Vacancy/attrition magnitudes; no disadvantage split.
- **What it shows**: first internationally comparable administrative data on unfilled vacancies: across 14 countries with data, **unfilled posts for fully qualified teachers at the start of 2022/23 were below 3% of teaching posts everywhere except Austria and Sweden**; in absolute terms from 0 (Korea) to 4,778 (Austria), 5,747 (Poland) and 6,704 (Sweden); Austria's unfilled posts equalled **107% of the previous year's teaching graduates** (Poland 94%, Sweden 77%, French Community of Belgium 72%, Netherlands 50%); unfilled shares are higher in secondary than primary in most countries. Across 19 countries, **an average of 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23** (under 3% in France, Greece, Ireland, Israel; over 10% in Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania); in five of seven countries with seniority data, at least 30% of resigning teachers had taught fewer than five years.
- **Limitation for this paper**: D8 has **no breakdown by school disadvantage or location** — pair it with PISA 2022 Vol. II (concentration) rather than citing it for concentration itself.
- **Where**: Chapter D8 ("How severe are teacher shortages across countries?"), **Tables D8.2, D8.3 and D8.4**.
- **Reference**: OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025: OECD Indicators*, OECD Publishing, Paris, https://doi.org/10.1787/1c0d9c79-en.

## Australia

### Buckley & Griselda / e61 Institute (2025) — VERIFIED via secondary sources (PDF itself behind a bot-wall). Rural/remote attrition numbers.
- **What it shows** (population-wide administrative tax data, PLIDA): annual exits from the profession are **7% for teachers in very remote areas versus 5.2% in major cities**; in the **Northern Territory about 7.4% of teachers leave the profession each year — roughly 40% above the average of other jurisdictions**; attrition also elevated in special education.
- **Status flag**: e61 is a non-partisan economic research institute (research-note/working-paper quality, not peer-reviewed). I could not open the PDF directly (site challenge page); numbers are consistent across three independent secondary reports (APO record 330924, The Educator, e61's own summary). **Verify against the PDF before final citation** — e61 Research Note, 4 June 2025.
- **Reference**: Buckley, J. and S. Griselda (2025), *Who Stays, Who Goes? A new look at teacher attrition using administrative data*, e61 Institute Research Note, Sydney, 4 June 2025, https://e61.in/who-stays-who-goes-a-new-look-at-teacher-attrition-using-administrative-data/.

### Halsey (2018) — VERIFIED as a government review; thin on original turnover numbers.
- **Status**: Commonwealth-commissioned *Independent Review into Regional, Rural and Remote Education — Final Report* (Emeritus Prof. John Halsey, Flinders University), published by the Department of Education and Training, April 2018; 11 recommendations, 53 actions; documents chronic difficulty staffing RRR schools and high turnover as a recurring submission theme. It is a legitimate government citation for the *policy salience* of rural staffing, but it is a review synthesising submissions, not a quantitative turnover study. Prefer e61/AITSL ATWD for numbers (note: AITSL ATWD *National Trends: Teacher Workforce* June 2025 is already in drafts/references.md).
- **Reference**: Halsey, J. (2018), *Independent Review into Regional, Rural and Remote Education — Final Report*, Commonwealth of Australia (Department of Education and Training), Canberra, https://www.education.gov.au/recurrent-funding-schools/resources/independent-review-regional-rural-and-remote-education-final-report.
- Also note: **TALIS 2024 (Table 1.19)** singles out Australia as having the largest public/private and among the largest disadvantage-related staffing gaps — a cleaner, cross-nationally comparable Australian data point than Halsey.

## Ranked best 5-6

1. **Hanushek, Kain & Rivkin (2004)**, *JHR* 39(2), 326-354 — canonical Q1 evidence for claim (1): teachers systematically move away from low-achieving, high-minority schools (bottom-quartile schools lose ~20% of teachers a year; implied compensating differentials 25-40%).
2. **Carver-Thomas & Darling-Hammond (2019)**, *EPAA* 27(36) — national US magnitude for claims (1)+(3): turnover ~50% higher in Title I schools (16% vs 11%), ~70% higher for math/science; explicit turnover-shortage link.
3. **Johnson, Kraft & Papay (2012)**, *TCR* 114(10) — core of claim (2): school-environment measures explain away much of the demographic gradient in satisfaction and career intentions; leadership, collegiality and culture matter most. *(Cite jointly with Simon & Johnson (2015), TCR 117(3), the review that generalises this: "not fleeing their students… fleeing the poor working conditions".)*
4. **Falch & Strøm (2005)**, *EER* 24(6) — non-US (Norway): with fully centralised wages, quit rates are higher in schools with high shares of minority and special-needs pupils — non-pecuniary school attributes drive exits.
5. **Karbownik (2020)**, *EER* 75 — non-US (Sweden), causal: exogenous reshuffling of pupils shows separations respond to student composition (10-percentile-point higher student credentials → up to 10 pp lower separation probability), mainly via between-school moves; cite with its caveat for claim (2).
6. **OECD (2023), PISA 2022 Results (Volume II)**, Figure II.5.4 / Table II.B1.5.2 — cross-national claim (3): staff shortages concentrate in disadvantaged schools in 30 systems (opposite in only one), in public schools in 36, and in rural schools on OECD average.

(2009) as US companion for (2); OECD EAG 2025 D8 for vacancy/attrition magnitudes; TALIS 2024 Fig. 1.6/Table 1.19 as the most recent OECD shortage survey; Ronfeldt et al. ## Suggested footnote (conditions vs pupils nuance)

> The concentration of exits in disadvantaged schools appears to reflect the working conditions that prevail in those schools more than the pupils themselves: in survey-based studies, measures of the school environment — principal leadership, collegial relationships, discipline climate and school culture — largely explain away the association between student demographics and teachers' satisfaction and turnover intentions (Johnson, Kraft and Papay, 2012[x]; Simon and Johnson, 2015[x]; see also Allensworth, Ponisciak and Mazzeo, 2009[x]). The distinction is not absolute: quasi-experimental evidence from Sweden shows that teacher separations also respond directly to pupils' prior achievement rather than to correlated demographic characteristics (Karbownik, 2020[x]), and in England much of the raw turnover gap between disadvantaged and other schools reflects the younger age profile of the teachers such schools are able to recruit (Allen, Burgess and Mayo, 2018[x]). The policy implication is nonetheless the same in each case: what distinguishes hard-to-staff schools is largely amenable to intervention.

## Flags

- **e61 (Buckley & Griselda 2025)**: numbers (7% vs 5.2%; NT 7.4%) verified only against secondary reports; the PDF (e61.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/teacher_attrition_final-20250604.pdf) is behind a bot challenge from this environment. Re-check before final.
- **Halsey (2018)**: verified as a Commonwealth review, but not a source of original turnover statistics — cite for policy salience only, or drop in favour of e61/AITSL ATWD + TALIS 2024 Table 1.19.
- **TALIS 2024 caveat**: the report itself notes that *most* systems do not show large shortage gaps by socio-economic intake; the >30 pp gaps are specific to Australia, Denmark, Estonia and New Zealand. Do not present TALIS as showing a universal disadvantage gradient — PISA 2022 Vol. II (30 of ~80 systems, only one reversed) is the safer cross-national claim.
- **EAG 2025 D8** has no disadvantage/rural split — use only for overall vacancy and attrition magnitudes.
- **Karbownik (2020)** measures mainly *between-school mobility*, not exits from teaching, and finds teachers respond to pupil ability itself — flag whenever it is cited alongside the "conditions, not kids" claim.
- **Hanushek, Kain & Rivkin (2004)** likewise cannot separate pupil characteristics from correlated conditions; use for claim (1) only.
- **Carver-Thomas & Darling-Hammond**: cite the 2019 peer-reviewed EPAA article (10.14507/epaa.27.3699), not the 2017 Learning Policy Institute report (10.54300/454.278), which would read as advocacy.
- Ronfeldt et al. (2013) is about the *consequences* of turnover (worst in low-achieving schools), not its level or drivers — keep it in that role.
