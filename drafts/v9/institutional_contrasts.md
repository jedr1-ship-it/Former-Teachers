# INSTITUTIONAL CONTRASTS — how law and institutional setting change the weight of each reason for leaving

Prepared 24 August 2026 for the literature-review section (v9). Companion to `drafts/v6/litreview_core.md` and `drafts/v6/litreview_taxonomies.md`; nothing already characterised there is re-described, only extended with the institutional layer.

**What this file is for.** The section is written idea by idea: a reason is named, anchored, corroborated, then *qualified* by showing that the institutional setting alters its force. This file supplies the qualifier for six reasons: the institutional fact with its source, the contrasting country, and — separately and explicitly — whether anything actually links that institution to teacher exit.

**Evidence conventions carried over.** `[CAUSAL]`, `[ASSOC]`, `[DESCRIPTIVE]`, `[QUAL]`. Added here: `[INSTITUTIONAL]` for a statement of law, regulation or collective agreement, which is a *fact about rules*, not evidence about behaviour. The recurring failure mode in this literature is treating `[INSTITUTIONAL]` as if it were `[CAUSAL]`. Each section ends with the two lines the drafters asked for.

**Headline caution for the whole file.** Of the six reasons, only **two** — pension design and accountability/inspection — have a documented, quantified link running from a specific institutional rule to observed teacher exit behaviour. The other four have well-documented institutional variation and no study connecting it to teacher exit. That asymmetry is itself a finding and should be stated in the paper rather than papered over.

## 1. FAMILY FORMATION AND CARE

### 1.1 The institutional facts

**The leave floor.** As of the April 2024 update of the OECD Family Database, **37 of 38 OECD members provide nationally mandated paid maternity/parental leave; the United States is the exception**, having no national paid family leave programme. OECD countries providing paid maternity leave offer on average **17.3 weeks** of maternity leave specifically, with wage replacement between 27% and 100%; counting maternity plus parental plus home-care leave available to mothers, the OECD average is **51.9 weeks paid**. US figures in the database are not comparable with the rest: they aggregate state paid-family-leave insurance schemes and, uniquely, employer-provided schemes (OECD Family Database, indicator PF2.1). `[INSTITUTIONAL]`/`[DESCRIPTIVE]`

**Spain — the strong-protection case.** Two distinct instruments stack.
- *Permiso por nacimiento y cuidado del menor*: raised from 16 to **19 weeks, fully paid, non-transferable, for each parent** (32 weeks for single-parent families) by **Real Decreto-ley 9/2025 of 29 July 2025**, in force 31 July 2025, ratified by the Congreso on 9 September 2025, retroactive to births from 2 August 2024; two of the nineteen weeks are flexible until the child is eight. It applies to public employees, teachers included. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- *Excedencia por cuidado de hijos*, **Article 89 of the TREBEP (Estatuto Básico del Empleado Público)**: career civil servants — which is what a *funcionario de carrera docente* is — are entitled to up to **three years' leave per child**, with **the specific post reserved for at least two years** and thereafter a post in the same locality at the same salary; the period counts for *trienios*, career progression and social-security rights. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- The combination means that in Spain a teacher can be absent for up to three years and return to a reserved post with seniority intact. Childbirth cannot, as a matter of law, cost a *funcionaria de carrera* her post. It says nothing about *interinas*, and Spain publishes no national teacher-attrition series at all (Tahull Fort and Molina-Luque 2025, already in the base), so the counterfactual cannot be measured domestically either.

**France — a comparable but different instrument.** French teachers have a *temps partiel de droit* to raise a child under three: quotités of 50/60/70/80% granted **as of right**, renewable by tacit reconduction, ending on the child's third birthday; for children born after 2004 the part-time period counts as **full time for pension service duration** (Ministère de l'Éducation nationale; academies' implementing notes). `[INSTITUTIONAL]`

**Germany and the Netherlands — statutory rights to reduce hours.** See §2; the family-formation relevance is that in both systems the standard response to a birth is an hours reduction inside the job, not an exit from it.

**England — a paid, conditional occupational scheme.** Statutory maternity leave is 52 weeks with 39 weeks paid. On top, the **Burgundy Book** (School Teachers' Conditions of Service) gives teachers with one year's continuous local-authority service **4 weeks at full pay, 2 weeks at 90%, 12 weeks at half pay plus SMP, then SMP to week 39** — and the twelve half-pay weeks are **repayable if the teacher does not return for 13 weeks**, a clause a head may waive. `[INSTITUTIONAL]` This is an unusual design: the occupational element is explicitly a retention bond.

**United States — the outlier, twice over.**
- Federal floor: **FMLA gives 12 weeks unpaid**, and only to employees meeting the 12-month/1,250-hour test at employers with 50+ employees.
- Subpart F of the FMLA regulations contains **special rules for instructional employees**: **29 CFR 825.602** lets a school **require a teacher who begins leave in the five weeks before the end of an academic term to remain on leave until the end of the term** (three weeks, for birth/placement/family-care leave taken near term end). The law that protects the job also lengthens the absence, unpaid.
- Coverage: NCTQ's district database (100 largest districts, largest district in each state, Council of Great City Schools members; n≈148) finds **only 18% of the largest districts provide paid parental leave for educators giving birth**, and under a quarter of districts in the database overall — though the number of large districts offering it **more than doubled in three years, from 27 to 64**. **15 states and DC** guarantee paid parental leave for all teachers; **only Arkansas and Delaware** give 12 weeks at full pay while also funding the substitute. A 2024 RAND survey found **about one-third of teachers report access to any paid parental leave**. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`

**Childcare.** Enrolment of children under 3 in early childhood education and care averaged **29% across the OECD in 2023**, up 9 percentage points in a decade (OECD, *Education at a Glance 2025*); the Nordic countries and the Benelux group sit at 50% or above (OECD Family Database, PF3.2). *Verify the exact country cells in PF3.2 before quoting any single country.*

### 1.2 Does any of this change whether childbirth ends a teaching career?

**No study located establishes it.** This is the same gap the base already records at §8.6 of `litreview_core.md`, and this session's searching did not close it:
- The canonical result — **67% of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely, with a newborn the dominant factor** — is Stinebrickner (2002) on the US NLS-72 cohort. A 1972 high-school cohort of women, in the pre-FMLA United States.
- No register-based teacher-specific child-penalty study was located for Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Germany or the Netherlands, despite the general child-penalty literature being built precisely on those registers (employment child penalty of **14% in Denmark against 41% in Germany**, Kleven and co-authors as summarised in the CEPR/VoxEU cross-country work). Teachers are not separated out.
- NCTQ's advocacy note states that **about 15% of teachers who leave cite family obligations, including pregnancy and childbirth**, and reports that in Delaware **only 3% of teachers used the paid-leave benefit in 2024** — a cost fact, not a retention estimate. The 15% figure is second-hand; trace it to the NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey before use.
- The nearest generalisable evidence is Rossin-Slater's work on California's paid family leave, reported as showing **small or neutral effects on women's retention** and no detrimental effect on career trajectories — not teacher-specific.
- Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026), already in the base, price leave hypothetically: **ten additional days of paid leave is worth about a 7% pay rise** in job-choice experiments. Stated preference, not behaviour.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** Spain guarantees a teacher who has a child 19 fully paid weeks (RDL 9/2025) *and* up to three years' *excedencia* with the post itself reserved for two of them and seniority preserved (TREBEP Art. 89), so in law childbirth can only interrupt a *funcionaria de carrera*'s career; in the United States the federal floor is 12 weeks unpaid (FMLA), only 18% of the largest districts pay for parental leave (NCTQ), and 29 CFR 825.602 lets the district *extend* a teacher's unpaid absence to the end of term. Same event, opposite institutional consequence.

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That the Spanish (or Nordic, German, Dutch) regime actually produces higher return rates or lower attrition among teacher-mothers than the US or English regime — no study, anywhere, measures teacher exit or return around childbirth in a contemporary OECD system, so the contrast must be presented as a difference in *legal exposure*, not in outcomes. Also not evidenced: that the US paid-leave expansion (27→64 large districts) has moved retention; that childcare enrolment rates relate to teacher exit; that the Burgundy Book repayment clause changes return behaviour; and any claim that the 67% labour-force-exit figure in Stinebrickner (2002) still holds — it is a 1970s cohort and should never be quoted in the present tense.

## 2. WORKING HOURS AND PART-TIME AVAILABILITY

### 2.1 Where part-time is a right, a norm, or neither

**Right, and strong (Netherlands).** The *Wet flexibel werken* gives an employee with 26 weeks' service the right to request a change in working hours, and the employer **must grant it unless there are weighty business or service interests** (*zwaarwegende bedrijfs- of dienstbelangen*); silence past the deadline means the request is **deemed granted**. `[INSTITUTIONAL]` This is a right to *obtain*, defeasible only on a high threshold.

**Right, and contested (Germany).** *Landesbeamte* teachers have historically had an entitlement to part-time down to 50%, plus separate entitlements for *Elternzeit*, family reasons and *Pflegezeit*. Following the SWK's 2023 expert report on teacher shortage, which recommended restricting part-time below 50% to narrowly defined grounds, **Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt and North Rhine-Westphalia curtailed part-time sharply, and Baden-Württemberg required teachers from 2024/25 to justify working below 75%** — with family reasons, parental leave and care leave preserved as exceptions. `[INSTITUTIONAL]` Germany is thus the documented case of a shortage causing a *withdrawal* of the flexibility right — a natural experiment nobody has yet evaluated.

**Right, and used moderately (France).** *Temps partiel de droit* at 50/60/70/80% to raise a child under three (§1.1), alongside discretionary *temps partiel sur autorisation*. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`

**Right to *ask* only (England).** The Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023, in force **6 April 2024**, makes the request a **day-one right**, allows **two statutory requests per 12 months**, and requires consultation before refusal — but leaves untouched the **eight statutory business grounds** on which an employer may refuse (Employment Rights Act 1996, s.80G), including cost, inability to reorganise work among existing staff, and detrimental impact on quality or performance. `[INSTITUTIONAL]` Timetabling and continuity are exactly the grounds school leaders cite (Sharp et al. 2019, in the base).

### 2.2 The prevalence figures

TALIS 2024, share of lower-secondary teachers employed part-time (defined as up to 90% of full-time hours):
- **Netherlands 66%** — 2nd of 55 systems, and the extreme case among large OECD systems. In TALIS 2018 only **41% of Dutch lower-secondary teachers worked full time**.
- **OECD average 19%.**
- **France 16%** — unchanged since 2018.
- **Japan 7%** — down 2 percentage points since 2018.
Germany, from national statistics rather than TALIS: **43.1% of teachers at general-education schools were part-time in 2023/24** (739,500 teachers), rising to **43.9% in 2024/25**; the female part-time rate (**50.7%**) is more than double the male rate (**22.6%**) (Statistisches Bundesamt). England, from NFER: **20% of secondary teachers part-time in 2018/19**, up from 17% in 2010/11 (Worth 2020); the 2024 School Workforce Census gap between headcount (513,400) and FTE (468,300) is consistent with a part-time share of roughly that order across all phases. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`

*Verification item:* whether England participated in TALIS 2024 is unclear — the DfE announced withdrawal in 2022, while OECD listings still show England (United Kingdom) as a TALIS participant. Do not cite an England TALIS 2024 figure until this is settled; use the School Workforce Census instead.

### 2.3 Does availability change exit or return?

**Documented demand, not documented effect.**
- **Unmet demand, England:** one in six secondary teachers would like to reduce hours, one in twelve by more than a day a week; many never ask, believing refusal certain, though **only 14% of those who did ask were refused** (Sharp et al. 2019; NFER blog reporting on the same survey). `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- **Price of the reverse move, Netherlands:** part-time teachers require a **23% net hourly wage increase to accept a full-time post** (Somers et al. 2024/2026, in the base). `[CAUSAL]` within a hypothetical choice setting. This prices the "convert part-timers to full-timers" policy and finds it expensive; it does not price the retention value of allowing part-time.
- **Hidden reserve, Netherlands:** ~62,000 qualified teachers working outside education against a secondary shortage of 13,600 FTE (Somers et al. 2024, in the base). High part-time prevalence and a large hidden reserve coexist.
- **The counter-finding, Germany:** Seibt and Kreuzfeld (2023), 5,905 part-time teachers at German upper-secondary schools with four weeks of logged working time — **reducing teaching hours alone does not improve mental health**; part-time groups do not differ significantly in ability to recover or burnout risk; **47% show burnout symptoms** and **42% intend to retire early**, driven by excessive workload (48%) and declining performance (32%). `[ASSOC]`, cross-sectional. Part-time here looks like a coping strategy that does not fix the underlying load, in the system with one of the OECD's highest part-time rates.
- **No causal estimate exists** of the effect of granting or withdrawing part-time access on teacher exit, in any system. The EEF trial of offsite planning time noted in the base is the only registered attempt in this space, and it tests a different intervention.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** In the Netherlands the law obliges the employer to grant an hours reduction unless weighty business interests prevent it (*Wet flexibel werken*) and 66% of lower-secondary teachers work part-time — the second highest of 55 TALIS systems; in Japan the same figure is 7% and in England the 2023 Act confers only a right to *request*, refusable on eight statutory business grounds, the very grounds (timetabling, continuity, cost) that school leaders give.

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That part-time availability retains teachers. Nobody has estimated it. The Dutch case cuts both ways — 66% part-time coexists with a 62,000-strong hidden reserve — and the German case is actively unhelpful to the optimistic reading: in a system with 43% part-time working, reducing hours does not improve teachers' mental health and 42% of part-timers still intend to retire early (Seibt and Kreuzfeld 2023). Also not evidenced: that the German Länder restrictions on part-time have increased exit (no evaluation exists, though the policy is a clean natural experiment and the paper could say so); and that England's 2024 day-one right has changed anything.

## 3. PAY AGAINST OUTSIDE OPTIONS

### 3.1 The comparative indicator

*Education at a Glance 2025*, Indicator D3, expresses teachers' **actual** salaries (including bonuses and allowances) against the earnings of **full-time, full-year tertiary-educated workers**. Primary level, 2024 reference:
- **OECD average: teachers earn 17% less** than tertiary-educated full-time full-year workers.
- **United States: 37% less** — the largest gap reported among OECD countries in the country-note series.
- **Slovak Republic: 26% less.**
- **Poland: 15% less** — i.e. *better* than the OECD average on this measure.
- **Portugal: 28% more** — teachers out-earn comparable graduates.
`[DESCRIPTIVE]`

**Two caveats the paper must carry.** (i) "Actual salaries" reflect the *age and seniority composition* of the teaching workforce, and Portugal's is among the oldest in the OECD — the share of teachers aged 50+ exceeded **50% in 2023** against an OECD average of 38%, having risen from about 31% in 2013 (EAG 2025). A seniority-loaded scale plus an ageing workforce mechanically inflates the ratio. (ii) The ratio is not the absolute level: Poland's near-average *ratio* coexists with low absolute pay.

**The domestic US series.** EPI's regression-adjusted teacher weekly wage penalty relative to other college graduates reached **26.9% in 2024** — teachers earning **73.1 cents on the dollar**, against **93.9 cents in 1996**. `[ASSOC]` Advocacy-institute source; the trend is widely accepted, the level is sensitive to the controls.

### 3.2 Does the salary motive weigh more where the ratio is worse?

**Not demonstrated, and one prominent case runs the wrong way.**
- The tempting move is to pair the pay ratio with the *rank of pay* in national leaver surveys — pay 6th (22% of respondents) in the Dutch leaver study (Van Casteren et al. 2023) against 1st (79%) in the Polish one, both already in `litreview_taxonomies.md`. But EAG puts **Poland's relative pay at −15%, better than the OECD average**, so the ranking difference cannot be read off the relative-pay indicator. The base's own formulation — "the *rank* of pay is a function of the national wage level, not of the causal structure" — is the safe one, and even that is an interpretation, not an estimate.
- **Portugal is the sharpest anomaly and should be used as such.** It has the highest relative pay in the country-note series (**+28%**) *and* one of the lowest shares of teachers satisfied with salary (**under 20%**, with Iceland, Malta and Serbia) *and* one of the lowest intentions to leave (**below 6%**, with Korea and Viet Nam) (OECD, TALIS 2024). Relative pay, felt pay grievance and intention to leave are three different things in the same country.
- Cross-national causal work on pay exists, but it is about *selection*, not exit: Hanushek, Piopiunik and Wiederhold (2019) identify the effect of teacher cognitive skills on student performance across 31 countries by exploiting **international differences in the relative wages of non-teacher public-sector employees** — evidence that relative pay shapes who becomes a teacher, not who leaves.
- The best-identified pay–retention estimates remain national and are already in the base: Falch (2011) for Norway (a ~10% premium cuts voluntary quits by 6pp; short-run supply elasticity ≈1.25), Clotfelter et al. (2008), Feng and Sass (2018), Sims and Benhenda (2022) — against See et al. (2020), who conclude money recruits but does not durably retain. **None of these is designed to compare the size of the pay effect across institutional settings.**
- OECD's own framing in *Education Policy Outlook 2024* and EAG 2025 is that low salaries "can contribute" to shortages and that competitive salaries "may help" keep attrition low — hedged policy language, not an estimate, and it should be quoted as such if quoted at all.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** On the same OECD indicator, primary teachers earn **37% less** than tertiary-educated full-time full-year workers in the United States and **28% more** in Portugal, against an OECD average of 17% less (EAG 2025) — a ~65-point spread in the outside-option gap that any account of the salary motive has to survive; and Portugal, the high-ratio case, nonetheless has under 20% of teachers satisfied with their pay (TALIS 2024).

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That the pay elasticity of exit is larger where the relative-pay ratio is lower. No cross-country study estimates it. Do not infer it from the rank of pay in national leaver surveys — Poland ranks pay first with a better-than-average ratio, which breaks the inference. Do not treat Portugal's +28% as evidence that generous relative pay retains teachers: the ratio is inflated by the OECD's third-oldest teaching workforce, and Portuguese teachers are among the least satisfied with their salaries in TALIS. Do not present the EPI 26.9% penalty as an OECD-comparable figure; it is a different construct on different data.

## 4. PENSION DESIGN AND RETIREMENT TIMING

### 4.1 The two architectures

**United States — occupational defined benefit with accrual spikes.**
- Teachers are enrolled in **state-level DB plans** whose pension-wealth accrual is highly non-linear, spiking in the mid-to-late 50s then falling or turning negative — the "pull-then-push" structure (Costrell and Podgursky 2009, in the base). `[DESCRIPTIVE]` of incentives
- **Teachers in 15 states and DC — roughly 40% of US public-school teachers — are not covered by Social Security**, a legacy of pre-1983 state opt-outs (Alaska, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Massachusetts, Missouri, Nevada, Ohio, Rhode Island, Texas). `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- **Vesting**: most states require a minimum of five years to qualify for any pension, **17 states require ten**; the Bellwether analysis reports that roughly half of teachers never qualify for even a minimum benefit. `[DESCRIPTIVE]` Advocacy-institute source; the vesting rules themselves are verifiable, the "half" figure needs checking against the underlying model.
- Behavioural evidence: Ni and Podgursky (2016) show a Stock–Wise option-value model fits the Missouri retirement hazard and predicts out of sample the response to 1990s enhancements; Fleck et al. (2025) find enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4–10pp (both in the base). `[CAUSAL]` in the structural/quasi-experimental sense.

**Europe — earnings-related public or civil-service schemes, reformed in the 2000s.**
- **Germany**: teachers are typically *Beamte* under *Beamtenversorgung*; a civil servant with five years' service is entitled to a pension; early retirement without proof of incapacity is possible from 63 (60 with severe disability); the standard age is rising to 67 for the 1964 cohort. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- **Netherlands**: teachers are in **ABP**, the funded public-and-education scheme; the 2006 reform abolished the favourable early-retirement route for cohorts born from 1950.
- **France**: teachers are state civil servants; the 2023 reform raised the legal age from 62 to 64.
- **Korea**: *honorary retirement* is available after 20 years' service — an institutional feature that shapes the exit statistics directly (see §5).
- **Japan**: mandatory retirement age, being raised in stages from 60 to 65 — retirement remains the majority of teacher exits (MEXT School Teachers Survey, in the base).

### 4.2 Evidence that design changes timing — including, at last, outside the United States

**Germany, the cleanest European case.** *Versorgungsabschläge* — pension deductions of up to **10.8%** for retirement on grounds of *Dienstunfähigkeit* before 63 — were introduced in **2001**. In the year before, **64% of all teacher retirements were on incapacity grounds**; the share fell to **34% in 2003 and 28% in 2004**, and the **average retirement age of teachers rose from 59 to 62** over the same period (Statistisches Bundesamt). `[DESCRIPTIVE]`, an interrupted time series with an obvious candidate cause and no formal identification — say "coincided with", not "caused by", unless a proper evaluation is found. This directly narrows the gap the base records at §6.7 ("no study prices the effect of pension design on teacher exit timing in any European system"): the German series is not a study, but it is a documented European instance of a pension rule moving retirement timing.

**Netherlands, quasi-experimental but not teacher-specific.** Montizaan, Cörvers and de Grip (2010) exploit the January 2006 ABP reform, which cut pension wealth for public-sector workers born in **1950 or later** but not for those born in 1949, using matched survey and administrative data on male public-sector employees. The shock **postponed expected retirement** and raised training participation (in large organisations). `[CAUSAL]` — a clean discontinuity. ABP covers education, but the published analysis is of the public sector as a whole, so it evidences the *mechanism* in a European earnings-related scheme without evidencing it for teachers. A separate Dutch register study of the same reform reports work participation to the statutory retirement age rising from **61% to 82% at age 64**.

**The contested US valuation literature** (Fitzpatrick 2015 vs Ni, Podgursky and Wang 2022 vs Fuchsman, McGee and Zamarro 2023) is already fully handled in the base at §6.3–6.5 and §12.5; do not re-litigate it here, but note that its subject is *valuation*, not *timing*, and the timing evidence is not in dispute.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** In the United States, teacher pension wealth accrues non-linearly with a spike in the mid-to-late fifties that then reverses, concentrating exit at ages young by professional standards (Costrell and Podgursky 2009; Ni and Podgursky 2016), and ~40% of teachers in 15 states are outside Social Security altogether; in Germany, the introduction of *Versorgungsabschläge* of up to 10.8% in 2001 coincided with the share of teacher retirements taken on incapacity grounds falling from 64% (2000) to 28% (2004) and the average teacher retirement age rising from 59 to 62 — the same lever, pulled in the opposite direction, moving the same margin.

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That the German shift was *caused* by the deductions — it is an official time series around a policy date, not an evaluation, and other reforms and cohort effects are uncontrolled. That the Dutch 2006 discontinuity applies specifically to teachers — Montizaan et al. study male public-sector workers, teachers unidentified. That European teachers respond to pension incentives with the same option-value rationality demonstrated in Missouri. That pension design affects *pre-retirement* exit (the US literature is about retirement timing; the young teachers who dominate attrition are precisely the group Fuchsman et al. find indifferent to plan type). The base's §6.7 gap statement should be softened, not deleted: there is still no European study that *prices* the effect.

## 5. PROFESSIONAL STANDING, CONFLICT AND LEGAL EXPOSURE

### 5.1 Three institutional responses to the same pressure

**Korea — legislation created by an exit crisis.** After the suicide of a first-year teacher at **Seoi Elementary School, Seoul, in July 2023**, the National Assembly passed amendments to **four acts on the protection of teachers' rights on 21 September 2023** (the *Special Act on the Improvement of Teachers' Status and the Protection of Their Educational Activities* amended by Act No. 19735 of 27 September 2023, together with the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, the Early Childhood Education Act and the child-abuse punishment legislation). A **2026 amendment** further widened the definition of an infringement from complaints "repeatedly raised without legitimate purpose" to complaints made "repeatedly **or in a manner that causes significant disruption to educational activities**", capturing single malicious complaints, and extended coverage to non-face-to-face teaching. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- Why it matters for the taxonomy: in the KEDI survey material already in the base, **40.9% attributed the teacher exodus to the infringement of teachers' rights (교권 침해)** — a cause with no counterpart in any Anglophone taxonomy, and one the Korean state has answered with statute rather than with pay or workload policy.

**France — a pre-existing duty of protection, under visible strain.** *Protection fonctionnelle* obliges the administration to protect an agent attacked or threatened in the course of duty (legal costs, medical costs, support). Requests in education **tripled between 2020 and 2024**: 2,218 in 2020, **4,948 in 2023** (a 123% rise on 2020), a further **~30% rise in 2024**; **82.6% were granted**; **89.5% of 2024 requests followed an assault**; primary and secondary teachers are affected in roughly equal proportions. `[DESCRIPTIVE]` *Source chain runs through the education press (VousNousIls, franceinfo) reporting ministry/DGRH figures — trace to the primary ministerial or Sénat document before publication.*

**Japan — the hours regime as the institution.** *Kyūtokuhō* (the Act on Special Measures concerning Salaries and Other Conditions for Education Personnel) pays teachers a flat **4% adjustment allowance in lieu of overtime pay**, with the consequence that overtime is neither paid nor formally counted. The **first major revision in over 50 years was enacted on 11 June 2025**: the allowance rises **1 percentage point a year to 10% by 2031**, every prefectural and municipal board of education must draw up and publish a **workload-management plan**, and a supplementary provision sets a target of cutting average monthly overtime by about **30%, to 30 hours**. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`
- The load being regulated: Japanese full-time lower-secondary teachers report **55.1 hours per week**, the highest of 55 TALIS 2024 systems, against an OECD average of 41 — while spending **fewer** hours actually teaching (17.8 vs 22.7). Hours fell 3.9 since 2018.
- The outcome variable in Japan is not resignation. FY2023 saw a record **7,119 public-school staff on mental-health sick leave**, and FY2024 a record **1,319 teachers resigning for mental illness** (MEXT, in the base) — withdrawal is partial and reversible, which is why the Japanese case belongs in the "degree of withdrawal" dimension the base proposes.

### 5.2 Does any of it link to exit?

- **Korea:** in the first two months of **2025, 3,487 teachers took early retirement, down from 5,212 in the same period of 2024**, a fall press reporting **attributes partly to the strengthened legal protections**. `[DESCRIPTIVE]` This is a two-month year-on-year comparison in press coverage of KEDI/ministry figures, with no counterfactual, no seasonality adjustment and an obvious attribution problem. It is the closest thing in this file to an institution-to-exit link for this reason, and it is not good enough to carry a claim. It *is* good enough to justify saying that Korea has run the natural experiment and that someone should evaluate it.
- **France:** no study links *protection fonctionnelle* — its availability, its grant rate, or its adequacy — to teacher exit or to intention to leave. Périer (2025), in the base, records the felt absence of institutional support qualitatively; the DEPP resignation series (0.05% in 2008/09 to over 0.3% in 2020/21) is not decomposed by cause.
- **Japan:** no evaluation of the 2025 *kyūtokuhō* revision exists — it is barely in force, and the allowance does not reach 10% until 2031. The pre-reform association between long hours and mental-health leave is descriptive.
- **Cross-nationally:** OECD (2025), TALIS 2024 finds stress from **student intimidation/verbal abuse and from maintaining discipline more closely associated with intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures** — the closest general support for treating legal/authority exposure as a first-order motive, but it measures intention, and it measures pupil behaviour rather than parental or legal exposure.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** Korea answered a teacher-exit crisis with statute — four acts amended within nine weeks of the Seoi Elementary case in 2023, and a 2026 widening to cover single malicious complaints — while Japan's answer to the same family of pressures was to reprice unmeasured overtime, raising the *kyūtokuhō* allowance from 4% toward 10% by 2031 and mandating published workload plans in a system whose teachers already work the longest hours of 55 TALIS 2024 systems (55.1 per week against 41). Two states, two institutional instruments, and in neither case an evaluated effect on exit.

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That Korea's 2023 laws reduced teacher exit. The only figure available is a two-month year-on-year fall in early retirements (3,487 in early 2025 against 5,212 in early 2024) reported in the press with a causal gloss attached by the reporter; it must not be presented as an effect. That France's *protection fonctionnelle* moderates the weight of conflict and legal exposure as a reason to leave — the tripling of requests documents rising demand for protection, nothing about exit. That Japan's 2025 revision will change hours or exits. That "professional standing" or "status" drives exit anywhere: the base already records (§13) that status is documented as falling and never estimated as a cause.

## 6. ACCOUNTABILITY AND INSPECTION REGIMES

### 6.1 The two poles

**England — high-stakes external inspection.** Ofsted inspection, with published judgements, has been the pivot of school accountability. The regime itself changed inside the study period: **single-word overall judgements were scrapped with effect from 2 September 2024**, following the coroner's finding that the inspection process likely contributed to the death of headteacher **Ruth Perry**, whose school was moved from "outstanding" to "inadequate"; sub-judgements continued, with **school report cards** intended to follow. `[INSTITUTIONAL]` Any claim about "the English accountability regime" must be dated: the grade whose effect is measured in the quasi-experimental literature no longer exists in the same form.

**Finland — the light-touch pole, by design and by date.** Finland **abolished its central education inspectorate in 1991**, replacing it with a national evaluation council conducting periodic sample-based evaluation; since **2013** this is the Finnish Education Evaluation Centre (FINEEC), which assesses **samples** of grade-9 students (Finnish and mathematics every three years, other subjects every five), publishes findings and makes **non-binding** recommendations. There is no comprehensive standardised testing system and **formal teacher evaluation is not required and rarely occurs**. `[INSTITUTIONAL]`

### 6.2 What the accountability climate does

**The behavioural estimate (England).** Sims (2016), DoQSS Working Paper 16-14, UCL — difference-in-differences on the School Workforce Census linked to Ofsted outcomes: an **"Inadequate" judgement raises teacher turnover by 3.4 percentage points, about 25% of average turnover**, while an **"Outstanding" judgement produces no change**. `[CAUSAL]` The effect is asymmetric and identified off the lowest grade; the outcome is leaving the *school*, not the profession. Already in the base at §3.4; the institutional point to add is that this is the **only quantified estimate anywhere of an accountability rule moving teacher behaviour**, and it exists because England has both a discrete inspection judgement and a linked administrative census — which Finland, by construction, has neither of.

**The stated estimate (England).** Perryman, Bradbury, Calvert and Kilian (2025), *British Journal of Educational Studies* 73/2, pp. 181–200, from the *Beyond Ofsted* project: **76% of respondents thought Ofsted had a negative effect on retention and 30% had considered leaving as a result of their most recent inspection**. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`/`[QUAL]`, self-selected respondents to a project explicitly seeking views on alternatives to inspection. The pairing with Sims (2016) — 30% considered, 3.4 percentage points moved — is the cleanest illustration of the intention/behaviour gap in the whole file.

**The cross-national gradient.** Jerrim and Sims (2022), *Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability* 34, pp. 5–32: more than **100,000 teachers in over 40 countries** (TALIS 2018), accountability measured from headteacher reports on use of assessment data, public availability of results and the existence of an inspectorate. Findings: a **modest positive correlation** between the strength of system accountability and teachers' and headteachers' stress about accountability; **~45% of teachers on average report feeling stressed by accountability**; little within-school transmission from head to staff, but **strong "emotional contagion" of accountability stress between colleagues**. `[ASSOC]` This is the bridge that lets the paper say the accountability climate is a system-level variable and not merely an English preoccupation — and its effect size is modest.

**Two findings that cut against the simple story, and should be kept.**
- Sims and Jerrim (2017) on TALIS 2013, 34 countries: **a school's inspection grade is not negatively associated with job satisfaction or turnover intentions once working conditions are controlled** (in the base, §3.2).
- OECD (2025), TALIS 2024: **discipline and student intimidation outrank accountability and reform pressures** as correlates of intention to leave (in the base, §7.1).
- And the Finnish pole does not deliver contentment: Räsänen et al. (2020) find **49.5% of Finnish comprehensive-school teachers have turnover intentions, 24.1% persistently**, with the largest single stated category being **lack of commitment**, not accountability (in the base). Abolishing the inspectorate in 1991 has not produced a workforce that does not think about leaving.

**USABLE CONTRAST:** England has the only quantified estimate in the literature of an accountability rule moving teachers — an "Inadequate" Ofsted judgement raises turnover by 3.4 percentage points, ~25% of average turnover, with no effect from "Outstanding" (Sims 2016) — precisely because it has discrete published judgements and a linked workforce census; Finland abolished its inspectorate in 1991 and evaluates samples of pupils with non-binding findings, has no comparable rule to estimate, and still reports about half its comprehensive-school teachers holding turnover intentions (Räsänen et al. 2020).

**NOT EVIDENCED:** That light-touch accountability lowers attrition. The comparison is unidentified (Finland differs on entry selectivity, status, class size, pay and workforce composition), the Finnish intention data are high, and the one cross-national estimate of the accountability–stress link is explicitly "modest" (Jerrim and Sims 2022). Not evidenced either: that Ofsted judgements drive teachers out of the *profession* — Sims (2016) measures moves out of the school; that the 76%/30% figures from *Beyond Ofsted* describe behaviour or a representative population; and that the September 2024 removal of single-word judgements has changed retention, which nobody has yet had time to measure. If the paper cites the 3.4-point estimate it should note that the graded judgement it identifies has since been abolished.

## SUMMARY TABLE — where the institutional layer is real evidence and where it is only real law

| Reason | Institutional variation documented? | Linked to teacher exit? | Strength of the link |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Family formation and care | Yes, sharply (Spain/US) | **No** | Nothing. Base gap §8.6 stands. |
| 2. Working hours / part-time | Yes (NL/DE/FR/EN/JP) | **No** | Demand documented; effect never estimated; German evidence is discouraging. |
| 3. Pay against outside options | Yes (EAG D3, 65-point spread) | Within countries only | Good national quasi-experiments; no cross-institutional comparison. |
| 4. Pension design | Yes (US DB vs European) | **Yes, partly** | US structural/quasi-experimental; German time series; Dutch discontinuity (public sector, not teachers). |
| 5. Standing, conflict, legal exposure | Yes (KR/FR/JP) | **No** | One two-month press-reported drop in Korea. Not usable as an effect. |
| 6. Accountability and inspection | Yes (England/Finland) | **Yes, for one rule** | Sims (2016) DiD, 3.4pp, turnover not attrition. |

**The two reasons with no documented institutional contrast in the outcome sense are 1 (family formation) and 5 (professional standing and legal exposure)** — in both, the law varies enormously and nobody has measured what the variation does. Reason 2 is a near-miss: the institutional variation is the most extreme of any in this file, and the evidence base is the weakest relative to how confidently the lever is recommended.
