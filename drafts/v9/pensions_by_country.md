# Teacher pension system design and retirement-timing incentives across OECD countries

Research memo for the former-teachers policy paper. All web sources accessed 26 August 2026.

Verification labels used throughout:
- **[V]** = verified against the primary source or the publisher's own page (article page, official scheme or statistical office document).
- **[A]** = taken from an abstract, publisher summary, or reputable secondary source (search result snippet, union or ministry guidance, press coverage); the underlying primary document was not opened in full. Treat as reliable but re-check before final citation.
- **[G]** = gap: no teacher-specific evidence found; the absence is itself the finding.

Headline answer to the lead author's question: the Costrell and Podgursky (2009) finding of non-linear pension wealth accrual with spikes at rule-set ages is not a US curiosity. It is the structural signature of final-salary or career-average defined-benefit (DB) schemes, and most OECD countries still cover most of their current teaching force through exactly such schemes, usually civil-service or public-sector schemes with their own rule-set ages. Teacher-specific causal evidence that retirement timing responds to these kinks exists for at least the United States, France, Germany and the Netherlands. In NDC and DC systems (Sweden, Norway post-2011/2020, Australia, and Denmark's funded occupational tier) the accrual profile is close to actuarially neutral by design, and no comparable focal-age spike exists in the pension system itself. A comparative table is feasible and is drafted below; the scheme-type and age columns can be filled for every country from official sources, while the teacher-specific behavioural-evidence column can be filled for only about half.

## 1. United States

**Scheme.** Public school teachers are covered by state (and some city) retirement systems, almost all final-average-salary DB plans; teachers in roughly a dozen states (about 40 per cent of teachers) are outside Social Security. A minority of states have moved new hires to hybrid, cash-balance or DC choices. **[V/A]** Overview: Mihaly, K. and Podgursky, M. (2023), "Teacher Pensions: An Overview", Educational Researcher, 52(2), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X221108197 [A].

**Ages.** No single statutory age: each plan sets unreduced-benefit eligibility by age and/or service combinations (e.g. "rule of 80/85/90", 30-years-and-out, or age 60-65 thresholds), with reduced early benefits below them. **[V]** via Costrell and Podgursky (2009).

**Non-linearity.** The core reference. Costrell, R. and Podgursky, M. (2009), "Peaks, Cliffs, and Valleys: The Peculiar Incentives in Teacher Retirement Systems and Their Consequences for School Staffing", Education Finance and Policy, 4(2), 175-211, https://direct.mit.edu/edfp/article-abstract/4/2/175/10094 (open pre-print: https://edre.uark.edu/_resources/pdf/efpcostrell09.pdf). Pension wealth accrual in six state teacher plans is "highly nonlinear and heavily loaded at arbitrary years": low accrual early, sharp spikes in the mid-to-late fifties at eligibility thresholds, then flat or negative accrual (working an extra year reduces lifetime pension wealth). **[V]**

**Timing response, teacher-specific (all quasi-experimental or structural on administrative data, reputable journals):**
- Furgeson, J., Strauss, R.P. and Vogt, W.B. (2006), "The Effects of Defined Benefit Pension Incentives and Working Conditions on Teacher Retirement Decisions", Education Finance and Policy, 1(3), 316-348, https://direct.mit.edu/edfp/article-abstract/1/3/316/10040. Pennsylvania teachers 1997-99 under temporarily enhanced early-retirement incentives; pension incentives have large, significant effects on retirement. **[A]**
- Brown, K.M. (2013), "The link between pensions and retirement timing: Lessons from California teachers", Journal of Public Economics, 98, 1-14, https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/course/brown_jpube13.pdf. Uses the 1999 CalSTRS benefit-factor enhancement (maximum factor raised from 2.0 to 2.4 per cent, longevity bonus at 30 years) as a natural experiment; teachers delay retirement in response to the shifted incentive ages. **[V]**
- Ni, S. and Podgursky, M. (2016), "How Teachers Respond to Pension System Incentives: New Estimates and Policy Applications", Journal of Labor Economics, 34(4), 1075-1104, https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlabec/doi10.1086-686263.html. Structural Stock-Wise option-value model on Missouri administrative panel data; fits retirement spikes at eligibility thresholds and predicts responses to 1990s enhancements out of sample. **[V/A]**
- Koedel, C. and Xiang, P.B. (2017), "Pension Enhancements and the Retention of Public Employees", ILR Review, 70(2), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0019793916650452. The 1999 St Louis enhancement raised stay incentives dramatically but did not raise retention: a caution that back-loaded DB wealth is a blunt retention tool even while it concentrates exits at eligibility ages. **[A]**
- Fitzpatrick, M.D. and Lovenheim, M.F. (2014), "Early Retirement Incentives and Student Achievement", American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 6(3), 120-154, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.3.120. Illinois 1990s early-retirement incentive triggered large-scale teacher retirements (i.e. strong take-up of a priced window) without lowering, and probably raising, test scores. **[A]**

## 2. Germany

**Scheme.** Most established teachers are Beamte (tenured civil servants) covered by Beamtenversorgung: a non-contributory, tax-financed final-salary DB scheme (maximum 71.75 per cent of final pay after 40 years), entirely separate from the statutory pension insurance. Non-tenured contract teachers are in the general statutory scheme (gesetzliche Rentenversicherung, a points system) plus the VBL supplementary scheme. **[V/A]** Sources: dbb lexicon "Versorgungsabschlag", https://www.dbb.de/lexikon/themenartikel/v/versorgungsabschlag.html; lehrer nrw guide "Pensionierung und Ruhegehalt" (2023), https://lehrernrw.de/wp-content/uploads/2023-01-pensionierung-und-ruhestand-2023-mit-lesezeichen-stand-1-1-23.pdf.

**Ages.** Regular civil-service age limit rising in steps from 65 to 67 (mirroring the statutory scheme); many Länder let teachers retire at the end of the school year in which they reach the limit. Voluntary early retirement on request from 63 (Antragsaltersgrenze), from 60 for the severely disabled, and at any age on grounds of incapacity (Dienstunfähigkeit). **[A]** (same sources).

**Non-linearity.** Explicit and rule-based: a permanent Versorgungsabschlag of 0.3 per cent per month (3.6 per cent per year) of early retirement, capped at 10.8 per cent for incapacity retirement, was introduced from 1998 and extended in 2001 (Versorgungsänderungsgesetz) to incapacity and disability routes. Before 2001, early exit via incapacity was essentially unpriced, creating a large discontinuity in favour of early exit; after 2001 the discontinuity was substantially flattened. **[V/A]** Source: dbb lexicon above; Rechtslupe, "Versorgungsabschlag bei Dienstunfähigkeit", https://www.rechtslupe.de/verwaltungsrecht/beamtenrecht/versorgungsabschlag-bei-dienstunfaehigkeit-321132.

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** Official statistics show one of the cleanest policy-response patterns in the OECD: in 2000, the year before deductions were extended, 64 per cent of newly retiring teachers left via incapacity; from 1993 to 2001 the share exceeded half every year; by 2014-2017 it had fallen to about 11-12 per cent, and the average teacher retirement age rose accordingly. Statistisches Bundesamt (Destatis) attributes the decline to the introduction of the deductions. **[V]** Sources: Destatis press release 455/16, 15 December 2016, https://www.destatis.de/DE/Presse/Pressemitteilungen/2016/12/PD16_455_742pdf.pdf?__blob=publicationFile; Destatis press release 509/18, 2018, "Zahl der Pensionierungen von Lehrkräften 2017 erneut rückläufig", https://www.destatis.de/DE/Presse/Pressemitteilungen/2018/12/PD18_509_742.html; Altis, A., "Entwicklungen im Bereich Beamtenversorgung", Wirtschaft und Statistik 2/2011 (Destatis), https://www.destatis.de/DE/Methoden/WISTA-Wirtschaft-und-Statistik/2011/02/beamtenversorgung-022011.pdf?__blob=publicationFile. Complementary German-population causal evidence (not teacher-specific): Hanel, B. (2010) on the 1992/1997 introduction of actuarial deductions, Labour Economics, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537109001146 **[A]**; and Seibold, A. (2021), "Reference Points for Retirement Behavior: Evidence from German Pension Discontinuities", American Economic Review, 111(4), 1126-1165, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20191136 - massive bunching at statutory ages beyond what financial incentives explain; statutory ages act as reference points. **[V]**

## 3. France

**Scheme.** Teachers are fonctionnaires under the state civil-service pension code (Code des pensions civiles et militaires de retraite): final-salary DB, 75 per cent of the final six months' index salary for a full career, plus the RAFP supplementary points scheme. Pre-1990-corps instituteurs are "catégorie active" with earlier ages; professeurs des écoles and secondary teachers are "sédentaire". **[V/A]** Sources: SNES, "Les âges de la retraite", https://www.snes.edu/ma-carriere/retraites-carriere/les-ages-de-la-retraite/; Ministère de l'Education nationale, reform implementation note, https://www.education.gouv.fr/projet-de-reforme-des-retraites-les-mesures-qui-concernent-les-personnels-de-l-education-344095.

**Ages.** The 2023 reform raises the legal age from 62 to 64 (three months per birth cohort) and the full-rate contribution requirement to 43 years; active-category instituteurs' early age moves from 57 to 59. The décote-cancellation age (age at which the early-retirement penalty vanishes regardless of contribution years) remains 67 for sédentaires and 62 for actives. Note: implementation of two measures was paused for some cohorts in the 2026 social-security financing law; check status at final drafting. **[A]** Sources: education.gouv.fr above; aide-sociale.fr, "Retraite Enseignant 2026", https://www.aide-sociale.fr/retraite-enseignant/.

**Non-linearity.** Since the 2003 Fillon reform extended décote and surcote to civil servants: a permanent penalty (décote) per missing quarter for retiring before the full-rate condition is met, and a bonus (surcote) per additional quarter worked beyond it, around a moving full-rate pivot. This creates exactly the kinked accrual profile of the US plans, with the pivot defined by contribution quarters rather than a single age. **[V/A]** (sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific (the French quasi-experiment the lead author asked to verify - it checks out):**
- Baraton, M., Beffy, M. and Fougère, D. (2011), "Une évaluation de l'effet de la réforme de 2003 sur les départs en retraite. Le cas des enseignants du second degré public", Economie et Statistique, 441-442, 55-78, https://www.persee.fr/doc/estat_0336-1454_2011_num_441_1_9613. Administrative panel of public secondary-school teachers; difference across cohorts hit by the 2003 reform. The probability of retiring between 60 and 61 fell by around 9 percentage points for teachers with about 37.5 contribution years at 60. **[V]** (Persée article page confirmed; cited variously as 2011 issue/2012 print.)
- Fougère, D. and Gouëdard, P. (2021), "The effects of financial incentives and disincentives on teachers' retirement decisions: Evidence from the 2003 French pension reform", CEPR Discussion Paper 16673, https://cepr.org/publications/dp16673 (also SSRN https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3960277). High-school teachers respond significantly to the surcote incentive to work past the legal age. **[A]** (CEPR/SSRN pages returned 403; details from search summaries and the LIEPP project page, https://www.sciencespo.fr/liepp/en/content/primary-and-secondary-school-teachers-retirement-france.html.)
- CEPR Discussion Paper 15142 (2020), "Slow Down Before You Stop: The Effect of the 2010 French Pension Reform on Older Teachers' Sick Leaves", https://cepr.org/publications/dp15142. Sample of 38,652 high-school teachers; the 2010 rise in the minimum legal age lowered the probability of retiring at 60 and increased short sick leaves before retirement among affected teachers. **[A]** (authorship not verified from the blocked CEPR page; associated with the LIEPP teacher-retirement project).

## 4. Netherlands

**Scheme.** Teachers belong to ABP, the funded occupational scheme for government and education (one of the world's largest pension funds): final salary until 2004, career-average DB since, on top of the flat-rate state AOW pension. Early-retirement pre-funding (VUT, then FPU) was abolished by the 2006 VPL legislation for workers born in 1950 or later. **[V/A]** Sources: ABP, "Retirement", https://www.abp.nl/werkgevers/pensioen-bij-abp/engelstalige-pensioeninformatie/retirement; European Commission, Netherlands pension country fiche (2021), https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2021-05/nl_-_ar_2021_final_pension_fiche.pdf.

**Ages.** AOW age rose stepwise to 67 (2024) and is now linked to life expectancy; ABP allows flexible drawing between 60 and 72 with actuarial adjustment (earlier = permanently lower). **[V/A]** (sources above).

**Non-linearity.** Historically strong: VUT/FPU made early exit at the scheme window almost free, a classic spike. Post-2006 the occupational scheme is broadly actuarially adjusted around a moving target age, so the profile has been deliberately flattened; the AOW age itself remains a focal point. **[A]**

**Timing response, teacher/public-sector-specific.** The 1949/1950 birth-cohort discontinuity created by the 2006 reform is one of the cleanest natural experiments anywhere, on ABP administrative and matched survey data for public-sector (including education-sector) workers:
- Montizaan, R., Cörvers, F. and de Grip, A. (2010), "The effects of pension rights and retirement age on training participation: Evidence from a natural experiment", Labour Economics, 17(1), 240-247, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537109001146. The cut in pension rights for the 1950 cohort postponed expected retirement and raised training participation. **[A]**
- de Grip, A., Lindeboom, M. and Montizaan, R. (2012), "Shattered Dreams: The Effects of Changing the Pension System Late in the Game", Economic Journal, 122(559), 1-25, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1508396. Same discontinuity; strong effects on expected retirement and mental health of affected workers. **[A]**
- Euwals, R., van Vuuren, D. and Wolthoff, R. (2010), "Early Retirement Behaviour in the Netherlands: Evidence From a Policy Reform", De Economist, 158(3), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10645-010-9139-0 (working paper: https://www.netspar.nl/assets/uploads/vanvuuren07013.pdf). The move from the actuarially unfair VUT to the fairer FPU shifted civil servants' (including teachers') retirement later and spread it out. **[A]**

## 5. United Kingdom (England and Wales)

**Scheme.** The Teachers' Pension Scheme (TPS), an unfunded statutory public-service scheme. Final salary for legacy members (NPA 60 for pre-2007 entrants, 65 for 2007-2015 entrants); since April 2015 career average (CARE) accruing 1/57 of pay per year, revalued at CPI + 1.6 per cent in service. The McCloud remedy (2015-2022 service choice) applies. All members also accrue the state pension. **[V/A]** Sources: House of Commons Library, "Teachers' Pensions - 2015 reforms" (SN06731), https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/SN06731/; House of Commons Library briefing CBP-10179, https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10179/CBP-10179.pdf; NEU scheme guides, https://neu.org.uk/advice/your-rights-work/pensions/teachers-pension-scheme.

**Ages.** CARE NPA equals State Pension age (66, rising to 67 during 2026-28). Benefits can be drawn from the minimum pension age of 55 (57 from 2028) with actuarially reduced benefits; phased retirement is possible. Teachers never had a special early statutory age, but the legacy NPA of 60 still governs a large stock of accrued rights. **[V/A]** (sources above; TPS early-retirement factsheet, https://www.teacherspensions.co.uk/members/search/-/media/793a7407bd0a4cb1bc5f00dbb35987db.ashx).

**Non-linearity.** Present but milder than US plans: a cliff at each NPA (60/65/SPA) with an actuarial reduction for each year taken early (TPS "Actuarially Reduced Benefits"; roughly 4-5 per cent per year near NPA, set by scheme factors) and late-retirement uplifts in the CARE scheme. Because CARE accrual is uniform per year, the "peaks and valleys" wealth profile of US final-salary plans is much attenuated; the remaining focal points are the NPAs themselves. **[A]** Sources: TPS factsheet above; Teachers Financial Planning explainer, https://www.teachersfp.co.uk/teachers-pension/actuarial-reduction-and-the-teachers-pension/.

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No quasi-experimental study of UK teachers' retirement timing responding to TPS incentives was found. The closest work values TPS accrual rather than timing: Disney, Emmerson and Tetlow (IFS), "The value of teachers' pensions in England and Wales", https://ifs.org.uk/journals/value-teachers-pensions-england-and-wales. This is a genuine evidence gap worth stating in the paper.

## 6. Spain

**Scheme.** Two-track. Teachers who became career civil servants (funcionarios de carrera) before 1 January 2011 are in the Régimen de Clases Pasivas (state DB, administered with MUFACE mutualism); entrants from 2011 onwards are in the general Social Security system (Régimen General). Clases Pasivas is therefore a large closed scheme covering most of the current older teaching force. **[V/A]** Sources: CCOO Federación de Enseñanza, "Las pensiones del funcionariado docente: clases pasivas" (2024 and 2025 editions), https://fe.ccoo.es/198f00647c12c46dd2fb81facd2628fc000063.pdf and https://fe.ccoo.es/d2ec4ffee9e8c21267aea1b0c9373051000063.pdf; VidaCaixa explainer, https://www.vidacaixa.es/articulos/jubilacion-y-pensiones/jubilacion-pasiva. (Union and provider guides; flagged secondary, but consistent with the governing statute.)

**Ages.** Clases Pasivas: voluntary retirement (jubilación voluntaria) at 60 with 30 years of recognised service (since 2011 the last five years must be under Clases Pasivas); forced retirement at 65 with possible extension to 70 on request. General-scheme teachers: ordinary age 65-67 depending on contribution record, with the general early-retirement coefficients. **[A]** (sources above).

**Non-linearity.** The 60-with-30-years rule is a pronounced focal point: pension is computed from the regulador base with a service-years multiplier that reaches 100 per cent at 35 years, so a teacher who qualifies faces near-zero (even negative) pension-wealth accrual from additional work, a Spanish analogue of the US "valley". Sector documents report that a majority of eligible Clases Pasivas teachers do retire voluntarily at or near 60, in contrast to general-scheme workers. **[A]** Sources: CCOO guides above; STES congress paper on teacher retirements (2024), https://www.stes.es/Congreso-2024/09-JUBILACIONES-PANELES_CONGRESO_22-02-24-9.pdf.

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No peer-reviewed quasi-experimental study of Spanish teachers' retirement timing was found; the evidence is administrative/descriptive (concentration of departures at 60 under Clases Pasivas). The dual-regime structure (pre/post-2011 entrants) is itself a natural experiment awaiting study.

## 7. Italy

**Scheme.** Teachers are state employees in the general INPS system (the public-employee institute INPDAP was merged into INPS in 2011); Italy is transitioning from earnings-related DB to a notional defined contribution (NDC) system (Dini reform 1995), fully NDC for post-1995 entrants and pro-rata for others. The Fornero reform (Law 214/2011) harmonised public and private rules, raised ages, indexed them to life expectancy and accelerated the NDC transition. **[V/A]** Sources: ETUI, "Pension reforms in Italy: background summary", https://www.etui.org/covid-social-impact/italy/pension-reforms-in-italy-background-summary; World Bank (2019), "The Italian NDC Scheme: Evolution and Remaining Potholes", https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/522961556878860848/pdf/The-Italian-NDC-Scheme-Evolution-and-Remaining-Potholes.pdf; European Commission, 2024 Ageing Report Italy fiche, https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/document/download/82b762d7-21ce-4992-aa97-888fd2c66205_en?filename=2024-ageing-report-country-fiche-Italy.pdf.

**Ages.** Old-age pension at 67 (life-expectancy indexed) for everyone including teachers; seniority ("pensione anticipata") at 42 years 10 months of contributions (41-10 women); episodic early windows ("Quota 100/102/103", "Opzione donna") have come and gone. Teachers have no special lower age, but school-sector rules fix one retirement window per year (1 September), which concentrates all departures at the school-year boundary. **[A]** (sources above).

**Non-linearity.** Under the residual DB/mixed rules the seniority thresholds are strong focal points (retiring the year one crosses the contribution threshold); under the maturing NDC component the transformation coefficients rise with age, making accrual approximately actuarially neutral within the 62-71 window. Italy is therefore mid-transition between the two worlds. **[A]** (World Bank and EC sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** partially. The Fornero reform is widely used as a quasi-experiment for older workers generally (e.g. on productivity and displacement: Economia Politica 2017, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40888-017-0079-x), but no published study specifically on teachers' retirement timing (or its classroom effects) was found in this search. Reportable gap.

## 8. Portugal

**Scheme.** Teachers who entered public service by 31 December 2005 are in the Caixa Geral de Aposentações (CGA), the civil-service DB scheme, closed to new entrants from 2006; later entrants are in the general Segurança Social regime. Rules of the two systems have been progressively converged. **[V/A]** Sources: CGA portal, "Pensão de aposentação", https://www.cga.pt/pensao-de-aposentacao; SPN (teachers' union) FAQ on aposentação, https://www.spn.pt/Artigo/perguntas-frequentes-sobre-aposentacao-2014; DECO Proteste, https://www.deco.proteste.pt/investe/reforma/direitos/noticias/2022/02/professores-reforma-60-anos.

**Ages.** Normal pension age tracks the general regime's life-expectancy-linked age (66 years plus months, around 66y7m in the mid-2020s); early retirement possible from 55 with 30 years' service (rules vary by cohort), subject to penalties; long-career exceptions exist. Teachers have no special lower age and union campaigns for one have not succeeded. **[A]** (sources above).

**Non-linearity.** Strong early-exit pricing: a reduction of 0.5 per cent per month of anticipation plus the sustainability factor (fator de sustentabilidade) cut for early pensions, producing penalties that can exceed 30-40 per cent; conversely no penalty at the personal full age. The profile is kinked at the full-pension condition. **[A]** (SPN and DECO sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No teacher-specific causal study found. Descriptively, the heavy penalties are associated with a rapidly ageing teaching force and very late average exit; this is discussed in sector documents but not causally identified.

## 9. Austria

**Scheme.** Two-track by hire date and status. Pragmatised (tenured civil-servant) teachers are under the Beamten pension law (final-salary DB, historically 80 per cent of final pay); the 2004 Pension Harmonisation Act (APG, in force 2005) introduced a unified pension account (1.78 per cent accrual per year, best-40-years then lifetime base) with a parallel calculation (Parallelrechnung) for those under 50 in 2004; teachers hired after 2004, and all contract teachers (Vertragslehrer), are effectively under the general ASVG/APG system. **[V/A]** Sources: Öffentlicher Dienst, "Pensionsharmonisierungsgesetz BGBl I 142/2004", https://oeffentlicherdienst.gv.at/dienstrecht/novellen/pensionsharmonisierungsgesetz-bgbl-i-nr-142-2004/; Austrian Parliament press item PK0720/2004, https://www.parlament.gv.at/aktuelles/pk/jahr_2004/pk0720; ÖLI-UG teacher pension-law notes, https://oeliug.jimdoweb.com/oeli-rechtsinformation/pensionsrecht/.

**Ages.** Standard age 65 (women's ASVG age converging to 65 by 2033); "corridor pension" from 62 with sufficient insurance years, at a deduction; long-service ("Hacklerregelung") routes have been repeatedly tightened. Teachers have no special age. **[A]** (sources above).

**Non-linearity.** Deductions per year of retirement before 65 (corridor deductions of several per cent per year; various caps; the 2004 "loss cap" limited harmonisation losses to 5-10 per cent), plus bonuses for deferral: a kinked, focal-age profile during the long transition; the mature APG account is closer to neutral but not fully actuarial. **[A]** (sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** Austrian quasi-experimental retirement literature (e.g. Manoli and Weber on severance and pension thresholds) covers private-sector workers, not teachers. No teacher-specific study found.

## 10. Finland

**Scheme.** Teachers are in the public-sector earnings-related scheme, administered by Keva (municipal, state, church employers unified under the Public Sector Pensions Act JuEL from 2017), part of the national earnings-related pension system; there is no separate teacher plan. **[V]** Sources: Keva, https://www.keva.fi/en/pensions/; Finnish Centre for Pensions (ETK), public-sector providers, https://www.etk.fi/en/finnish-pension-system/administration-and-supervision/parties-to-the-pension-scheme/public-sector-pension-providers/.

**Ages.** The 2017 reform links the old-age retirement age to birth cohort (65 for those born 1962-64, thereafter tied to life expectancy); flexible drawing with an increment for deferral. Historically teachers (like nurses and police) had lower occupational retirement ages; these were abolished for new hires from 1 July 1989, with grandfathered "personal/professional retirement ages" only for pre-1989 staff who elected to keep them - so the teacher-specific early age has essentially aged out of the system. **[V/A]** Sources: Keva old-age pension pages, https://www.keva.fi/en/pensions/information-about-pensions/pension-options/old-age-pension/; Finnish Government press release, "Retirement age rising also in the public sector", https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1271139/elakeiat-nousevat-myos-julkisella-sektorilla; Työeläke.fi, https://www.tyoelake.fi/en/different-pensions/old-age-pension-your-retirement-age-is-based-on-your-year-of-birth/.

**Non-linearity.** Moderate: accrual is uniform (1.5 per cent of pay per year, no final-salary loading); early drawing of the partial old-age pension carries an actuarial 0.4 per cent per month reduction and deferral earns a 0.4 per cent per month increment, so the design is close to actuarially neutral around the cohort age; the cohort age itself remains the focal point. **[A]** (ETK/Keva sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No teacher-specific causal study found; Finnish register-based retirement studies exist for the whole workforce.

## 11. Denmark

**Scheme.** Older folkeskole teachers hired before 1993 are tjenestemænd (civil servants) with a state DB tjenestemandspension; since then teachers are employed on collective agreements with fully funded DC labour-market pensions (Lærernes Pension) plus the universal folkepension and ATP. The civil-servant tier is closed and shrinking. **[A]** Sources: Nordic Co-operation, "Danish retirement pension", https://www.norden.org/en/info-norden/danish-retirement-pension; MPISOC Denmark pension map, https://www.mpisoc.mpg.de/en/social-law/research/research-projects/pension-maps/project-website/denmark/state-pension/; TES report on Danish teachers' shift from civil-servant status, https://www.tes.com/magazine/archive/danes-mirror-british-experience.

**Ages.** Folkepension age 67 (2026), rising by statute with life expectancy to 68, 69 and, under the 2025 law, 70 for those born after 31 December 1970 - the highest legislated retirement age in Europe; teacher unions have publicly objected that classroom work is not sustainable to 70. Tjenestemænd may retire earlier with actuarial reductions (fradrag) per year before the folkepension age. **[V/A]** Sources: norden.org above; CNN, "Denmark raises retirement age to 70", 23 May 2025, https://www.cnn.com/2025/05/23/business/denmark-retirement-age-rise-70-intl-scli.

**Non-linearity.** The DC tier is actuarially neutral by construction; the closed tjenestemandspension retains early-retirement deductions and therefore a kink at the reference age. Denmark thus illustrates within one country the DB-to-DC flattening of incentives. **[A]**

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** None found.

## 12. Sweden

**Scheme.** Teachers are covered by the national system - NDC inkomstpension plus the funded premium pension (DC) and the guarantee pension - topped by sectoral occupational schemes: municipal teachers by KAP-KL/AKAP-KR (AKAP-KR is fully DC), state-sector by PA 16. The old DB occupational promises survive only for older cohorts. **[V/A]** Sources: OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2023 country note Sweden, https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/10/pensions-at-a-glance-2023-country-notes_2e11a061/sweden_6b95f735/fde8cb58-en.pdf; European Commission Sweden pension fiche, https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2021-05/se_-_ar_2021_final_pension_fiche.pdf.

**Ages.** No fixed pension age: NDC benefits are claimable from a minimum age (raised to 63 in 2023, linked to the "target age" riktålder of 67 for the guarantee pension) up to any age, with annuities recalculated by cohort life expectancy at each claiming age; employment protection lasts to 69. **[A]** (OECD note above).

**Non-linearity.** By design close to actuarially neutral: each year of delay raises the annuity roughly in line with actuarial fairness, so no pension-wealth spike exists at any particular age; retirement is correspondingly dispersed. This is the canonical contrast case to DB civil-service schemes. **[V/A]** (OECD note and EC fiche above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No teacher-specific study found - consistent with the absence of teacher-specific kinks to study. Swedish evidence on claiming behaviour concerns the whole population.

## 13. Norway

**Scheme.** Teachers belong to the Norwegian Public Service Pension Fund (Statens pensjonskasse, SPK) for occupational pension, on top of the National Insurance Scheme (folketrygden). The 2011 national reform introduced flexible, actuarially neutral drawing of the public pension from 62; the 2020 public-sector reform replaced the old 66-per-cent final-salary gross scheme with an all-years accrual "påslagspensjon" for those born 1963 or later, with life-expectancy divisors. **[V]** Sources: SPK, "Pension accrual from 2020", https://www.spk.no/en/retirement-pension/retirement-pension-and-afp-for-you-born-in-1963-or-after/pension-accrual-from-2020/; SPK, "The Norwegian pension system", https://www.spk.no/en/retirement-pension/the-norwegian-pension-system/; MPISOC Norway pension map, https://www.mpisoc.mpg.de/en/social-law/research/research-projects/pension-maps/project-website/norwegen/public-sector-occupational-pension-schemes/.

**Ages.** Flexible 62-75 drawing in both tiers for younger cohorts, with the general age of 67 as the reference; teachers do not hold a special lower age (særaldersgrense applies to police, military, etc.). **[V/A]** (SPK sources above).

**Non-linearity.** Post-reform accrual is close to actuarially neutral (divisor mechanism: later drawing = higher annual pension); the pre-2020 gross scheme had a strong focal age (65/67 with AFP top-ups) for older cohorts, which still governs those nearing retirement now. **[V/A]** (SPK sources above).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** None found; Norwegian quasi-experimental work on the 2011 flexibility reform covers private-sector AFP workers chiefly.

## 14. Japan

**Scheme.** Public school teachers were covered by the Mutual Aid Association (kyōsai kumiai) pension for local public employees, and private school teachers by the Private School Mutual Aid (PMAC/Shigaku Kyōsai); on 1 October 2015 the employee pension systems were unified and civil servants and private school personnel were absorbed into Employees' Pension Insurance (kōsei nenkin), with the mutual aid bodies remaining as administering insurers plus small occupational top-ups. **[V]** Sources: PMAC, "Structure of Public Pension System", https://www.shigakukyosai.jp/en/pension/pension03.html; Mutual Aid Association of Public School Teachers guide, https://www.kouritu.or.jp/content/files/about/kumiai_goannai_english.pdf.

**Ages.** Public pension payable from 65 (earnings-related portion's eligibility age completed its rise to 65). Separately, the mandatory retirement age (teinen) for national and local public employees including public school teachers, previously 60, is being raised one year every two years from fiscal 2023 to reach 65 in fiscal 2031, with re-employment schemes bridging the gap meanwhile. **[V/A]** Sources: Xinhua/ecns, "Japan raises retirement age of civil servants", 1 April 2023, https://english.news.cn/asiapacific/20230401/77e0c9ba1ff34e1bbbc6974d2705fbc0/c.html; Chambers, "Overview of the Japanese Mandatory Retirement Age System", https://chambers.com/articles/overview-of-the-japanese-mandatory-retirement-age-system.

**Non-linearity.** For teachers the binding discontinuity is the mandatory retirement age itself rather than pension accrual: exit is concentrated at the teinen age by employment law, with a lump-sum retirement allowance whose formula also peaks there. Claiming before 65 (from 60) carries a reduction of 0.4 per cent per month and deferral to 75 an increment of 0.7 per cent per month in the public pension: kinked but broadly actuarial. **[A]** (Chambers and MHLW sources; MHLW white paper, https://www.mhlw.go.jp/english/wp/wp-hw11/dl/11e.pdf).

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No teacher-specific econometric study found; the mandatory-age mechanics leave little scope for choice-based timing among Japanese teachers, itself a design lesson.

## 15. Korea

**Scheme.** Public school teachers are in the Government Employees Pension System (GEPS); private school teachers and staff in the separate Teachers' Pension (Korea Teachers Pension, TP/Sahak Yeonkeum), both earnings-related DB schemes distinct from the National Pension. Reforms in 2009 and 2015 cut accrual (to 1.7 per cent), raised contributions (to 9 per cent) and raised the pension eligibility age stepwise to 65 by 2033. **[A]** Sources: JILPT presentation, "Current Status of Aging in South Korea", https://www.jil.go.jp/english/events/seminar/20250326/documents/Korea.pdf; UNESCAP working paper, "Income Security for Older Persons in the Republic of Korea", https://www.unescap.org/sites/default/d8files/knowledge-products/SDD%20Working%20Paper%20Ageing%20Income%20ROK%20v1-4.pdf; Wikipedia overview (secondary), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension_policy_in_South_Korea.

**Ages.** Teachers' mandatory retirement age is 62 under the education-officials statutes (regular civil servants 60), while the GEPS pension age is moving to 65: an emerging income gap between mandatory exit and pension receipt that is a live policy dispute. **[A]** (JILPT and Korea Herald, https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10483119; the age-62 figure for teachers is well established in Korean law but was not verified here against the statute - flag for checking.)

**Non-linearity.** DB formula with a 10-per-cent-per-year (approx.) early-claiming reduction for up to five years; the mandatory age concentrates exits as in Japan. **[A]**

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No causal teacher-specific study found in English-language journals.

## 16. Australia

**Scheme.** Current teachers are in superannuation: employer-funded DC accounts under the Superannuation Guarantee (12 per cent of pay from July 2025), typically with Aware Super/state funds; the old state DB schemes for teachers (e.g. NSW State Superannuation Scheme, closed 1985-1992; QSuper DB closed 2008) survive only for long-tenured members. The means-tested Age Pension sits underneath. **[V/A]** Sources: State Super (NSW), SSS overview and FAQ, https://www.statesuper.nsw.gov.au/sss/overview and https://www.statesuper.nsw.gov.au/sss/about-sss; SuperFind guide to Australian DB funds, https://superfind.com.au/guides/defined-benefit-funds/.

**Ages.** Superannuation preservation age 60 (for those born after 30 June 1964); Age Pension age 67. Closed DB schemes have normal retirement at 60 (SSS) with early voluntary retirement from 55. No teacher-specific statutory ages. **[V/A]** (State Super pages above; OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025 lists Australia's normal retirement age at 67).

**Non-linearity.** The DC system is actuarially neutral - account balances accrue smoothly and no age-specific pension-wealth spike exists; the tax-free-from-60 rule and Age Pension means test create milder focal points at 60 and 67. Members of the closed DB schemes still face classic DB kinks at their scheme ages. **[A]**

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** No teacher-specific quasi-experimental study found; Australian retirement research focuses on preservation-age and Age Pension discontinuities for the general population.

## 17. Canada

**Scheme.** Provincial teacher pension plans, jointly sponsored DB: the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan (OTPP) is the exemplar (2 per cent of best-five average salary per year, CPP-integrated, conditional indexation); similar plans exist in Quebec (RREGOP), Alberta (ATRF), BC (Teachers' Pension Plan) etc.; plus the public CPP/OAS. **[V]** Source: OTPP, "The basics of your pension", https://www.otpp.com/en-ca/members/life-events/learning-about-the-plan/defined-benefit-pensions/.

**Ages.** OTPP: unreduced pension at the "85 factor" (age plus qualifying years = 85) or at 65; early reduced pension from 50. No statutory teacher retirement age (mandatory retirement abolished). **[V]** Sources: OTPP, "Types of pensions", https://www.otpp.com/en-ca/members/preparing-to-retire/do-your-research-before-you-retire/types-of-pensions/.

**Non-linearity.** Explicit: pensions taken before eligibility are reduced by 2.5 per cent per point short of the 85 factor, or 5 per cent per year under age 65, whichever is less - i.e. a pension-wealth spike exactly at the 85-factor date, structurally identical to US "rule of" plans. Financial-planning practice in Ontario openly organises teachers' retirement dates around reaching the factor. **[V]** Sources: OTPP pages above; Educators Financial Group, "Early retirement 101: the 85/90 factor", https://www.educatorsfinancialgroup.ca/early-retirement-before-85-90-factor/.

**Timing response, teacher-specific.** **[G]** for peer-reviewed causal work: no published quasi-experimental Canadian teacher study was found (Statistics Canada work covers mandatory-retirement rules generally, https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11f0019m/11f0019m2005271-eng.pdf). Descriptive concentration of retirements at the 85 factor is well documented by the plans themselves.

## Does pension generosity retain teachers? (US evidence, and the reconciliation with undervaluation)

This section answers the coordinator's supplementary question. Bottom line: the "generous pensions, low attrition" pattern across US states is correlational and is not supported by the quasi-experimental literature as a general causal claim. What the causal literature supports is narrower: back-loaded DB wealth exerts a strong retention pull on teachers who are close to their eligibility spike ("golden handcuffs" in the literal sense), and almost none, per dollar, on early- and mid-career teachers, who heavily discount deferred wealth. That is exactly the reconciliation the lead author proposes, and the evidence does support it, with one caveat noted under (2).

### (1) Does DB generosity correlate with, or cause, lower teacher exit?

**Correlational claims.** The claim that states or plans with better pensions have lower attrition comes mainly from descriptive work by the National Institute on Retirement Security (NIRS) and the UC Berkeley Labor Center: a six-state study (Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Kentucky, Missouri, Texas) reporting that pensions "exert a clear retention effect" and that most teachers in those states serve 20+ years; and NIRS's "three Rs" reports estimating turnover-cost savings from DB plans. **[A]** Sources: NIRS/Berkeley six-state study coverage, https://laborcenter.berkeley.edu/teacher-pensions-vs-401ks-in-six-states/ and https://www.nirsonline.org/articles/new-study-analyzes-teacher-pension-plans-in-six-states/; NIRS, "Revisiting the Three Rs of Teacher Retirement Systems", https://www.nirsonline.org/research/revisiting-the-three-rs-of-teacher-retirement-systems-recruitment-retention-and-retirement/. Caution for the paper: NIRS is funded by the public-pension community; these studies are descriptive, do not condition on salaries, unionisation or labour-market differences across states, and should be cited as correlational advocacy-adjacent evidence only, never as causal.

**Quasi-experimental and administrative evidence points the other way for all but late-career teachers:**
- Koedel, C. and Xiang, P.B. (2017), "Pension Enhancements and the Retention of Public Employees", ILR Review, 70(2), 519-551, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0019793916650452. The 1999 St Louis benefit enhancement immediately and dramatically raised the incentive to stay, yet caused no detectable increase in retention; the authors conclude such enhancements were not cost-effective retention tools. **[A]** Causal (policy discontinuity).
- Goldhaber, D., Grout, C. and Holden, K.L. (2017), "Pension Structure and Employee Turnover: Evidence from a Large Public Pension System", ILR Review, 70(4), 976-1007, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0019793916678424 (open version: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED619727.pdf). Washington State teachers who moved from the traditional DB plan to the hybrid DB-DC plan did not exhibit higher turnover; transferees' turnover was 1-4 percentage points lower. Weakening the DB "handcuff" did not raise exit. **[A]** Causal-ish (plan comparison with selection caveats the authors address).
- Quinby, L. and Wettstein, G. (2021), "Do deferred benefit cuts for current employees increase separation?", Labour Economics, 73, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537121001160 (working paper: https://crr.bc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/wp_2019-3_revised.pdf). Triple-differences on Rhode Island's 2005 cut for non-vested state employees: benefit cuts raised separation by 2.4 percentage points overall (labour-supply elasticity with respect to pension benefits of about 0.25), but teachers were significantly less responsive than other occupations. So generosity is not irrelevant, but for teachers specifically the margin is weak. **[V/A]** Causal.
- Ni, S. and Podgursky, M. (2016, cited in the US section) provide the structural counterpart: the option-value model implies the retention force of DB wealth is concentrated near eligibility spikes; and Ni, S., Podgursky, M. et al. (2022), "Teacher Pension Plan Incentives, Retirement Decisions, and Workforce Quality", Journal of Human Resources, 57(1), 272-, https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/57/1/272, extend this to workforce-quality simulations. **[A]**
- Koedel, C., Ni, S. and Podgursky, M. (2014), "Who Benefits from Pension Enhancements?", Education Finance and Policy, 9(2), 165-192, https://direct.mit.edu/edfp/article/9/2/165/10190. The late-1990s Missouri enhancements were windfalls for teachers already near retirement; novice and future teachers were made worse off through higher contribution rates. Generosity of this back-loaded kind cannot retain early-career teachers because they capture almost none of it. **[A]**
- McGee, J.B. and Winters, M.A. (2017), "How Pensions Contribute to the Premium Paid to Experienced Public School Teachers", Educational Researcher, 46(5), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0013189X17721906. NYC and Philadelphia: late-career teachers often earn a larger compensation premium from pension accrual than from salary; retention and withdrawal patterns "do not suggest DB systems prevent teachers from leaving", and back-loading penalises mobile and young teachers. **[A]** Correlational/accounting, reputable journal. On the popularisation of the term: Costrell, R. and Podgursky, M. (2010), "Golden Handcuffs", Education Next, 10(1), https://www.educationnext.org/golden-handcuffs/ **[A]**.
- The coordinator mentioned "Wiswall": no pension-retention paper by Wiswall was found; Wiswall's teacher work concerns teacher-quality dynamics, not pensions. Flagged so the lead author does not cite it by mistake. **[G]**

**Verdict on (1):** the cross-state correlation exists in descriptive data but every credible quasi-experiment that shocks pension generosity for teachers (enhancement up: Koedel and Xiang; structure weakened: Goldhaber et al; benefits cut: Quinby and Wettstein for teachers) finds small or zero retention responses outside the late career. Correlation is plausibly driven by omitted state-level factors (salary levels, unionisation, overall public-sector compensation and labour markets) and by the mechanical fact that DB states retain their near-eligibility veterans.

### (2) Reconciliation with undervaluation of deferred wealth

- Fitzpatrick, M.D. (2015), "How Much Are Public School Teachers Willing to Pay for Their Retirement Benefits?", American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 7(4), 165-188, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20140087 (NBER WP 20582, https://www.nber.org/papers/w20582). Illinois teachers offered the chance to buy an upgraded pension multiplier at varying prices revealed a willingness to pay of about 20 cents per dollar of present value of expected retirement benefits: deep undervaluation of deferred compensation. **[V/A]** Note: a published Comment exists, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy (2023), https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20200763 - not read here; check it (and any reply) before leaning on the exact 20-cent figure. **[A]** A Saint Louis University working paper replicating low teacher WTP in another setting exists (https://www.slu.edu/research/sinquefield-center-for-applied-economic-research/-pdf/working-paper-20-03-teacher-wtp-pensions-2.pdf) but is a working paper; cite cautiously if at all. **[A]**
- The proposed reconciliation - deferred wealth retains most those close to eligibility, and little per dollar for early-career teachers - is supported by the structure of the evidence: (i) Ni and Podgursky's (2016) option-value estimates locate the behavioural response at the pension-wealth spikes late in careers; (ii) Kim, Kim, Koedel, Ni, Podgursky et al. simulations (below) show late-career incentives are cheap precisely because the target group is close to the reward; (iii) Fitzpatrick's WTP subjects were mostly far from retirement, where discounting and mobility risk crush valuation; (iv) Koedel and Xiang's null is for mid-career teachers facing a distant enhancement; (v) Quinby and Wettstein's small teacher response is to cuts in far-off benefits for the non-vested. No single paper tests the full gradient directly, so the paper should present this as a coherent reading of the ensemble, not as an established stylised fact; the caveat is that Fitzpatrick's 20-cent estimate applies even to upgrades priced near retirement in her setting, which cuts slightly against a pure "distance" story (her sample includes employees buying at retirement). Flag this nuance rather than smoothing it over. **[A]**
- Behavioural reinforcement: Seibold (2021, AER, cited above) shows even at retirement, responses are driven more by rule-set reference ages than by wealth increments, consistent with focal ages, not marginal generosity, doing the work.

### (3) Cost-effectiveness: pension spending versus salary as retention instruments

- Fitzpatrick (2015) is the benchmark: if teachers value a dollar of deferred pension wealth at about 20 cents, current salary dominates pension generosity dollar-for-dollar as a recruitment and retention instrument; she concludes the results "cast doubt on the ability of deferred compensation schemes to attract employees". **[V/A]**
- Johnston, A.C. (2025), "Preferences, Selection, and the Structure of Teacher Pay", American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 17(3), 310-346, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20210763 (NBER WP 33088, https://www.nber.org/papers/w33088). Discrete-choice experiment linked to teacher-effectiveness data: for maximising teacher utility, retention or student achievement alike, schools underutilise salary and performance pay and overutilise retirement benefits. The most direct modern cost-effectiveness statement available. **[A]**
- Kim, D., Koedel, C., Kong, W., Ni, S., Podgursky, M. and Wu, W. (2021), "Pensions and Late-Career Teacher Retention", Education Finance and Policy, 16(1), 42-65, https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/edfpol/v16y2021i1p42-65.html. Structural simulations on Missouri data: targeted late-career salary bonuses and DROP-style deferred-retirement arrangements both buy extra teaching years at relatively low cost, with DROPs cheapest per incremental year because they self-select teachers revealing a preference to keep working. Untargeted across-the-board pension generosity is the expensive way to buy retention. **[V]** (verified via publisher listing and RePEc record).
- Koedel and Xiang (2017) provide the negative benchmark: the St Louis enhancement bought essentially zero retention for substantial liability, i.e. very poor cost-effectiveness. **[A]**

**Implication for the paper.** The tension the lead author flags is real but resolves cleanly: pension generosity as such is a weak and expensive retention lever except at the eligibility spike, where it is a powerful timing lever (which is the subject of the rest of this file). If the policy aim is retaining former or late-career teachers, the literature favours targeted late-career instruments (bonuses, DROPs, phased retirement) and salary over general DB enrichment; if the aim is explaining exit timing, the spike does the work, not the generosity level.

## General lessons the collected evidence supports

1. **Non-linear accrual with rule-set focal ages is the norm wherever teachers sit in final-salary or career-average DB civil-service schemes** (US states, Germany, France, Spain's Clases Pasivas, Portugal's CGA, Austria's Beamten scheme, UK legacy sections, Canadian provincial plans, closed schemes in Denmark, Norway and Australia). The "peaks, cliffs and valleys" of Costrell and Podgursky (2009) generalise, though the kink takes country-specific forms: service-year multiples (Spain 60/30, Canada factor 85, US rule-of-X), contribution-quarter pivots with décote/surcote (France), or flat percentage deductions per month of early exit (Germany, Portugal, Austria, UK).

2. **Teacher retirement timing responds to these kinks wherever it has been credibly studied.** Quasi-experimental or administrative evidence exists for four countries and all point the same way: US teachers bunch at eligibility spikes and respond to enhancements (Furgeson et al 2006; Brown 2013; Ni and Podgursky 2016); French secondary teachers cut retirement at 60-61 by about 9 percentage points after the 2003 décote/surcote reform (Baraton, Beffy and Fougère 2011) and respond to the surcote (Fougère and Gouëdard 2021); German teachers' incapacity-route early retirements collapsed from 64 per cent to about 12 per cent of exits once the 2001 deductions priced that route (Destatis); Dutch public-sector workers including education staff postponed retirement when the 2006 reform cut early-retirement wealth for the 1950 cohort (Montizaan et al 2010; de Grip et al 2012).

3. **Pricing early exit works, but with side-effects.** The same reforms that shifted exit ages produced measurable collateral outcomes: increased pre-retirement sick leave among French teachers after the 2010 reform (CEPR DP15142), lower mental health in the treated Dutch cohort ("Shattered Dreams"), and in the US, enhancement of back-loaded benefits proved a poor retention instrument (Koedel and Xiang 2017) while a well-priced early-retirement window did not harm, and may have helped, student achievement (Fitzpatrick and Lovenheim 2014). A paper on former teachers should not equate later exit with better outcomes per se.

4. **Actuarially neutral NDC/DC systems remove the focal-age spike and spread retirements** (Sweden, Norway post-2020, Australia's superannuation, Denmark's DC tier, Finland's near-neutral adjustments, Italy's maturing NDC component). Tellingly, no teacher-specific bunching literature exists for these countries; there is no kink to bunch at. The remaining discontinuities there come from outside the pension formula: minimum claiming ages, tax rules, and the state pension age.

5. **Statutory and mandatory ages matter beyond money.** Seibold (2021) shows German retirees bunch at statutory ages far in excess of what incentives explain (reference-point behaviour), and in Japan and Korea the mandatory retirement age (60 rising to 65; 62), not accrual, is the binding discontinuity for teachers. Moving the rule-set age is therefore itself an effective lever even when incentives are weak.

6. **The direction of travel is convergence and closure of special teacher/civil-service arrangements**: Spain closed Clases Pasivas to entrants (2011), Portugal closed the CGA (2006), Austria harmonised (2004), Finland abolished teachers' lower occupational ages (1989), the UK moved teachers to career-average with NPA at State Pension age (2015), Japan merged mutual-aid schemes into the general employee pension (2015), Italy merged INPDAP into INPS (2011). New teachers increasingly face the general system; the strong non-linear incentives persist mainly for the current older stock of teachers, which is exactly the population a paper on former teachers is about.

7. **Generosity is not the same lever as timing.** Descriptive cross-state US comparisons associating generous DB plans with low attrition do not survive quasi-experimental scrutiny: enhancements bought no retention (Koedel and Xiang 2017), weakening DB structure did not raise turnover (Goldhaber, Grout and Holden 2017), and teachers barely responded to deferred-benefit cuts (Quinby and Wettstein 2021), while teachers value a dollar of deferred pension wealth at roughly 20 cents (Fitzpatrick 2015) and optimal-compensation estimates say schools overweight retirement benefits relative to salary (Johnston 2025). The retention force of DB pensions is concentrated on late-career teachers near their eligibility spike; see the dedicated section above.

8. **Evidence gaps worth stating in the paper:** no OECD-wide teacher-pension synthesis exists; Education at a Glance no longer publishes teacher retirement ages (use Eurydice conditions-of-service pages for EU countries); and outside the US, France, Germany and the Netherlands there is no credible causal teacher-specific timing study, including for the UK, Canada and Spain despite sharp, well-documented kinks - each of these is a feasible future quasi-experiment (e.g. Spain's 2011 dual-regime discontinuity).
