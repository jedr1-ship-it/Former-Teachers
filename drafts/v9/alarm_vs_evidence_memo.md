# Reconciliation memo: alarm, crisis and functional attrition

Prepared for the lead author from the five audited research packets and the verified files in `drafts/v9/`. All claims below are drawn exclusively from material marked sustained or sustained with qualification, with the auditors' qualifications applied. All URLs accessed 28 August 2026.

## 1. Why is everyone so alarmist

### 1.1 The alarm is real, loud and almost never about measured attrition

Across the inventory, one pattern holds. The headline claims that sustain the crisis narrative rest on intentions, vacancies, projections and demography. The single prominent claim that is actually about attrition is UNESCO's statement that global primary-teacher attrition almost doubled from 4.62 per cent (2015) to 9.06 per cent (2022), presented in the public summary without methodology or data source (UNESCO, 2024, https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/global-report-teachers-what-you-need-know). The OECD participates in the framing despite its own data. Education at a Glance 2025 titles its new indicator "How severe are teacher shortages across countries?" while the indicator reports average attrition of 6.5 per cent across 19 systems and states that rates "have remained relatively stable in most countries and economies" over the past decade (OECD, 2025, https://doi.org/10.1787/1c0d9c79-en). This appears to be exaggeration in framing more than fabrication of figures, and it rests on four recurrent mistranslations.

### 1.2 Mistranslation one, intentions read as exits

Intention surveys supply most of the arresting numbers. The NEA declared a "five-alarm crisis" and an "exodus" on the basis of a January 2022 member poll in which 55 per cent said they planned to leave education sooner than planned (NEA, 2022, https://www.nea.org/about-nea/media-center/press-releases/nea-survey-massive-staff-shortages-schools-leading-educator-burnout-alarming-number-educators); measured exits that cycle rose from 6.5 per cent (2019) to 9.8 per cent (2020) and were back at 7.7 per cent by 2023-24 (Aldeman and Yi, 2025, https://www.educationnext.org/are-teachers-abandoning-teaching-data-show-teacher-turnover-remains-low/). In England the NEU's 2022 survey of 1,788 teachers produced the 44-per-cent-gone-by-2027 headline, and its 2023 survey of 17,891 members found 41 per cent intending to leave within five years (NEU, 2023, https://neu.org.uk/latest/press-releases/state-education-2023-recruitment-and-retention), while the administrative leaving rate stayed within its 7 to 11 per cent band. TALIS 2024 leads on the finding that one in five teachers under 30 intend to leave within five years, up 3 percentage points since 2018 and reaching 53 per cent in Latvia (OECD, 2025, https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/results-from-talis-2024_90df6235-en/full-report/sustaining-the-teaching-profession_c761a598.html).

The evidence that this translation fails is direct and same-population. Among 102,970 US teachers, 32.5 per cent of those intending to leave "as soon as possible" had left a year later, against 6.8 per cent of the rest, and the intent-exit correlation is r = 0.15; the authors state that intent "should not be considered a proxy for actual turnover" (Nguyen, Bettini, Redding and Gilmour, 2025, EEPA, working paper at https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai22-537.pdf; note the data are pre-pandemic SASS/TFS waves). During COVID the same gap appeared in real time. Roughly a quarter of US teachers expressed a desire to leave their jobs in January 2021 and 42 per cent said they had considered leaving or retiring from their current position, while attrition, as of mid-2021, had not risen (Zamarro, Camp, Fuchsman and McGee, 2021, https://edre.uark.edu/_resources/pdf/teacher_turnover_covid.pdf).

### 1.3 Mistranslation two, raw counts, turnover and vacancies read as an exit wave

England's 2023 "record 40,000 quit" coverage reported 39,930 non-retirement leavers in 2021/22 (8.8 per cent), around 44,000 with retirees (9.7 per cent); the record is a property of a larger workforce, and the rate sat inside the 2010-24 band (SecEd, 2023, https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/news/backlash-over-dfe-spin-as-record-40-000-teachers-quit-the-chalkface). The US "Great Resignation" in schools, examined with administrative data, amounts to modest movements. In Massachusetts, turnover was 14.8 per cent into fall 2020, at or below pre-pandemic levels, and rose to 17.5 per cent into fall 2021, with exits from the state's public-school workforce at 8.0 per cent then 9.4 per cent, a 15 per cent rise on the 2019 baseline of 8.2 per cent (Bacher-Hicks, Chi and Orellana, 2023, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0013189X231153659). In Washington, attrition fell in the first pandemic year, "contrary to media reports about a 'wave of resignations and retirements'", before reaching 8.91 per cent in 2022, about one point above the ceiling of the previous 37 years; the same brief notes that the 2022 figures bring the data closer to the crisis rhetoric, so the rebuttal is time-limited to 2020-21 (Goldhaber and Theobald, 2023, https://caldercenter.org/sites/default/files/2024-11/CALDER%20Policy%20Brief%2032-0223.pdf). The strongest documented state rise, Arkansas, is 5 percentage points (20 per cent) entering 2022-23 after two stable years; reading that as something short of an exodus is our inference, since the authors themselves emphasise compositional risks (Camp, Zamarro and McGee, 2024, https://doi.org/10.3102/01623737241258184).

Vacancy counts undergo the same inflation. The published national US estimate is at least 39,700 vacant posts, about 1.8 per cent of positions, alongside at least 288,000 posts held by underqualified teachers, both conservative lower bounds assembled from inconsistent state reporting (Nguyen, Lam and Bruno, 2024, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23328584241276512; the earlier working-paper pair was 36,000 and 163,000, 1.67 and 5.16 per cent, https://doi.org/10.26300/76eq-hj32). Neither the federal government nor most states publish shortage data at all, and vacancies are "highly localized, causing shortages and surpluses to coexist" (Edwards, Kraft, Christian and Candelaria, 2025, https://doi.org/10.3102/01623737241235224).

### 1.4 Mistranslation three, projections read as data

The modern US alarm was launched by a supply-demand projection, a 60,000 shortfall in 2015-16 projected to reach 112,000 by 2018, in a report that simultaneously described attrition as "hovering near 8 percent annually over the last decade" (Learning Policy Institute, 2016, https://learningpolicyinstitute.org/press-release/nation-staring-crisis-finding-keeping-qualified-teachers). The EPI escalation was definitional, counting credential gaps in high-poverty schools as part of the shortage (Garcia and Weiss, 2019, https://www.epi.org/publication/the-teacher-shortage-is-real-large-and-growing-and-worse-than-we-thought-the-first-report-in-the-perfect-storm-in-the-teacher-labor-market-series/). Germany's discourse is a duel of long-range projections. The VBE's "up to 158,000 missing by 2035" is Klemm's own high scenario commissioned by the union (VBE, 2022, https://www.vbe.de/presse/pressedienste/pressedienste-2022/politik-verschleiert-abermals-realitaet-bis-2035-fehlen-bis-zu-158000-lehrkraefte), while the KMK's arithmetic gap of about 68,000 (need 463,000, supply 395,000, 2023-2035) is built from retirements, pupil growth and policy expansion, with attrition nowhere among the named causes, and carries the KMK's own warning that such a difference "lässt allerdings keine Rückschlüsse auf die tatsächliche Lehrkräfteversorgung ... zu" (KMK, 2023, https://www.kmk.org/fileadmin/Dateien/pdf/PresseUndAktuelles/2023/2023-12-08_Erlauterungspapier_Medien_LEB-LEA_2023_KMK.pdf). Australia's official "shortage crisis" rests on a projected 4,100 secondary shortfall and falling ITE completions, announced through ministerial statements that "too many people" are leaving (Australian Government, 2022, https://ministers.education.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/Teacher%20Workforce%20Shortages%20-%20Issues%20paper.pdf), in the same years in which linked tax records showed teachers with the lowest attrition of all 97 occupation minor groups, falling from 5.9 to 5.1 per cent (Buckley and Griselda, 2025, https://e61.in/who-stays-who-goes-a-new-look-at-teacher-attrition-using-administrative-data/).

The historical precedent is documented in print. The 1999-2000 US scare converted a ten-year cumulative hiring projection into "the projected shortage of 2.2 million teachers", a distortion Wayne attributed to "mistake or ... interest group politics", noting that excluding retirements "only about one in 20 teachers leaves each year" (Wayne, 2000, https://epaa.asu.edu/index.php/epaa/article/view/438). Candidate production in fact rose over the long term from the mid-1980s, from roughly 175,000 to a peak near 300,000 education degrees around 2011 with a modest decline after, and only about half of graduates are hired in a typical year (Cowan, Goldhaber, Hayes and Theobald, 2016, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X16679145; Dee and Goldhaber, 2017, https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/es_20170426_understanding_and_addressing_teacher_shortages_in_us_pp_dee_goldhaber.pdf).

### 1.5 Mistranslation four, recruitment failure narrated as flight

France is the clearest case. The 2022 "crise d'attractivité" arose because first-degree concours candidacies nearly halved in one year, from 98,644 to 55,146 for a constant 9,900 posts, leaving 3,733 posts unfilled (16 per cent), partly an artefact of the reform moving the concours to the end of the M2 year (Assemblée nationale, 2022, https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/16/questions/QANR5L16QE7; Sénat, 2022, https://www.senat.fr/questions/base/2022/qSEQ221003531.html). Resignations of tenured teachers meanwhile ran from 0.05 per cent of titulaires (2008) to about 0.34 per cent (2021), a doubling in four years in absolute terms from 1,417 (2017-18) to 2,836 (2021-22), on a DEPP series that excludes retirement and remains among the lowest components in the OECD; France's total attrition is below 3 per cent in EAG 2025. England shows the same asymmetry, record unfilled vacancies of 6 per 1,000 teachers in 2023/24, six times the 2010/11 level, driven by postgraduate secondary recruitment at 48 per cent of target in 2023/24 and 62 per cent in 2024/25, while leaving rates returned to pre-pandemic levels (NFER, 2025, https://www.nfer.ac.uk/press-releases/government-spending-review-is-last-chance-to-meet-6-500-new-teacher-target-as-unfilled-teacher-vacancies-hit-record-high/; DfE, 2024, https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/initial-teacher-training-census/2024-25).

### 1.6 Who exaggerates, and why it works

The amplifiers are identifiable. Unions convert member intentions into exit forecasts (NEA, NEU, VBE); governments adopt crisis framing around projections (Australia, France); media convert both into exodus narratives, with the Washington Post's "catastrophic teacher shortage" of August 2022 running alongside contemporaneous academic dissent, "Attrition is definitely up, but it's not a mass exodus of teachers" (Goldhaber, quoted in Hechinger Report, 2022; discourse documented in Education Week, 2022, https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/will-there-really-be-a-mass-exodus-of-teachers/2022/02). Shortage news is cyclical, peaking near 4,000 annual mentions in 2001 and 2015 in step with economic expansions (Dee and Goldhaber, 2017, URL above), consistent with the countercyclical pattern of actual attrition (Goldhaber and Theobald, 2022, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0013189X211060840). Finally, a visibility asymmetry makes small aggregate movements feel enormous. Washington's 1.6-point attrition rise meant districts had to find over 1,000 extra hires in a state training around 3,000 new teachers a year (Goldhaber and Theobald, 2023, URL above); an unfilled post is locally visible in a way a stable national rate never is.

## 2. Is there a real crisis we missed

Yes, in three places, and the paper should say so with figures.

### 2.1 Outside the OECD

UNESCO reports global primary attrition rising from 4.62 per cent (2015) to 9.06 per cent (2022), and the need for 44 million primary and secondary teachers by 2030, 15 million in sub-Saharan Africa at an estimated US$120 billion a year in salary financing (UNESCO, 2024, https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/global-report-teachers-what-you-need-know). The doubling figure is published without methodology and should be quoted with that caveat, but the country-level numbers are unambiguous. Primary attrition is reported at 28 per cent in Benin and 21 per cent in Sierra Leone (figures from around 2020-22), against a mid-2010s average of 4.8 per cent across 30 sub-Saharan countries, a comparison that mixes vintages and should be flagged as such (UNESCO/Teacher Task Force, 2023, https://teachertaskforce.org/sites/default/files/2023-11/2023_TTF-UNESCO_Global-report-on-teachers-Addressing-teacher-shortages_EN.pdf). Nothing in the OECD record resembles these levels.

### 2.2 The rising-attrition minority within the OECD

The verified OECD map identifies a small group where the exit rate itself is moving. Denmark's five-year cohort attrition rose from 18 to 23.7 per cent (2013 to 2018 cohorts); Estonia runs at 8 to 12 per cent a year with exits up by a third in two years; Lithuania averages 7 per cent and rising, with the OECD's highest measured rate (12.5 per cent); Korea's total exits rose from 8,367 (2017) to 11,766 (2022, about 2.7 per cent); Hungary is partial but rising (`drafts/v9/attrition_oecd_map.md`, sources in the sweep files). EAG 2025 itself flags Austria and Estonia as up more than 2 percentage points over the decade (OECD, 2025, URL above). Voluntary components are rising beneath stable or falling totals in France (0.05 to 0.34 per cent), Japan (non-retirement about 1.4 to 2.0 per cent) and Germany (non-age component 2.5 to 4.5 per cent). These cases deserve alarm of a specific, national kind; they do not generalise.

### 2.3 The entry side, which is genuinely deteriorating

Where attrition is flat, recruitment frequently is collapsing. England achieved 62 per cent of its postgraduate secondary target in 2024/25, up from 48 per cent, with physics at 688 entrants against a target of 2,250, 31 per cent of target, a shortfall of around 70 per cent (DfE, 2024, https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/initial-teacher-training-census/2024-25). France left 3,185 of 27,589 concours posts unfilled in 2024, improving in 2025 (first-degree unfilled posts down 55 per cent to 525), though external CAPES mathematics still lost 253 posts, about one in four (Vie-publique, 2024, https://www.vie-publique.fr/en-bref/294892-concours-enseignants-2024-3-185-postes-non-pourvus). US teacher-preparation enrolment fell by more than a third between 2010 and 2018, with completers down from nearly 214,000 to about 160,000 (NCTQ, 2023, https://www.nctq.org/research-insights/data-brief-how-do-trends-in-teacher-preparation-enrollment-and-completion-vary-by-state/). Norwegian applications to teacher education fell 21.9 per cent in a single year, the first-choice share of applicants falling from 10.2 per cent (2019) to 6.3 per cent (2023), the fourth consecutive decline (Khrono, 2023, https://www.khrono.no/krisetall-for-laererutdanningen/776263). The supply identity closes the reconciliation. Shortage equals demand growth plus retirements plus attrition minus entry, and in every documented OECD case the moving term is entry or demand.

### 2.4 Concentration by subject, school and place

Aggregate stability masks severe local churn. US turnover (movers plus leavers, 2011-13 data) is nearly 50 per cent higher in Title I schools and nearly 70 per cent higher for mathematics and science teachers in those schools (Carver-Thomas and Darling-Hammond, 2019, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1213629.pdf). England's physics teachers leave the state-funded sector at 11 per cent a year against 9.8 per cent for secondary overall (NFER, 2025, https://www.nfer.ac.uk/blogs/a-widespread-lack-of-specialist-physics-teachers-persists-due-to-recruitment-and-retention-challenges/). The Dutch primary shortage is 8.1 per cent of teacher employment nationally, about 7,730 FTE, and 15.8 per cent inside the five largest cities against 6.6 per cent outside (OCW Trendrapportage via Arbeidsmarktplatform PO, 2024, https://arbeidsmarktplatformpo.nl/dossier/landelijke-aanpak-personeelstekort/feiten-en-cijfers/). This is the empirical content behind Edwards et al.'s coexisting shortages and surpluses.

### 2.5 Demography

Retirement bulges, rather than resignations, will drive replacement demand in several systems. The share of secondary teachers aged 50 or over rose from 31 to 56 per cent in Portugal and 39 to 56 per cent in Greece between 2013 and 2023; Italy remains among the oldest at 52 per cent despite a marked decline from 69 per cent (OECD EAG 2025, indicator D8, https://doi.org/10.1787/1c0d9c79-en). Germany's 68,000 projected gap is explicitly demographic (KMK, 2023, URL above), and Dutch teachers working past 65 rose about 46 per cent since 2020.

## 3. How can leaving be good

### 3.1 The conceptual case is settled outside education

Management science established forty years ago that counting all exits as loss overstates the problem; the functional taxonomy separates exits from which the organisation benefits from those it should mourn (Dalton, Todor and Krackhardt, 1982, https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/amr.1982.4285499), and the optimal aggregate turnover rate is greater than zero and organisation-specific, the rate that minimises the sum of retention and turnover costs (Abelson and Baysinger, 1984, https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amr.1984.4277675). Economics supplies the mechanism. Jobs are experience goods whose match quality is revealed only on the job, so separations concentrated early in tenure are efficient dissolutions of poor matches (Jovanovic, 1979, https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v87y1979i5p972-90.html). This is directly verified for teaching. Teacher-school match quality, estimated from output data, has two-thirds the explanatory power of teacher quality itself, and teacher effectiveness rises after a move (Jackson, 2013, https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article/95/4/1096/58314/Match-Quality-Worker-Productivity-and-Worker).

### 3.2 In practice, attrition is selective in the right direction, on average

US value-added evidence consistently finds leavers less effective than stayers, especially in the first year. In New York City, 23 per cent of first-year fourth- and fifth-grade teachers in the bottom quartile of maths value-added transferred or left against 15 per cent of the top quartile, and two-thirds of first-years leaving the state system were below the median stayer, although the pattern is much weaker and inconsistent in years two and three (Boyd, Grossman, Lankford, Loeb and Wyckoff, 2008, https://www.nber.org/papers/w14022). North Carolina replicates the average pattern, more effective early-career teachers being likelier to stay in their schools and in the profession (Goldhaber, Gross and Player, 2011, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ910058). Managed selectivity yields measured gains. Under Washington DC's IMPACT evaluation system, exits induced among low performers raised achievement by 0.21 SD in maths and 0.14 in reading, concentrated in high-poverty schools (Adnot, Dee, Katz and Wyckoff, 2017, https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373716663646); simulation puts the value of deselecting the bottom 5 to 8 per cent at around 0.4 SD (Hanushek, 2011, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2010.12.006); effective principals practise strategic retention, retaining good teachers and shedding weak ones (Grissom and Bartanen, 2019, https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831218797931).

### 3.3 The strict conditions, without which the argument becomes complacency

Four conditions bound the claim. First, who leaves. Selectivity is imperfect and sometimes bimodal; in Florida both the least and the most effective exit at elevated rates, and mobility worsens sorting across schools (Feng and Sass, 2017, https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/32836/1001506-Teacher-Quality-and-Teacher-Mobility.pdf), and the DCPS gains arose under a high-stakes evaluation regime, not from spontaneous attrition. Second, who replaces them. Turnover degrades the replacement pool where supply is thin; a 10-point rise in North Carolina turnover raises the novice share by 3.6 points and provisional licences by 1.3 points (Sorensen and Ladd, 2020, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED591843.pdf), and replacement quality moves with the outside labour market, recession entrants being about 0.10 SD more effective in maths (Nagler, Piopiunik and West, 2020, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/705883). Third, where the churn lands. Turnover harms achievement where it clusters, most in schools with many low-performing and Black pupils (Ronfeldt, Loeb and Wyckoff, 2013, https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831212463813), through lost school-specific experience and grade reassignment (Hanushek, Rivkin and Schiman, 2016, https://hanushek.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Hanushek%2BRivkin%2BSchiman%202016%20EER%2055.pdf); in England a 10-point rise in the entry rate lowers GCSE scores by about 0.5 per cent of a SD via incoming teachers' lack of school-specific human capital (Gibbons, Scrutinio and Telhaj, 2021, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2021.102079). Fourth, no numerical optimum exists. The most direct test finds an inverted-U only for demanding secondary outcomes, with a monotonically negative relationship for the core task (Meier and Hicklin, 2008, https://academic.oup.com/jpart/article-abstract/18/4/573/1090880), so the paper should assert that the optimum exceeds zero while declining to name it.

## 4. What the paper should say

1. State the stability finding plainly, with the long series (US, England, Washington, Sweden, Israel) carrying the weight.
2. Treat the alarm as a documented chain of four mistranslations (intentions, counts, projections, recruitment), naming the actors and citing Wayne (2000) as precedent.
3. Concede immediately and specifically where alarm is justified, the developing world, the rising-attrition minority (Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania, Korea, Hungary), the entry-side collapse, and concentration by subject and school.
4. Present the supply identity so stable exits and real shortage are shown to be arithmetically compatible.
5. Quote intention figures only alongside the Nguyen et al. conversion rate.
6. Make the functional-turnover case through Jovanovic, Jackson and Adnot et al., always naming the IMPACT setting.
7. State the four conditions (selectivity, replacements, location, no known optimum) in the same breath as the benefit.
8. Keep Ronfeldt et al. as the standing counterweight, never as an afterthought.
9. Avoid asserting a numerical healthy rate anywhere.
10. Draft passage for the paper follows.

> Teacher attrition, where it has been measured over decades, is stable and unexceptional. In fact, the leaving rate moves within a narrow band in every long administrative series, and teachers leave their profession at rates similar to, or lower than, comparable professions. For example, in the United States attrition has run between 5 and 8.5 per cent a year since 1988, about the rate at which nurses leave nursing (Aldeman and Yi, 2025), and in Washington State it stayed between 5 and 8 per cent for 35 consecutive years (Goldhaber and Theobald, 2022); in Australia, linked tax records show teachers with the lowest attrition of all 97 occupation minor groups, falling among early-career entrants (Buckley and Griselda, 2025); in England the rate has sat inside a 7 to 11 per cent band since 2010, and only a fifth of teachers who declare an imminent intention to leave are gone a year later (Nguyen et al., 2025, report a third in the United States). The shortages governments correctly observe arise elsewhere, in collapsed recruitment into training, in retirement bulges, and in the concentration of exits in particular subjects and disadvantaged schools, while attrition itself reaches genuinely alarming levels only outside the OECD and in a small group of systems where the rate is measurably rising. A moderate level of leaving is, moreover, consistent with a healthy occupation, since early exits dissolve poor matches and departures of the least effective can raise achievement where capable replacements exist (Adnot et al., 2017). Nonetheless, none of this excuses the places where turnover clusters and harms, and the case for calm about the average is also the case for precision about the margins (Ronfeldt et al., 2013).
