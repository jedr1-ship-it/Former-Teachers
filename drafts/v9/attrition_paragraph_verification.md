### Expert caveats to state for Aldeman and Yi (claim 1, CPS measurement)

1. Occupation coding error. The CPS estimate compares an occupation reported now with an occupation reported for a year earlier; independently coded occupation pairs are known to overstate true occupational change substantially (misclassification in either report counts as a "leaver"). The level of 7.6% therefore carries an upward measurement bias of unknown size; the cross-profession comparison survives if the error is similar across professions, which is plausible but untested.
2. Proxy responses. Roughly half of CPS person records are answered by another household member, adding misreporting of occupation.
3. Small profession samples. Aldeman and Yi pool a decade of CPS data (2015 to 2024) because annual samples of nurses, accountants and social workers are small; year-to-year comparisons are noisy.
4. Destination-blind definition. "Leaving the profession" includes retirements and exits to non-employment, not only career switches; Harris and Adams show the teacher excess is concentrated at retirement ages.
5. Venue. Education Next is research-based commentary, not peer reviewed; its credibility here rests on replicating the peer-reviewed Harris and Adams (2007) with public microdata.

## How the paragraph should change

1. Claim 4 sentence, as written, is false and must be replaced. On the extended country sweep, the defensible statement is: occupation-level annual exit-rate benchmarks exist in one further OECD system (Sweden), sector-level or stock-based comparisons exist in several more (Flanders, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Denmark, Norway), and everywhere else the comparison is simply absent. Suggested rewording: "Comparable benchmarks are rarer elsewhere: Swedish register data show annual exits of at most about 2 per cent across teachers, preschool teachers, nurses and police alike (Adermon and Laun, 2018), while Flemish, Dutch and Swiss statistics compare education only with other sectors or with the whole economy; for most OECD systems no cross-profession benchmark has been published at all." A shorter fix: replace "for any other OECD system" with "for most other OECD systems, the main exception being Sweden (Adermon and Laun, 2018)".
2. Claim 2 needs a definitional flag, since the Australian 5 to 6 per cent is a stricter measure than the US and English figures. Insert, for example: "on a stricter definition that counts only those not teaching in any of the next three years". The "lowest of the 97 occupation groups" clause can stand unqualified, as the ranking uses one definition for all occupations.
3. Claim 1 attribution: keep both citations but let Harris and Adams carry the ordering they actually found. Option: "7.6% of teachers leave the profession in a given year, similar to nurses and accountants and well below social workers (Aldeman and Yi, 2025), an ordering already visible in the 1990s (Harris and Adams, 2007)". If the sentence keeps "about the rate at which nurses leave nursing", it should rest on Aldeman and Yi alone, since Harris and Adams measured teachers above nurses (7.73 v 6.09).
4. Claim 6: add the setting in-line, e.g. "Where low-performing teachers were induced to leave under Washington DC's IMPACT evaluation system, their replacements raised student achievement by 0.21 standard deviations in mathematics and 0.14 in reading, gains concentrated in high-poverty schools (Adnot et al., 2017)". Avoid wording implying that ordinary, unmanaged attrition produces these gains.
5. Claim 7: if a size is quoted, use a range defensible from verified text: "with mathematics losses of up to roughly 10 per cent of a standard deviation under complete turnover" (covers the verified 5.6 to 9.4 and the reported published 8.2 to 10.2), or drop the number. The clause "in disadvantaged schools most of all" is fully sustained.
6. Claim 8: hedge the first clause and widen the second, e.g. "A degree of attrition is consistent with an occupation connected to its labour market, and the object of policy is less the level of departure than its composition and its concentration, since turnover disrupts most where it clusters in disadvantaged schools (Ronfeldt, Loeb and Wyckoff, 2013)."
7. Verification-status footnote for the lead author: Harris and Adams (2007) and the published Ronfeldt et al. (2013) body text remain unavailable in full open text; their headline findings are verified from abstracts, publisher-page snippets and the near-final open working papers. Everything else in the paragraph is now verified from full primary text.

## Country coverage extended

Systematic sweep for any published comparison of teacher exit rates with other professions, country by country. "Real comparison" means a published source that sets a teacher (or education) exit measure against at least one non-teaching comparator on a common definition. Verification status marked per item.

The paragraph may truthfully say that most OECD systems publish no such benchmark, but not that none does.
