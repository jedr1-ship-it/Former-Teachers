## TASK A — TALIS evidence

### A1. Is the teaching profession valued in society?

**OECD average.** The TALIS 2024 report text does not print an OECD-average share for the
"valued in society" item (the average sits in Table 7.24, distributed via stat.link, which was
unreachable); the report gives the cross-country range instead. The last published OECD average
is from TALIS 2018: "On average across the OECD countries and economies that participate in
TALIS, only 26% of teachers 'agree' or 'strongly agree' that their profession is valued in society"
(TALIS 2018 Vol. II, p. 77, Figure II.2.1, Table II.2.1). Principals: 37% (ibid., p. 33, Table II.2.8).

**Cross-country range, TALIS 2024 (lower secondary).** "While over eight in ten teachers 'agree' or
'strongly agree' that teachers are valued in society, in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Viet Nam, less
than one in ten teachers report the same in the French Community of Belgium, Croatia, France,
Hungary, Portugal, the Slovak Republic and Slovenia (Table 7.24)." (TALIS 2024, p. 241.)
Executive summary: "teachers feel most valued in Viet Nam, where over 92% say they feel valued
by society" (TALIS 2024, p. 28). TALIS 2018 best/worst for comparison: Viet Nam 92%,
Singapore 72%, UAE 72%, Korea 67%; 5% or less in the French Community of Belgium, the
Slovak Republic and Slovenia (TALIS 2018 Vol. II, p. 77).

**Trend.** 2013→2018: "Since 2013, 8 education systems have experienced significant deterioration
in the perceived prestige of the profession, but 12 have seen significant improvement … sometimes
by over 10 percentage points" (TALIS 2018 Vol. II, p. 33, Table II.2.5). 2018→2024: "In around
one-third of education systems, the share of teachers who think the teaching profession is valued
in society has increased since 2018 (Figure 7.5). This increase is over 10 percentage points in
Bulgaria, Colombia, Denmark, Kazakhstan, Saudi Arabia and Shanghai (China). In contrast, the
share … has decreased by more than 10 percentage points since 2018 in Alberta (Canada)*,
Australia, Finland, Korea and Norway*." (TALIS 2024, p. 242, Table 7.29.) Note for the paper's
Finland anchor: Finland is one of the five systems with a >10-point fall since 2018.

**Link to retention (TALIS 2024, p. 242, Table 7.30).** "Teachers who agree that their profession is
valued in society are more than 10% less likely to want to leave teaching within the next five years,
on average, after controlling for teacher and school characteristics, teachers' intrinsic motivation,
contract modalities and satisfaction with the terms of employment (including salary)." Chapter 7
highlight (p. 231): "Teachers' sense of value matters, and their social status can be improved."

**Adjacent standing items (TALIS 2024).**
- Valued by policymakers: from over eight in ten (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Uzbekistan, Viet Nam) to
  "less than one in ten in Croatia, Estonia, France, Italy, Portugal, Slovenia and Spain (Table 7.24)"
  (p. 239). Teachers who agree are "around 35% less likely to want to leave teaching within the
  next five years" before full controls (p. 240, Table 7.27).
- Valued in the media: over eight in ten in Saudi Arabia, UAE, Uzbekistan, Viet Nam; "less than one
  in ten … in the French Community of Belgium, Croatia, France, Japan and Poland (Table 7.24)"
  (p. 241); associated with 25% lower intention to leave before full controls (p. 242, Table 7.28).

Worst-ranked item: administrative workload. Verbatim (p. 115): "On average, around half of
teachers report general administrative work as a source of stress (52%), the largest share
compared to all other demands." Chapter 3 highlight (p. 104): "Administrative workload remains a
considerable demand for teachers."

Selected within-item detail:
- Discipline: 55% of novice vs 41% of experienced teachers (p. 122).
- Parents' concerns: 44% of female vs 35% of male teachers (p. 130, Table 3.53); above 50% in
  Japan, Korea, Lithuania, Portugal and Slovenia (p. 129).
- Change/reform: 44% of teachers "would like to see a period of stability before new changes are
  introduced"; 31% agree "too many change initiatives are introduced in their schools"; 31% "report
  being asked to implement changes without the necessary resources" (pp. 130–131, Table 3.54).
  Highlight (p. 104): "stress is more strongly associated with the experience of constant,
  unsupported change … than to resource shortages."

TALIS 2018 comparison (same item battery, OECD average-31, "quite a bit"/"a lot", Table II.2.43,
report pp. 94–95): administrative work 49%; being held responsible for students' achievement 44%;
keeping up with changing requirements from authorities 41%; too much marking 41%; maintaining
classroom discipline 38%; addressing parent or guardian concerns 34%; too much lesson
preparation 33%; modifying lessons for students with special needs 31%; too many lessons to
teach 28%; extra duties due to absent teachers 25%; intimidated or verbally abused by students
14%. (Also: "18% of teachers report experiencing a lot of stress in their work", p. 58/92.)
Direction of travel 2018→2024 on the professional-factor items: parents' concerns 34→42,
discipline 38→45, accountability for achievement 44→45, admin 49→52, verbal abuse 14→18.
(Caveat: country coverage differs slightly between rounds — OECD average-31 in 2018 vs the
2024 OECD averages; use "broadly comparable", not exact trend arithmetic.)

**Stress and intention to leave (TALIS 2024, p. 235, Figure 7.2).** "Regression analyses indicate
that discipline and behaviour issues might be more closely associated with teachers' intention to
leave teaching than many other demands, such as workload, adapting teaching to diverse learning
needs, accountability and keeping up with reforms … teachers who report being intimidated or
verbally abused by students, or who consider maintaining classroom discipline as sources of
stress 'quite a bit' or 'a lot' are twice as likely to wish to leave teaching within five years."

### A3. Autonomy, satisfaction (profession vs school), and career intentions

**Instructional autonomy (TALIS 2024, Ch. 5).** Share of teachers with "substantial" or "full"
autonomy, OECD average (Table 5.31): designing and preparing lessons 93%; selecting teaching
methods 92%; implementing the curriculum flexibly "three out of four teachers" (~75%); selecting
learning objectives 71% (pp. 185–186). "Notably, only four education systems – Bulgaria,
Colombia, Italy and Viet Nam – report that more than 85% of teachers have substantial or full
autonomy across all five dimensions" (p. 186). Link to satisfaction and well-being (p. 193,
Figure 5.11/Table 5.61): "Instructional autonomy is consistently positively associated with
teachers' job satisfaction, before and after controlling for teacher and school characteristics …
teachers who report greater instructional autonomy are also less likely to experience stress at
work" (exceptions: Norway*, United States). Chapter 5 highlight (p. 169-171 area): teachers with
higher autonomy report higher adaptability and confidence. TALIS 2024 does not report a direct
autonomy→intention-to-leave regression in the body text.

**Satisfaction with profession vs current work environment (TALIS 2024, Figure 2.4, pp. 75–76,
Tables 2.6 and 2.8).** TALIS separates "satisfaction with the teaching profession" ("All in all, I am
satisfied with my job"; "The advantages of being a teacher clearly outweigh the disadvantages"; "If
I could decide again, I would still choose to work as a teacher"; "I regret that I decided to become
a teacher") from "satisfaction with the current work environment" ("I enjoy working at this school";
"I would recommend this school as a good place to work"; "I would like to change to another school
if that were possible"). "Almost nine in ten teachers report that, all in all, they are satisfied with their
jobs (on average), ranging from 79% in Japan to 98% in Albania" (p. 76). "Four out of five teachers,
or more depending on the measure, report being satisfied with their current work environment, on
average (Table 2.8)" (p. 76). Contrast worth printing: near-90% job satisfaction coexists with
single-digit "valued in society" shares in seven European systems (A1).

**Career intentions (TALIS 2024, Ch. 7).** "Two out of ten teachers under age 30 report intending
to leave teaching within the next five years, on average" (p. 232, Figure 7.1, Table 7.1); up
3 percentage points since 2018 (p. 233, Table 7.2). Among teachers not about to retire, 17%
intend to leave within five years, "ranging from less than 6% in Korea, Portugal and Viet Nam to
over 30% in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania (Table 7.6)" (p. 234). Most common reasons among
under-30 intending leavers: "personal or family reasons (46%), a job outside of education (46%),
and further education or training (42%) (Table 7.5)" (p. 233). Retention correlates (all p. 231
highlights unless noted): overall job satisfaction → "five times less likely to want to leave teaching
within the next five years" (p. 235, Table 7.8); satisfaction with employment terms excluding salary
→ "almost 40% less likely" (pp. 231, 254 area, Table 7.x); salary satisfaction → 25% less likely
(p. 254 area); "Salary is just one of many factors shaping teachers' decisions to leave the
profession. Intrinsic motivations … are more consistent predictors of teachers' career intentions"
(p. 231). Joy of teaching: intention to leave is "18 percentage points lower in the upper quartile of
teaching enjoyment compared to the bottom quartile (Table 7.23)" (p. 239).

### A4. Parents, complaints and abuse in TALIS 2024

- **Feeling valued by parents.** "Some 65% of teachers report that in their school, teachers are
  valued by parents and guardians, on average (Table 6.38). Over 90% of teachers report this in
  Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Uzbekistan and Viet Nam. On the other end of the scale,
  less than 50% of teachers say they are valued by parents and guardians in the French
  Community of Belgium, Croatia, France and Japan." (p. 222; chapter highlight version p. 200.)
  By comparison, around seven in ten teachers feel valued by students (53%–96% range,
  Table 6.29, p. 217).
- **Parental complaints as a stressor.** "Addressing parents' concerns is a source of stress for
  approximately 42% of teachers on average, and for more than 50% in Japan, Korea, Lithuania,
  Portugal and Slovenia" (p. 129, Table 3.16) — framed by TALIS as "another form of
  accountability". Female teachers 44% vs male 35% (p. 130).
- **Abuse directed at teachers (principals' reports).** "Verbal abuse or intimidation directed at
  teachers or staff is less prevalent. According to principals' reports, these concern 5% of teachers
  on average. On school premises, the figure exceeds 15% in Australia, the Flemish Community of
  Belgium and Malta. Online teacher intimidation … concerns 1% of teachers across OECD
  education systems (Table 3.38)." (p. 122.) Caution: this item covers abuse from any source, not
  parents specifically; TALIS 2024 has no measure of parental complaints procedures or legal risk.

## TASK B — country examples beyond Finland

(Finnish anchor per the paper: Räsänen et al. 2020, lack of professional commitment ranked first
among teachers intending to leave.)

### B1. England — DfE Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders (government statistical research series)

- Wave 2 (fieldwork 2023; report September 2024). 36% of teachers and leaders were considering
  leaving the state sector within 12 months for reasons other than retirement, up from 25% in 2022
  (main report, p. 252, Table 13.4). Among those considering leaving, "High workload was the most
  important factor … 94% … rated this as important or very important (Figure 13.1). The next most
  important reasons were stress and/or poor wellbeing (important for 84%), and teachers' views
  not being valued by policymakers (83%)." (p. 260.) So a pure standing/valued motive ranks
  **third of sixteen**, ahead of pay (63%), pupil behaviour (41%) and dealing with parents (41%).
- Standing context, wave 2: "The majority (76%) of teachers and leaders disagreed that the
  teaching profession was valued by society. This is a larger proportion than in 2022, when 69%
  disagreed … the proportion strongly disagreeing also increased, from 33% to 43%." (p. 249,
  Figure 12.9.)
- Wave 4 (fieldwork 2025; summary report November 2025). 29% considering leaving (p. 31).
  Reasons among considerers: stress/poor wellbeing 89%, high workload 89%, lack of state school
  funding 76%, "Teachers' views not being valued by policymakers" 71% — still fourth of a longer
  list and ahead of pay (49%) (Figure 10.1, pp. 32–33). Standing trend (Table 7.1, p. 25): agreement
  that "teaching profession is valued by society" 17% (2022) → 12% (2023) → 12% (2024) → 15%
  (2025); agreement that "teachers' views are valued by policymakers" 6% → 4% → 5% → 7%.

### B2. United States — NCES Teacher Follow-up Survey 2012–13 (federal statistical release, actual leavers)

Goldring, Taie and Riddles (2014), NCES 2014-077. Among public school teacher **leavers**
working in another occupation, asked whether aspects were better in teaching or in the current
position (Table 7, p. 13):
- "Professional prestige": better in current position **52.2%**, better in teaching 8.4% (interpret
  the 8.4% with caution per NCES CV flag), not better or worse 39.4%.
- "Autonomy or control over own work": better in current position **57.4%**, better in teaching 11.7%.
- "Influence over workplace policies and practices": better in current position **58.5%**.
- "Recognition and support from administrators/managers": better in current position **44.9%**.
- For contrast, salary was better in the current position for only 43.5% and benefits for 9.5% —
  i.e. leavers report larger gains in prestige, autonomy and influence than in pay.
Most-important-reason distribution for leaving (Table 5, p. 11): left involuntarily 9.7%; personal
life factors 38.4%; career factors 13.0%; salary and other job benefits 6.8% (CV flag); school
factors 6.3%. (The TFS single-choice categories do not include a "feeling valued" option; the
Table 7 comparisons are the standing evidence.) The TFS was the last dedicated federal
follow-up of leavers; no newer TFS wave exists.

### B3. Australia — Brandenburg et al. 2024 (peer-reviewed national study of actual leavers)

Brandenburg et al. (2024), *The Australian Educational Researcher* 51:2381–2400 (open access).
Survey of 256 former teachers who left Australian schools mostly between 2016 and 2022; 175
nominated a single key reason for leaving (Table 1, p. 2389): school leadership 17.71%; workload
16.57%; workplace environment 9.71%; student behaviour 7.43%; **lack of respect 6.86%**
(fifth); lack of personal satisfaction 5.71%; administrative load 5.14%; salary only 4.03%
(eighth). Summing the explicitly standing/purpose-coded single-choice categories (lack of
respect, lack of personal satisfaction, lack of recognition 1.14%, lack of professional recognition
1.14%, low status of profession 0.57%) gives 15.4% — more than double the salary share — but
note this summation is ours, not the authors'.
Government context (AITSL, *National Trends: Teacher Workforce*, June 2025, 2023 Australian
Teacher Workforce Survey, ~50,500 respondents,
https://www.aitsl.edu.au/research/australian-teacher-workforce-data/atwd-reports/national-trends-teacher-workforce-jun2025):
39% of teachers intended to leave before retirement; top cited reasons workload 75%, work-life
balance 69%, stress/wellbeing 68%, administrative workload 63%, insufficient pay 52%.
(Accessed via the AITSL report page 2026-09-02; the page is dynamic — figures should be
re-checked against the page before print. An earlier ATWD release reporting "recognition and
reward" as the third most common reason (66%, 2022 survey) could NOT be verified against a
primary PDF — the AITSL file now 404s — so do not print that figure.)

Rejected/not used: Merrimack/EdWeek teacher survey (press-adjacent), Pew Research 2024
(neither government nor peer-reviewed), Monash "Perceptions of Teaching" reports (university
report series, not peer-reviewed in themselves), the AITSL Key Metrics Dashboard June 2024
(primary file no longer retrievable).

## Safe sentences the paper could print

Each sentence is fully supported by the page cited.

1. "In TALIS 2018, only 26% of teachers across the OECD agreed that their profession is valued
   in society, and in TALIS 2024 the share still ranged from over 80% in Viet Nam, Uzbekistan and
   Kazakhstan to under 10% in France, Portugal, Hungary, Croatia, Slovenia, the Slovak Republic
   and the French Community of Belgium." (TALIS 2018 Vol. II, p. 77; TALIS 2024, p. 241.)
2. "Between 2018 and 2024, the share of teachers who felt their profession was valued in society
   fell by more than ten percentage points in Finland, Australia, Korea, Norway and Alberta
   (Canada)." (TALIS 2024, p. 242.)
3. "Teachers who agree that their profession is valued in society are more than 10% less likely to
   want to leave teaching within five years, even after controlling for intrinsic motivation, contract
   type and satisfaction with pay and other employment terms." (TALIS 2024, p. 242, Table 7.30.)
4. "Administrative workload is the most widely reported source of stress in TALIS 2024 — cited by
   52% of teachers across the OECD, the largest share of any demand — followed by maintaining
   classroom discipline and accountability for student achievement (45% each), and addressing
   parents' concerns (42%)." (TALIS 2024, pp. 115, 122, 128–129, Table 3.16.)
5. "Teachers who find classroom discipline highly stressful, or who report being intimidated or
   verbally abused by students, are twice as likely to want to leave teaching within five years — a
   stronger association than for workload, accountability or reform-related stress." (TALIS 2024,
   pp. 231, 235.)
6. "Although almost nine in ten teachers say they are satisfied with their jobs, TALIS distinguishes
   satisfaction with the profession from satisfaction with the current school, and it is the
   profession-level signals — feeling valued, enjoying teaching, intrinsic motivation — that predict
   intentions to stay more consistently than salary." (TALIS 2024, pp. 76, 231.)
7. "About two-thirds of teachers (65%) feel valued by parents and guardians at their school, but
   addressing parents' concerns is a source of stress for 42% of teachers — and for more than half
   in Japan, Korea, Lithuania, Portugal and Slovenia." (TALIS 2024, pp. 129, 222.)
8. "In England, among the 36% of teachers and leaders considering leaving the state sector in
   2023, 'teachers' views not being valued by policymakers' was the third most cited factor (83%),
   behind workload (94%) and stress (84%) but well ahead of pay (63%); by 2025 only 15% of
   teachers and leaders agreed that the teaching profession is valued by society." (DfE, Working
   Lives of Teachers and Leaders, wave 2 report pp. 252, 260; wave 4 summary p. 25.)
9. "In the last US federal follow-up of teachers who left the profession, majorities of leavers in
   other jobs said their new position was better than teaching for autonomy (57%), influence over
   workplace policies (59%) and professional prestige (52%) — larger margins than for salary."
   (NCES 2014-077, Table 7, p. 13.)
10. "In a national peer-reviewed survey of Australian teachers who had left the profession, 'lack of
    respect' ranked fifth among seventeen single-choice reasons for leaving (6.9%), ahead of
    salary (4.0%); leadership (17.7%) and workload (16.6%) ranked first and second."
    (Brandenburg et al. 2024, Table 1, p. 2389.)

Caveats to carry into the text: the TALIS 2024 report does not print a 2024 OECD average for the
"valued in society" item (use the range and trend, or pull Table 7.24 when stat.link is reachable);
TALIS 2018 stress averages are OECD average-31 while TALIS 2024 averages cover a slightly
different OECD set, so describe cross-round movement qualitatively; the NCES prestige figure is
from 2012–13 (no newer TFS exists); TALIS measures parental *concerns* as an accountability
stressor but has no item on complaints procedures or legal exposure.
