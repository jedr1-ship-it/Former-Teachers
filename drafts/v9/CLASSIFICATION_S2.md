# Section 2: the paper's own classification of the reasons teachers leave

## Why a new classification
The frameworks in use each answer a question this paper does not ask. Push and pull (Lee)
sorts by direction of force; the personal, school and external grouping (Nguyen, Pham,
Crouch and Springer) sorts by where a variable sits in a regression; job demands and
resources sorts by psychological function; the leaver typologies sort people instead of
reasons. All of them were imported from organisational behaviour written between 1958 and
1986, and three things they leave out matter for re-entry policy: the loss of professional
meaning and standing, whether the departure was chosen or forced by circumstance, and
whether the condition that produced the exit still exists when a return is offered.

## The classification
Reasons are grouped by WHAT THE MOTIVE ATTACHES TO. Five families:

1. THE WORK ITSELF — purpose, autonomy, professional standing, and exposure to conflict or
   legal risk. (Finland's largest stated category is lost commitment; Korea's is the
   infringement of teachers' professional rights.)
2. THE LOAD — hours, intensity, administrative demands and pupil behaviour.
3. THE PEOPLE — school leadership, collegial support and school climate.
4. THE LIVELIHOOD — pay relative to outside options, pension design and its timing
   incentives, and contract security.
5. THE LIFE — family formation and care, health, partner and geography, and career stage.

## The two dimensions that order them, and the figure
Each motive is placed on two axes that a policy reader can act on:
- VERTICAL: strength of evidence linking the motive to OBSERVED exit, not to stated
  intention. Three bands: limited, mixed, robust. This is the axis that disciplines the
  section, because the motives that dominate survey responses are not the ones that best
  predict who actually goes.
- HORIZONTAL: who can act. School leaders and employers; education ministries; authorities
  outside education (finance, pensions, labour law).
FIGURE 1 plots every motive on these axes, coloured by family. Its point is visible at a
glance: the two motives with the strongest evidence on observed exit sit with school
leaders and employers, while the motives that dominate public debate sit either where the
evidence is mixed or where education ministries cannot act alone.

## The cross-cutting distinction
Within each family, a motive can operate as a CHOICE or as a CONSTRAINT. The distinction is
absent from the standard frameworks and decides what re-entry policy can expect: a teacher
who left because no part-time post existed, or because a move followed a partner, remains
available on terms the system controls, while a teacher who left because the work lost its
purpose does not return for a better-designed timetable.
