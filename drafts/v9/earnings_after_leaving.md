# Earnings after leaving teaching: what the evidence establishes

Evidence note settling the challenge to the paper's claim that former teachers "generally earn less afterwards".
Compiled 25 August 2026. Primary sources retrieved and read in full except where noted.

---

## 0. The distinction that decides the dispute

Almost every apparent contradiction in this literature dissolves once four different comparisons are kept apart. The lead author's objection and the paper's claim are not, as stated, about the same object.

| # | Comparison | What it answers | Typical sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Post-exit earnings vs **the leaver's own last teaching salary** | "Did my pay go up after I left?" | Often **positive**, because leavers are disproportionately early-career and low on the scale, and because time passes and real wages grow |
| **B** | Post-exit earnings vs **matched teachers who stayed** | "Would I have done better staying?" | Almost always **negative**, and widening with time |
| **C** | **Conditional on employment** vs **unconditional** (zeros included) | "Is this about wages or about hours and labour-force exit?" | Unconditional is far worse: ~20% of US leavers have no earnings |
| **D** | **FTE-adjusted** vs **actual** earnings | "Is the fall a price or a purchase of hours?" | Actual falls much more than FTE-adjusted where part-time moves are common |

A fifth distinction matters for §4: the **individual-leaver** question (A–D above) is not the **occupational-average** question that OECD Indicator D3 answers.

The lead author's contention has two halves. The **dispersion half is correct and well documented everywhere**. The **mean half is not correct for the United States** on any of the best US evidence, under either comparison A or B. It is correct for England under comparison A only, and reverses under comparison B.

## 1. United States

### 1.1 Brummet, Penner, Pharris-Ciurej and Porter (2024/2025) — the best US evidence

**Data.** School district human-resources records for a single large, diverse urban public school district in the western United States (pseudonymised in the paper as **"Coastline Unified School District", CUSD**), linked at the person level to **Internal Revenue Service tax records** held at the US Census Bureau. Annual earnings and employment for **all educators leaving the district over 12 years, 2003–04 to 2014–15**. Published *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis* 47(2), 503–531; online first 26 February 2024. CC-BY.

> **Access caveat.** The SAGE full text (`journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/01623737241227906`) is behind Cloudflare bot protection and returned HTTP 403 to every retrieval route attempted (direct, PDF, EPUB, text proxy); the Internet Archive is blocked by this session's egress policy; no working-paper, eScholarship, CORE or Census CES version exists. The figures below are therefore taken from the **published abstract** (verified identically via Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and the ERIC API, i.e. three independent copies of the publisher's own text) and from **Education Week's March 2024 interview-based report on the study**. Items sourced only from Education Week are marked **[EdWeek]** and should be verified against the article before publication.

**Destinations.**
- **More than half** of leavers obtain another position **within education** (neighbouring districts, private schools, education-adjacent roles). [EdWeek reports "nearly 60%".]
- **About one fifth** exit the labour force / have no earnings.
- **Fewer than a quarter** take a position outside education.

**Mean earnings — the decisive finding.**
> "The majority of leavers remain in education and **mean earnings are slightly below pre-exit earnings even 8 years later**." (abstract, verbatim)

This is comparison **A** — post-exit earnings against the leaver's own pre-exit teaching pay — and it is **negative**. Against comparison **B**, the gap is larger: **[EdWeek]** after eight years, teachers who left the district earned roughly **$10,000 a year less** than teachers who stayed.

**The distribution — reported explicitly.**
- **Roughly 20% of leavers are unemployed** (no earnings).
- **The bottom quartile of employed leavers earn less than $20,000 a year.**
- **The top quartile of employed leavers earn more than nearly all stayers, sometimes exceeding $100,000.**
- The paper plots **earnings trajectories at the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles**, separately for leavers **employed in education**, leavers **employed outside education**, and **stayers**. Employed leavers' earnings **diverge much more than stayers'**.
- **[EdWeek]** seven years out, both men and women who left education averaged **just under $40,000**; the **top-earning quarter of men** who left education averaged **over $90,000**, the top-earning quarter of women **about $60,000**. About **1 in 5** achieved substantially higher income after eight years; **about 10% of early-career leavers who left education doubled their salaries within four years**; top STEM teachers reached **$100,000+** within a few years.

**By destination.** **[EdWeek]** Emily Penner: *"Staying in education and moving up a little bit was the ticket — not guaranteed for everybody, but very strongly associated with earning more later."* The high-variance, fat-right-tail outcomes are concentrated among the **minority who leave education altogether**; the majority who stay in education have compressed, moderate outcomes.

**Mechanism for the losses.** Post-exit employment and earnings declines are associated with **the presence of children or a high-earning spouse for voluntary female leavers**. Male leavers were substantially more likely to be employed and to reach high-paying non-education roles **[EdWeek]**. This is comparison **C**: a large share of the mean shortfall is labour-force withdrawal, not a wage cut.

**What this study does and does not license.** It is one large urban district, not a national sample; it is administratively exact within that frame. It is the only US study that observes *all* leavers including those who leave the labour force entirely, because IRS records cover them. That is precisely why its mean is below pre-exit pay while studies conditioning on employment show smaller or positive changes.

The published *Education Finance and Policy* 7(1), 8–43 version reports **more than 130,000 teachers, roughly 35,000 of whom left the classroom**. The tables quoted below are from the **CESifo Working Paper No. 2996 (March 2010)** version, which uses **more than 90,000 teachers, roughly 20,000 leavers** — the working paper is openly available and was read in full; the published tables are equivalent in structure but the cell values will differ.

Authors' own reading: *"Florida teachers leaving for full-time employment in other industries are not taking jobs that are, on average, better compensated than their positions as teachers. Taking into account fringe benefits, which are typically more generous for public school teachers … would presumably strengthen this conclusion."*

**Distribution — explicit percentile ratios (Figures 2a/2b unweighted, 3a/3b weighted):**
- Among leavers to **non-teaching district jobs**, the **90/50 earnings ratio stayed at 1.4** across the move.
- Among leavers to **other industries**, the **90/50 ratio rose from 1.3 to 1.8** — a sharp increase in dispersion.
- Unweighted, "a substantial share of both groups of leavers had very low levels of earnings after leaving, suggesting that they moved into part-time employment. Among teachers not working in public school districts, **the distribution peaks at a level below $10,000**."

**By teacher effectiveness.** Among grade 4–8 teachers leaving for **other industries**, a **1 SD increase in value-added to student maths and reading achievement is associated with 6–9% higher earnings outside teaching** (published version: 6–8%). The effectiveness–earnings gradient is **stronger outside teaching than inside it for the same teachers**, implying teacher pay is compressed relative to individual opportunity wages. Note the level result and the gradient result point in opposite directions and must not be conflated: **effective teachers are better paid outside than ineffective ones, while the typical leaver is not better paid outside than they were inside.**

**Selection note (favours the paper's claim).** Leavers to other industries had *lower* average value-added (−0.04 SD) than stayers and district-internal movers (+0.02 to +0.03 SD), and lower teaching earnings before leaving. Because most exits are voluntary, leavers should have *higher* opportunity wages than stayers — so the observed non-gain is a conservative estimate.

### 1.3 Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner (2006) — Georgia

**Data.** Georgia educational-system administrative records merged with **Unemployment Insurance wage records**.

**Finding (abstract, verbatim):** *"we find very strong evidence that this common perception is not correct"* — the perception being that most attrition is driven by the allure of higher-paying alternative occupations. **"Very few of those who leave teaching take jobs that pay more than their salary as teachers."** The majority of exiters from public schools do not move to higher-paying jobs outside teaching; they are more likely to **exit the labour market entirely** or **switch to a lower-paying job in a private school**.

> **Access caveat.** The full text could not be retrieved: the University of Western Ontario repository (`ir.lib.uwo.ca`) has been decommissioned, `economics.uwo.ca/people/stinebrickner_docs/teachers.pdf` now redirects to a 404 loop, SSRN returned 403 and De Gruyter is paywalled. The characterisation above is from the publisher/RePEc abstract plus **Chingos and West's own summary**, which reads: *"Podgursky et al. (2004) merge employment data on Missouri teachers with earnings information from the state's unemployment insurance system, while Scafidi et al. (2006) do the same for Georgia. Both sets of authors conclude that, contrary to common perceptions, **very few exiting teachers take jobs that pay more than their prior salaries as teachers**."* **Cell-level numbers were not obtainable and should not be quoted.**

### 1.4 Podgursky, Monroe and Watson (2004) — Missouri

Missouri teacher records merged with state UI earnings records. Same conclusion as Georgia: exiting teachers largely do **not** move to higher-paying fields outside education; many move to part-time positions or leave the labour force temporarily or permanently in conjunction with family formation. (*Economics of Education Review* 23(5), 507–518.)

### 1.5 Newer state-longitudinal / linked-records work

**Kistler, Dougherty and Woods (2024)**, *Educational Researcher* 53(3), 156–166. State longitudinal data linked to **unemployment-insurance records**, focused on **career and technical education (CTE)** teachers. Finding: teachers in hard-to-staff CTE areas (health science, IT, STEM) are more likely to leave and harder to replace, and **"teachers with the greatest likelihood of leaving are also those who earn the most money in their post-teaching employment."** This is the cleanest US evidence that the **right tail is real and is concentrated in identifiable subject groups** — it is a statement about *who* gains, not about the average leaver.

**Dizon-Ross, Falken, Goldhaber, Lavallee Delgado and Theobald (2026)**, *AERA Open*, "The Front End of the CTE Teacher Pipeline". Washington State education records linked to UI wage records; runs the pipeline in reverse (prior industry earnings of people who *become* CTE teachers). Useful as a cross-check on in/out wage levels for the same skill groups, not as evidence on leavers.

**"Do Former Teachers Earn More Outside the Classroom? New Evidence on the Teacher Pay Penalty" (APPAM Fall Research Conference 2019, Paper 33005).** Statewide **Texas Education Agency** records linked to **Texas Workforce Commission UI records**. Reported result: former teachers **employed full time** receive **+22%** in wages when they stay in the K-12 sector and **+25%** when they leave education; returns to effectiveness, higher degrees and maths/science are greater outside teaching; returns are largest for leavers into Finance and Insurance, Public Administration, and Professional/Scientific/Technical Services.

> **Do not cite this as it stands.** `appam.confex.com` does not resolve from this session (DNS failure), the authors could not be established, and no published version was located. Note also that these are **comparison A, conditional on full-time employment (C and D both)** — which is exactly the specification under which Chingos and West also find small gains for men. It is not a counter-example to Brummet; it is the same phenomenon measured with the zeros and the part-timers removed. **Verify authorship and publication status before any use.**

### 1.6 What the US evidence adds up to

- **Mean/median against own pre-exit pay, all leavers (zeros included): negative.** Brummet et al. (still below at 8 years); Chingos and West (median −23% for movers to other industries).
- **Mean against matched stayers: clearly negative.** ~$10,000/year at 8 years **[EdWeek]**.
- **Median against own pre-exit pay, restricted to full-time employed movers to other industries: roughly flat**, −5% for K–8, +1% for 9–12, **+2% to +4% for men**.
- **The right tail is genuinely fat**, but it belongs to a minority — top-quartile employed leavers, disproportionately male, STEM/CTE, and leaving education altogether.
- **No US study using linked administrative or tax data finds that the average leaver earns more than they did as a teacher, once non-employment is counted.**

## 2. The distribution, specifically

**Studies that report percentiles, variance or shares:**

| Study | Distributional statistic reported | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Brummet et al. (2024) | Percentile trajectories at 10/25/50/75/90 for leavers in education, leavers outside education, and stayers | Bottom quartile of *employed* leavers **< $20,000**; top quartile **> nearly all stayers**, sometimes **> $100,000**; ~**20% unemployed** |
| Brummet et al. **[EdWeek]** | Top-quartile means 7 years out | Men **> $90,000**; women **≈ $60,000**; overall mean **just under $40,000** |
| Chingos and West (2012 / CESifo 2010) | **90/50 earnings ratio** before and after exit | District movers **1.4 → 1.4**; movers to other industries **1.3 → 1.8** |
| Chingos and West | Kernel densities, Figures 2a/2b (unweighted) and 3a/3b (weighted) | Non-district leavers' density **peaks below $10,000**; "distribution of earnings among former teachers is considerably more dispersed than the earnings of the same groups while teaching" |
| Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015), England | **Figure G**: full distribution of leaver-vs-stayer wage differences with 95% CI | "**almost all saw their wages fall**"; **no** significant minority leaving for better-paid positions; a **significant minority with a fall of more than 20%** |
| Worth and McLean (2022), England | Earnings growth split at the **25th percentile of teacher earnings** (Figure C.1) | Below-25th-percentile leavers **+15%** in year 1; above-25th-percentile leavers **+4%** |

**Share of leavers who out-earn their teaching salary.** No study in this set reports this statistic as a single clean number. The closest quantifications are:
- **US:** the **top quartile of employed leavers** out-earns nearly all stayers (Brummet et al.), which — given ~20% non-employment — implies roughly **20% of all leavers**, consistent with **[EdWeek]**'s "about 1 in 5 achieved significantly higher income after 8 years". Scafidi et al. and Podgursky et al. both state that "very few" exiting teachers take higher-paying jobs.
- **England:** Worth, Bamford and Durbin's Figure G shows the distribution of the leaver–stayer difference lying **overwhelmingly below zero** at one year out. Against the leaver's *own* prior salary, however, Worth and McLean find the **average** leaver above it — so the England answer flips with the comparison.

**Other industries vs education-adjacent roles — the distributional split is the key result.**
- **US (Chingos and West):** movers to **district non-teaching roles** had *rising* medians (+6% unweighted, +22% full-time-weighted) and **no increase in dispersion (90/50 flat at 1.4)**. Movers to **other industries** had *falling* medians (−23% unweighted, −5% full-time-weighted) and **sharply rising dispersion (90/50 1.3 → 1.8)**.
- **US (Brummet et al.):** the percentile plots are drawn separately for leavers employed *in* education and *outside* education, precisely because the two distributions differ; the fat right tail lives outside education, and so does the fat left tail.
- **England (Worth, Bamford and Durbin):** leavers to **private schools, non-teaching school roles, and private-sector jobs outside the school sector** had wage differences **not statistically significantly different from stayers**; leavers who became **teaching assistants** or took **public-sector jobs (many in FE/HE)** saw falls of **25–30%**.

The correct summary is therefore not "leavers do badly" but: **the education-adjacent majority experiences a modest, low-variance outcome; the minority that leaves education entirely experiences a high-variance outcome with both tails fattened, and its centre is at or below teaching pay.**

## 3. Other countries

### 3.1 England — the strongest case for the lead author, and it still fails on comparison B

**Worth, J., S. Bamford and B. Durbin (2015), *Should I Stay or Should I Go? NFER Analysis of Teachers Joining and Leaving the Profession*.** Labour Force Survey, five-quarter longitudinal panel, **6,896 teachers including 936 leavers and 774 joiners, 2001–2015**.
- Excluding retirements, **51% of leavers went to destinations in the wider school sector**: private-school teaching **16%**, teaching assistant **15%**, non-teaching school role **19%**. Only **19%** took jobs outside the school sector, **40% of those in further or higher education**.
- **"On average, the wages of teachers that left for another job were ten per cent lower than those that stayed in teaching."** The gap survives controls for initial pay level, management responsibility, phase and age.
- Distribution (Figure G): **"there was no sign of a significant minority of teachers leaving for better paid positions outside teaching in the state sector. In fact, the reverse was true, with a significant minority experiencing a drop in wages of more than 20 per cent."**
- Authors' own caveat: LFS follows individuals for **one year only**, so this is a short-run estimate.

**Worth, J. and D. McLean (2022), *What teachers do next after leaving and the implications for pay-setting*, NFER for the Office of Manpower Economics / STRB.** **Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings panel, 30 years, leavers 1990–2017, tracked up to ten years after exit.** This is England's answer to the American administrative-data studies and it is the single most important source for this dispute.

*Destinations:* **72%** of leavers who took another job remained in the **wider education and childcare sector**. Only **2%** moved to a different professional or managerial career (rising to **3%** ten years out; **4%** among early-career leavers). 

*Comparison A — against their own last year of teaching (real, FTE-adjusted):*

| Years after leaving | Earnings vs last teaching year |
|---|---|
| 1 | **+7%** |
| 3 | **+13%** (annualised 4.3%) |
| 5 | **+16%** (annualised 3.2%) |
| 10 | **+21%** (annualised 3.0%) |

*Where the +7% comes from:* leavers in the **bottom 25% of teacher earnings** grew **+15%** in year one; those **above the 25th percentile** grew **+4%**. The overall +7% is "roughly similar to the **7.3%** pay progression an early-career teacher outside London would get moving from the first to the second spine point of the main pay scale" — i.e. **the average leaver's first-year gain is approximately one ordinary increment.**

*Comparison B — matched leavers vs matched non-leavers:*

| Years after reference year | Leavers | Similar non-leavers | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **+3%** | **+6%** | −3 pp |
| 5 | **+11%** | **+24%** | **−13 pp** |
| 10 | **+15%** | **+37%** | **−22 pp** |

Authors' conclusion: *"even though the earnings of teachers who left teaching for another job continued to grow after they left teaching, they may well have been higher had they stayed."* And: *"a move out of teaching was unlikely to have resulted in higher pay than what could have been earned by staying."*

*Comparison D:* FTE adjustment makes almost no difference in England (Figure C.2) — non-FTE-adjusted earnings are even slightly *higher* from two years out. So the English result is not an artefact of hours.

*Heterogeneity:* **female, primary and experienced** leavers fall further behind similar stayers than **male, secondary and early-career** leavers — the same gradient as the US.

*Methodological honesty required if cited:* leavers earned about **9% less than matched non-leavers in the year before leaving** even after the authors restricted matches to within £10,000, so the leaver–stayer gap is not a clean causal estimate. The authors defend the comparison on the ground that **pre-exit earnings growth trajectories were very similar** between the two groups.

**Bottom line for England:** the lead author's mean claim is **true under comparison A** (+7% at one year, +21% at ten) and **false under comparison B** (leavers +15% vs stayers +37% at ten years). Both come from the same report, and the report itself frames the second as the policy-relevant one.

### 3.2 Denmark — ROCKWOOL Fonden / Arendt

**Analysis by Jacob Nielsen Arendt, research professor, ROCKWOOL Fonden**, on Danish **administrative register data**, tracking teacher cohorts **up to 25 years after completion of education**.

- Teachers who leave a folkeskole job move to jobs paying about **DKK 2,000 less per month, a fall of about 6%**.
- Hours fall by about **30 minutes per week, about 2%**.
- Probability of being on sickness benefit is **12 percentage points lower** four years after the move (18% of all folkeskole teachers vs 6% of movers four years on).
- Antidepressant use falls **1.3 percentage points** four years after the move (6.7% average among teachers → 5.4% among movers).
- Context: in **2023, 33,000 trained teachers were not working in the folkeskole**, of whom **19,000 worked in entirely different sectors**. Retention of new entrants after 20 years has fallen from **68% to 57%**.
- Arendt's own interpretation: *"teachers who leave their profession in the public sector are willing to accept lower wages in exchange for working conditions that suit them better."*

> **Date correction for the paper.** The brief specifies "ROCKWOOL Fonden 2025". The publication located is dated **April 2026** (press release via Ritzau, 7 April 2026; publication page dated March–April 2026). No 2025 ROCKWOOL teacher-exit analysis was found. **Either re-date the citation to 2026 or confirm a separate 2025 item exists.** Note also that the accessible material is the publication page and press release; the underlying analysis report was not retrieved, and **no distributional statistics are published** — only means. If dispersion matters for the Danish case, the analysis would have to be requested from ROCKWOOL Fonden.

This is a **comparison A** result and it is **negative** — Denmark is a clean counter-example to the lead author's claim, on register data, with an explicit conditions-for-pay interpretation by the author.

### 3.3 Australia

**Cuervo, H. and E. Vera-Toscano (2025).** HILDA household panel, **22 waves**, individuals aged 22–75 who taught at some point; **13,855 observations on 987 individuals**, multivariate analysis on **895 teachers / 8,048 observations**; individual fixed-effects models.

- **Weekly wages: −6.2% immediately after leaving** relative to the last year in post (inflation-adjusted); **−9.4% by three years**, with some recovery from about six years out.
- **Hourly wages** show consistent drops across the ten post-exit years observed.
- **Hours** are not significantly different for the first four years; **+9.6% at five years**.
- Job satisfaction rises after exit (**+0.13 SD at one year, +0.19 SD at three**, per the team's earlier extraction), and leisure-time satisfaction is markedly higher.
- Cross-sectional levels (Table 3): teachers earn **AUD 1,784** weekly vs **AUD 2,064** for non-teachers, but work **37.9 vs 33.4** hours; **hourly wages are near-identical, AUD 43.9 vs 45.1**. Teachers report **higher** satisfaction with pay (7.5 vs 7.1) and much **lower** satisfaction with flexibility (6.5 vs 7.4) and leisure (6.2 vs 7.1).
- Destinations: a stable **21.8%→16.7%** go to other parts of the education/health/social-assistance sector; the share **outside education rises with time**, reaching **27.2%** at 11–15 years; **39.8%** of those more than 10 years out have retired.

Australia: **comparison A negative**, no published distributional statistics.

### 3.4 Netherlands

No study of **post-exit earnings** of Dutch teachers was located. The relevant Dutch work is about the **size and composition of the reserve**, not its pay:

- **Somers, M., L. Fleck, W. Groot and F. van Merode (2024), *The Hidden Reserve of Nurses and Teachers in the Netherlands*, ROA Research Memorandum 004E**, and the companion ESB article (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024). CBS register data on everyone qualifying as a teacher 2000–2021: **just over 62,000** qualified people working elsewhere, unemployed or studying; **19.8%** of primary-qualified and **22.7%** of secondary-qualified work in other sectors.
- Survey evidence (Somers et al., 2024a) has inactive Dutch teachers rating several working conditions in their current jobs **more favourably** than active teachers rate theirs, and naming **lower work pressure, more control over hours and more autonomy — ahead of salary** — as what would bring them back.
- CBS notes that teachers, particularly older ones, earn **less per hour** than same-age, same-education workers outside education. That is an Indicator-D3-type statement (§4), **not** a leaver statement.

**Do not attribute a Dutch post-exit earnings finding to any of these.**

### 3.5 Sweden

No study of teachers' earnings after leaving the profession was located. **IFAU Report 2024:11, *Lärares professionella villkor*** (retrieved and searched) is an evaluation of the **karriärstegsreform** and **lärarlönelyft**, i.e. pay reforms for serving teachers, not a leaver-earnings study. IFAU work reports that roughly **70% of teachers remain in the profession** over recent 20-year windows, which is a retention statistic only. **Sweden: no evidence available. Say so rather than inferring.**

### 3.6 Norway

**Statistics Norway (SSB), "Vanligere å forsvinne fra læreryrket enn å komme tilbake dit"**: of teachers employed in schools in 2008, **58.8% were still working in the school system in 2018**. Critically, **SSB states explicitly that it did not follow the salary development of those who quit against those who stayed**; instead it compares the salary *level* of school teachers with that of senior caseworkers in public administration, who earn clearly more than primary teachers and somewhat more than secondary teachers. **That is an occupational-average comparison, not a leaver comparison. Norway supplies no evidence on this question.**

### 3.7 Canada

No Canadian study of post-exit earnings using linked tax or administrative data was located. What exists is **exit-survey** evidence — e.g. the **Alberta Teachers' Association, *Exiting the Teaching Profession* (2024 and 2025)** — and Canadian Teachers' Federation survey work on intentions to leave. **Canada: no earnings evidence. Do not extrapolate from the US.**

## 4. OECD Education at a Glance, Indicator D3 — a different object

**What D3 measures (EAG 2025, Chapter D3, read in full).**
- **Table D3.2:** teachers' and school heads' **average actual salaries** relative to the earnings of **full-time, full-year tertiary-educated workers aged 25–64**, and relative to **similarly educated workers** weighted by teachers' own attainment distribution.
- **Headline:** *"In almost all countries and economies with available information, and at almost all levels of education, teachers' actual salaries are lower than those of tertiary-educated workers."* On average across the OECD, **primary teachers' actual salaries are 83%** of the tertiary-educated benchmark, **lower secondary 87%**, **upper secondary 91%**. Teachers earn more than tertiary-educated workers at all levels only in **Costa Rica, Peru, Portugal and Romania**.
- **Table D3.1 / Figure D3.6 (statutory salaries):** starting salaries average **60%** of similarly educated workers' earnings; top-of-scale salaries reach **97%**. School heads' actual salaries are **more than 50% above teachers'** and typically **above** the tertiary-educated benchmark.
- **WEB Table D3.5** gives relative salaries **by age group and gender**.

**How D3 relates to — and differs from — the leaver question. Four differences, all of which matter.**

1. **Different populations.** D3 compares *the average serving teacher* with *the average tertiary-educated worker*. The leaver question compares *a specific individual before and after a transition*, or against *a matched stayer*. Leavers are not a random draw from teachers, and the jobs they take are not a random draw from tertiary-educated jobs — **72% of English leavers and more than half of American leavers stay inside education**, so the destination distribution is nothing like the D3 denominator.

2. **Different counterfactual.** D3 answers "is teaching underpaid as an occupation?" It says nothing about whether *this* teacher can capture the tertiary-educated average by leaving. Chingos and West's effectiveness gradient is exactly the reason: the outside wage a teacher can command depends on characteristics that teaching pay does not price, so an occupational average cannot be read as an individual's outside option.

3. **Mean against median, explicitly flagged by the OECD itself.** Box D3.3 of EAG 2025 records that a 2024 OECD survey of 24 countries found *"the median is more relevant than the mean as the distribution of salaries can include a few very high salaries (right-skewed distribution of salaries)"*, and that for the nine countries supplying both, **the ratio computed on means differs from the ratio computed on medians by between 0.1% and 14%.** D3 currently divides a **mean** teacher salary by a **median** comparator earnings figure. This is the very mean/median hazard at issue in our dispute, present inside the OECD indicator itself.

4. **Further comparability biases named by the OECD** (Box D3.1 EAG 2021, Box D3.2 EAG 2023, Box D3.3 EAG 2025): inclusion of teachers in the comparator earnings data, restriction to full-time work, differences in data sources between salary and earnings series, **differences in working days between teachers and other tertiary-educated workers**, and differences in pension systems. Teacher pension entitlements are typically more generous than the comparator's and are **excluded from D3 entirely** — the same omission Chingos and West flag for Florida.

**Rule for the paper:** D3 may be used to say that *teaching is paid below the graduate average in most OECD countries*. It may **never** be used to say that *a teacher who leaves will earn the graduate average*, nor as corroboration for or against the lead author's claim. They are different objects and the paper should say so in a sentence where it first uses D3.

## 5. Verdict

### 5.1 Was the original claim wrong?

**"Teachers who leave the profession generally earn less afterwards" is defensible for the United States, Australia and Denmark but is loosely worded and would not survive a determined referee.** Three problems:

1. **"Generally" is doing unmarked work.** Against the leaver's *own* prior salary, the US result is "slightly below at 8 years" and the Florida full-time result is roughly flat — the claim as written implies a larger and more uniform loss than the data support.
2. **It omits the comparison.** The strongest and most defensible version of the claim is the *counterfactual* one (comparison B), where the evidence is unanimous and large. The paper currently asserts the weaker version.
3. **It is false as written for England**, whose own best study finds leavers earning **+7% at one year and +21% at ten** against their last teaching salary — and England is a country the paper relies on elsewhere.

The claim should be **revised, not withdrawn**.

### 5.2 Is the lead author's contention supported?

| Claim | Verdict | Country |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean earnings after leaving are HIGHER** | **Refuted** | **United States** — Brummet et al.: mean still *below* pre-exit pay at 8 years, and ~$10,000/yr below stayers **[EdWeek]**; Chingos and West: median −23% unconditional for movers to other industries; Scafidi et al. and Podgursky et al.: "very few" out-earn their teaching salary |
| | **Refuted** | **Denmark** (−6% monthly pay), **Australia** (−6.2% at 1 year, −9.4% at 3) |
| | **Partially supported** | **England** — true against own prior pay (+7%/+21%), false against matched stayers (+15% vs +37% at ten years) |
| | **Supported only under restrictions** | **US**, if you condition on **full-time** employment **and** exclude the ~20% with no earnings **and** look at **men**: Chingos and West find **+2% to +4%** for male leavers to other industries |
| **The distribution has fat tails; some gain a great deal, others lose** | **Fully supported** | **Everywhere it has been measured.** Brummet et al.: bottom quartile of employed leavers under $20,000, top quartile above nearly all stayers and sometimes above $100,000, ~20% unemployed. Chingos and West: 90/50 ratio rises 1.3 → 1.8 for movers to other industries while staying flat at 1.4 for district movers. Worth and McLean: +15% for bottom-quartile leavers vs +4% for the rest. Kistler et al.: the highest post-exit earners are the most likely leavers |

**In one line: the lead author is right about the variance and wrong about the mean, for the United States. The distributional point is a genuine and important correction to the paper; the mean point is not.**

### 5.3 Exact wording the paper should use

**Short form (one sentence, for use where the claim appears in passing):**

> Followed into their next jobs, most former teachers do not out-earn the colleagues they left behind, and in the United States the average leaver's earnings remain slightly below their own pre-exit teaching pay eight years later, though the spread around that average is wide (Brummet et al., 2024).

**Full form (the passage that should replace the current claim, and which concedes the lead author's valid point):**

> Former teachers do not, in the main, leave for better-paid work, but the average conceals two quite different populations. In the United States, linked district and Internal Revenue Service records show mean earnings still slightly below pre-exit teaching pay eight years after leaving, with about a fifth of leavers not employed at all; the bottom quartile of employed leavers earns under USD 20 000 a year while the top quartile out-earns almost every teacher who stayed, sometimes above USD 100 000 (Brummet et al., 2024). Florida records give the same shape by destination: median earnings rose 6% for those moving to other jobs inside school districts and fell 23% for those leaving for other industries, and the ratio of the ninetieth to the fiftieth percentile among the latter rose from 1.3 to 1.8 as they left (Chingos and West, 2012). Earlier state studies in Georgia and Missouri found that very few exiting teachers took jobs paying more than their teaching salaries (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006; Podgursky, Monroe and Watson, 2004). The English record is the informative exception and confirms the rule: leavers do earn about 7% more one year out and 21% more after ten than in their last year of teaching, yet comparable teachers who stayed gained 37% over the same decade, so the leaver still ends up behind (Worth and McLean, 2022). Danish register data and Australian panel data agree with the American direction, at about 6% below previous pay in both cases (ROCKWOOL Fonden, 2026; Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The safe statement is therefore about the counterfactual rather than the level: leaving teaching has not, on the available evidence, been a route to higher pay for the typical leaver, though it has been for an identifiable minority — disproportionately male, disproportionately in shortage subjects, and disproportionately those who leave education altogether.

**Sentence for the first use of OECD D3, to prevent conflation:**

> Indicator D3 compares the average serving teacher's salary with the average tertiary-educated worker's earnings — across the OECD, primary teachers earn 83% of that benchmark, lower secondary 87% and upper secondary 91% (OECD, 2025). It measures the standing of the occupation, not the outside option of any individual teacher: the jobs leavers actually take are overwhelmingly inside education, and the indicator's own methodological notes warn that it divides a mean teacher salary by a median comparator earnings figure, a difference worth up to 14% in the nine countries able to compute both (OECD, 2025, Box D3.3).

**Words to avoid.** "Generally earn less"; "take a pay cut"; "leave for higher pay". Each collapses one of the four comparisons in §0.
