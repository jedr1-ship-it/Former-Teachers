# Citation verification and upgrade: spillovers and tutoring claims

Sentence under review: "returners and retired teachers can bring benefits of their own, such as experience that spills over to colleagues, mentorship for beginning teachers and tutoring roles with experimental backing (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009; DeCesare et al., 2017; Gersten et al., 2015)".

DeCesare et al. (2017) already verified elsewhere; not treated here. All sources below accessed 29 August 2026. All are primary sources unless marked otherwise.

---

## Claim 1: "experience that spills over to colleagues"

### 1a. Verification of the current citation

**Jackson, C. Kirabo and Elias Bruegmann (2009), "Teaching Students and Teaching Each Other: The Importance of Peer Learning for Teachers", *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, Vol. 1, No. 4, pp. 85-108, https://doi.org/10.1257/app.1.4.85.**

- Verified against the AEA journal page and the full text of the NBER working paper version (No. 15202). Title, journal, volume, issue and pages confirmed. Journal quality: AEJ: Applied Economics is a top-field economics journal (American Economic Association flagship family, Q1, top decile).
- Data: statewide administrative data on all third- to fifth-grade students in North Carolina, 1995 to 2006. Full-text quote: "We use data on all third-grade through fifth-grade students in North Carolina between 1995 to 2006 from the North Carolina Education Research Data Center."
- Abstract (verified verbatim from aeaweb.org): "Using longitudinal elementary school teacher and student data, we document that students have larger test score gains when their teachers experience improvements in the observable characteristics of their colleagues. Using within-school and within-teacher variation, we show that a teacher's students have larger achievement gains in math and reading when she has more effective colleagues (based on estimated value-added from an out-of-sample pre-period). Spillovers are strongest for less experienced teachers and persist over time, and historical peer quality explains away about 20 percent of the own-teacher effect, results that suggest peer learning."

**Does it support EXPERIENCE spillovers specifically?** Partially. The headline result is a peer VALUE-ADDED (effectiveness) spillover, not an experience spillover. However, peer experience is the one observable characteristic that does carry the observables result. Full-text quotes:

> "for both math and reading, the coefficients on all the peer experience categories are positive and statistically significant."

> "One cannot reject, however, the joint hypothesis that coefficients for teacher peer characteristics, other than peer experience, are equal to zero at traditional levels for either math or reading."

But experience is dominated by value-added when both enter (footnote in full text): "In models that include both teacher peer experience and teacher peer value-added, one cannot reject the null hypothesis of no teacher peer experience effects (conditional on peer value-added)". Also directly relevant to returners: "Spillovers are strongest for less experienced teachers", i.e. experienced, effective colleagues benefit novice colleagues most, which is exactly the mechanism the sentence invokes.

**Verdict:** keep Jackson and Bruegmann (2009). It supports the claim as reworded below (effectiveness and experience spilling over, with the largest gains for less experienced colleagues); "experience that spills over" alone slightly overstates what the paper isolates.

### 1b. Strongest complement (recommended addition)

**Papay, John P., Eric S. Taylor, John H. Tyler and Mary E. Laski (2020), "Learning Job Skills from Colleagues at Work: Evidence from a Field Experiment Using Teacher Performance Data", *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, Vol. 12, No. 1, pp. 359-388, https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20170709.**

- Verified against the AEA journal page. Journal quality: AEJ: Economic Policy, top-field economics journal, Q1.
- This is the experimental complement to Jackson and Bruegmann: a randomised field experiment in Tennessee schools pairing low-performing with high-performing teachers on specific evaluated skills.
- Abstract (verified verbatim from aeaweb.org): "We study a program designed to encourage learning from coworkers among school teachers. In an experiment, we document gains in job performance when high- and low-skilled teachers are paired and asked to work together on improving their skills. Pairs are matched on specific skills measured in prior evaluations. Each pair includes a target teacher who scores low in one or more of 19 skills and a partner who scores high in (many of) the target's deficient skills. Student achievement improved 0.12 standard deviations in low-skilled teachers' classrooms. Improvements are likely the result of target teachers learning skills from their partner."

### 1c. Considered and not recommended as headline citation

**Sun, Min, Susanna Loeb and Jason A. Grissom (2017), "Building Teacher Teams: Evidence of Positive Spillovers from More Effective Colleagues", *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, Vol. 39, No. 1, pp. 104-125, https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373716665698.** Verified via Crossref and the Stanford CEPA abstract page (abstract-only verification; full text not consulted). Q1 journal; corroborates Jackson and Bruegmann with Miami-Dade transfer data but is observational and adds little beyond the two recommended citations. Optional third citation only.

### Recommendation for Claim 1

Cite **Jackson and Bruegmann (2009) and Papay et al. (2020)** together: the first for quasi-experimental evidence that effective and experienced colleagues raise peers' student outcomes (especially for novices), the second for direct experimental evidence that skills transfer between colleagues.

## Claim 2: "tutoring roles with experimental backing"

### 2a. Verification of the current citation

**Gersten, Russell, Eric Rolfhus, Ben Clarke, Lauren E. Decker, Chuck Wilkins and Joseph Dimino (2015), "Intervention for First Graders With Limited Number Knowledge: Large-Scale Replication of a Randomized Controlled Trial", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 52, No. 3, pp. 516-546, https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831214565787.**

- Citation verified via Crossref (title, journal, volume 52, issue 3, pages 516-546, June 2015) and the SAGE journal page. Journal quality: AERJ is the flagship AERA journal, Q1.
- Design: large-scale replication RCT of the Number Rockets Tier 2 mathematics tutoring intervention; 76 schools in four urban districts, 994 at-risk first graders; confirmatory effect size 0.34 SD on the TEMA-3 (p < .001), robust across six sensitivity analyses.
- **Who delivered the tutoring (the critical point).** Verified from the full text of the study's own final report (Gersten et al. 2012, *An Evaluation of Number Rockets: A Tier-2 Intervention for Grade 1 Students at Risk for Difficulties in Mathematics*, NCEE 2012-4007, Institute of Education Sciences, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED529429; this is the report of the same trial published as the 2015 AERJ article). Exact quotes:

> "Candidates were required to be certified teachers and/or have experience in the elementary (K-5) grades. Typical applicants were retired teachers or teachers active in the substitute teacher pool in the local district."

> "The percentage of tutors who reported they were retired teachers and/or worked as a substitute teacher was 77.3."

> "Locally recruited from retired teachers and substitute teacher pool" (tutor description table).

- **Caveat to flag:** tutors were hired as study staff through a temporary services company, not employed by the schools as returning staff: "Tutors were recruited and hired through a nationwide temporary services company... All tutoring activities were carried out by study staff; no school personnel were paid as part of this study." So the trial shows that retired and substitute teachers CAN deliver effective tutoring at scale; it is not a trial of a school re-employment scheme. The sentence's link is legitimate but should say "largely retired and substitute teachers" rather than imply school-employed returners.

**Verdict:** keep Gersten et al. (2015). It is the strongest available top-tier RCT in which the tutors were predominantly retired and substitute teachers.

### 2b. The original trial (context, not for citation)

**Fuchs, Lynn S., Donald L. Compton, Douglas Fuchs, Kim Paulsen, Joan D. Bryant and Carol L. Hamlett (2005), "The Prevention, Identification, and Cognitive Determinants of Math Difficulty", *Journal of Educational Psychology*, Vol. 97, No. 3, pp. 493-513, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.3.493.** Citation verified via Crossref (abstract-only verification of content). In the original single-district efficacy trial the tutors were the authors' graduate research assistants, not retired teachers. Evidence for ESSA states: "the tutors were the authors' graduate students, so that study did not meet inclusion standards"; Nickow et al. (working paper, full text): "When implemented at smaller scales as part of exploratory research studies, tutors were mostly graduate student research assistants (Fuchs et al., 2005; 2013), whereas school staff members were employed as tutors in a large-scale impact evaluation of the program (Gersten et al., 2015)." Do not cite Fuchs et al. for the returner claim; Gersten et al. (2015) is the right trial precisely because of who tutored.

### 2c. Strongest general anchor (recommended addition)

**Nickow, Andre, Philip Oreopoulos and Vincent Quan (2024), "The Promise of Tutoring for PreK-12 Learning: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 61, No. 1, pp. 74-107, https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312231208687.**

- Note: the published version is in *American Educational Research Journal* (Q1, AERA flagship), NOT *Review of Educational Research* as previously believed. Citation verified via ERIC record EJ1406037. Working paper version: NBER Working Paper No. 27476 (2020), a flagship working paper series, https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476.
- Published pooled effect (verified from ERIC abstract, abstract-only for the published version): "tutoring programs yield consistently substantial positive impacts on learning, with an overall pooled ES of 0.288 SD (SE = 0.029, p < 0.001)", with effects "particularly robust for programs employing teachers or paraprofessionals, delivered in earlier grade levels, scheduled at least three times weekly, and conducted during the school day".
- Tutor-type detail (verified from the full text of the NBER working paper, whose pooled estimate is 0.37 SD across 96 RCTs): Table 3A pooled effects by tutor type: teacher tutoring 0.50 SD (18 studies), paraprofessional 0.40 SD (46 studies), nonprofessional (volunteer) 0.21 SD (24 studies), parent 0.23 SD (11 studies). Full-text quote: "teacher tutoring programs yield the largest impacts, followed by paraprofessional tutoring programs, with nonprofessional and parent tutoring accounting for the lower end of the impact distribution." Cite the published AERJ version; the specific 0.50/0.40/0.21/0.23 breakdown is from the working paper version and should be attributed as such if used numerically.

### 2d. Other candidates checked and rejected

- UK National Tutoring Programme evaluations (NFER/Kantar for the Education Endowment Foundation): non-experimental or compromised comparison designs with well-documented implementation problems in the early years; not top-tier RCT evidence and not specific to retired or returning teachers. Not recommended.
- No German RCT matching the description "Chancen:gleich" could be identified in top-tier outlets; prominent European tutoring RCTs (e.g. Carlana and La Ferrara's Italian online tutoring trial, NBER WP 28834) used university student volunteers, not retired teachers. Not relevant to the returner link.
- Lee et al. (2011) Experience Corps used "older adults" as volunteer reading tutors, but these were community volunteers, not retired teachers specifically, and volunteer tutoring is the weakest category in Nickow et al. Not recommended.
- **Conclusion:** Gersten et al. (2015) remains the only top-tier RCT in which the delivery workforce was predominantly retired and substitute teachers. The "retired teachers delivered it" link is genuine but rests on this single trial, backed by the meta-analytic finding that teacher-delivered tutoring is the most effective category.

### Recommendation for Claim 2

Cite **Gersten et al. (2015) and Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2024)** together: the first as the direct large-scale RCT with retired/substitute teacher tutors (ES 0.34 SD), the second as the meta-analytic anchor showing teacher-delivered tutoring is the most effective tutor category.

## Recommended final wording of the sentence

> "returners and retired teachers can bring benefits of their own, such as skills and experience that spill over to colleagues, particularly less experienced ones (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009; Papay et al., 2020), mentorship for beginning teachers (DeCesare et al., 2017) and tutoring roles with experimental backing, retired and substitute teachers having delivered an effective large-scale numeracy tutoring programme in one major trial (Gersten et al., 2015; Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024)."

Minimal alternative if the sentence must stay short:

> "...such as skills and experience that spill over to colleagues (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009; Papay et al., 2020), mentorship for beginning teachers (DeCesare et al., 2017) and tutoring roles with experimental backing (Gersten et al., 2015; Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024)."

Key wording fixes: (1) "skills and experience that spill over" rather than "experience that spills over", because the strongest evidence is on effectiveness and skill spillovers, with experience the supported observable proxy; (2) if space allows, signal that the retired-teacher tutoring evidence comes from one large trial in which tutors were mostly retired and substitute teachers hired for the study, not re-employed school staff.
