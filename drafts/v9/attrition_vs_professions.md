# Teacher attrition compared with other professions, and whether some attrition is healthy

Research note supporting one paragraph of the policy paper. All figures below are traced to primary sources unless marked otherwise. Verification status is stated for every source. Access date for all URLs: 27 August 2026.

## 1. Headline verdict

Across every country where teacher exit rates have been compared with comparable occupations using representative or administrative data, teacher attrition is similar to, or lower than, attrition in comparable professions. The United States (CPS): teachers leave at about the same rate as nurses, close to accountants, well below social workers. England (Understanding Society): teachers leave at a somewhat higher rate than nurses (not statistically significant) and significantly higher than police officers. Australia (linked tax records): teachers have the lowest attrition of all 97 occupation minor groups, below nurses, police and doctors. The popular claims of exceptional attrition (40 to 50 per cent of new US teachers gone in five years; 30 to 50 per cent in Australia) are contradicted by official longitudinal data (17 per cent in five years in the US; roughly 2 to 6 per cent per year, and falling, for early-career teachers in Australia). The cross-national OECD average (including retirements) is 6.5 per cent a year. Separately, a strand of causal evidence shows that a non-zero exit rate is desirable when it is concentrated among low performers: in Washington DC, exits of low-rated teachers raised student achievement. The composition of attrition matters more than its level; the counterpoint is that turnover as such carries disruption costs (Ronfeldt et al.).

## 2. United States

### 2.1 The "Aldeman and Yi" piece: located and verified

The piece exists. It is:

- Chad Aldeman and Samuel Yi (2025), "Are Teachers Abandoning Teaching? Data show teacher turnover remains low", Education Next, published online 18 December 2025. https://www.educationnext.org/are-teachers-abandoning-teaching-data-show-teacher-turnover-remains-low/ [VERIFIED from source: full article fetched.]
- A companion summary appeared on Aldeman's Substack: Aldeman and Yi, "Teacher retention over the long, long term", 19 December 2025. https://www.chadaldeman.com/p/teacher-retention-over-the-long-long [VERIFIED from source.]

Venue: Education Next (Hoover Institution / Harvard PEPG journal; research-based commentary, not peer reviewed, but the underlying data are public CPS microdata). Data: Current Population Survey (US Census Bureau/BLS), replicating and extending Harris and Adams (2007), plus NCES data.

Verified findings and figures:
- Pooling 2015 to 2024 CPS data, "we estimate that 7.6 percent of teachers leave the profession in a given year".
- COVID spike and recovery: occupational exits rose "from 6.5 percent in 2019 to 9.8 percent in 2020" then fell back to 7.7 per cent by 2023 to 2024.
- Comparison: "teachers continue to leave their chosen profession at lower rates than social workers leave theirs. Teacher turnover also looks pretty similar to the rate at which nurses leave nursing and is close to the rate at which accountants leave accounting."
- Long run: "the rate at which teachers leave the profession has been remarkably stable through the decades" (series reconstructed back to the 1980s).
- Policy conclusion (from the Substack summary): treating turnover as a generic problem risks blunt policy; an approach that "prioritizes teacher quality over sheer quantity" serves educators and students better.

Note for the lead author: earlier Aldeman solo pieces make the same argument with BLS JOLTS data, e.g. "There Is No 'Big Quit' in K-12 Education" (The 74, February 2022), https://www.the74million.org/article/aldeman-there-is-no-big-quit-in-k-12-education-but-schools-have-specific-labor-challenges-that-need-targeted-solutions/ [located, not independently verified in full]. Cite the Education Next article as "Aldeman and Yi (2025)".

### 2.2 Harris and Adams (2007)

Douglas N. Harris and Scott J. Adams (2007), "Understanding the level and causes of teacher turnover: A comparison with other professions", Economics of Education Review, 26(3), 325-337. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2005.09.007 ; ERIC record: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ762749 [ABSTRACT-ONLY verification: the article is paywalled; findings verified from the ERIC/publisher abstract and from the replication description in Aldeman and Yi (2025). Exact table-level rates could not be retrieved.]

Verified (abstract level) findings:
- Data: matched Current Population Survey files; comparators chosen as similarly educated, disproportionately female, partly public-sector professions: nurses, social workers, accountants.
- "The average rate of teacher turnover is not significantly higher than these professions, even after controlling for other measured differences." Ordering: lower than social workers, about the same as accountants, slightly higher than nurses.
- Distinctive feature of teaching: turnover is more concentrated among older workers, reflecting earlier retirement; the high pension-to-salary ratio in teaching partially explains this. Working-age (non-retirement) exit is therefore even less exceptional than raw rates suggest.

### 2.3 BLS JOLTS quits comparison (own calculation from official API)

Bureau of Labor Statistics, Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, series JTS923000000000000QUR (quits rate, state and local government education) and JTS100000000000000QUR (quits rate, total private). https://www.bls.gov/jlt/ (data drawn via the BLS public API). [VERIFIED from source: retrieved directly from the BLS API on 27 August 2026.]

- 2024 average monthly quits rate: state and local government education 0.9 per cent; total private 2.3 per cent.
- 2025 average monthly quits rate: state and local government education 0.8 per cent; total private 2.2 per cent.
- Caveats for the paragraph: the JOLTS education series covers all public education employees (not only teachers), and quits into other schools count as quits; even so, monthly voluntary quit rates in public education run at roughly one third of the private-sector rate, consistent with education being a comparatively low-quit sector.

### 2.4 US early-career rate (debunking "50 per cent in five years")

Lucinda Gray and Soheyla Taie (2015), Public School Teacher Attrition and Mobility in the First Five Years: Results From the First Through Fifth Waves of the 2007-08 Beginning Teacher Longitudinal Study, NCES 2015-337, US Department of Education, April 2015. https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2015/2015337.pdf [VERIFIED from source: PDF retrieved; figures quoted from report text.]

- "Among all beginning teachers in 2007-08, 10 percent did not teach in 2008-09, 12 percent did not teach in 2009-10, 15 percent did not teach in 2010-11, and 17 percent did not teach in 2011-12." So cumulative five-year attrition was 17 per cent, not 40 to 50 per cent; some non-teachers later returned (3 per cent were returners in year five).

## 3. England / United Kingdom

Jude Hillary, Joana Andrade and Jack Worth (2018), Teacher Retention and Turnover Research. Research Update 4: How Do Teachers Compare To Nurses And Police Officers?, Slough: NFER (Nuffield Foundation funded), March 2018, ISBN 978-1-911039-74-7. https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/tntdmbr1/teacher_retention_and_turnover_research_research_update_4.pdf [VERIFIED from source: full PDF retrieved; figures quoted from report.]

- Data: Understanding Society (USoc) longitudinal survey, waves 2009 to 2017; 1,197 English state-sector teachers, 752 public-sector nurses, 198 police officers, full-time staff.
- Proportion of full-time staff leaving their profession per year: teachers 12.3 per cent, nurses 9.9 per cent, police officers 7.7 per cent. "There is a statistically significant difference between the leaving rates for teachers and police officers, but not for teachers and nurses."
- The report cautions that the USoc leaving rate for teachers is higher than in the administrative School Workforce Census (the official DfE leaving rate is around 10 per cent a year, including retirements), and notes teachers work the longest hours (50 per week in term time; about 45 annualised) with leavers motivated by workload and flexibility.
- The report also states explicitly that "there has been relatively little research conducted which compares retention across different public sector professions", which supports the scarcity point in Section 5.
- No NFER publication titled "Is teaching a leaky profession?" could be located; the profession-comparison evidence is in Research Update 4 and the project's final report (Worth and Van den Brande, Teacher Workforce Dynamics in England, NFER, 2018). Recommend citing Research Update 4.

England cross-check from OECD: England's attrition (about 10 per cent, including retirement) sits at the high end of the OECD distribution in Section 5.1.

## 4. Australia

### 4.1 The 30 to 50 per cent claim and its debunking

Paul Weldon (2018), "Early career teacher attrition in Australia: evidence, definition, classification and measurement", Australian Journal of Education, 62(1), 61-78. https://doi.org/10.1177/0004944117752478 ; ERIC: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1281975 [ABSTRACT-ONLY verification: publisher page blocked; abstract verified via ERIC.]

- Scholarly articles and Australian media claim that "30-50% of Australian teachers leave teaching within their first five years", but "there is no robust Australian evidence, and figures do not agree"; what evidence exists suggests attrition is dynamic, varies by school level and location, and "is not always negative".

### 4.2 Administrative-data measurement and cross-occupation comparison

Jack Buckley and Silvia Griselda (2025), Who Stays, Who Goes? A New Look at Teacher Attrition Using Administrative Data, e61 Institute Research Note, 4 June 2025. https://e61.in/who-stays-who-goes-a-new-look-at-teacher-attrition-using-administrative-data/ (PDF: https://e61.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/teacher_attrition_final-20250604.pdf) [VERIFIED from source: full PDF retrieved via archived copy; e61 is an independent, non-partisan economic research institute.]

- Data: linked Australian administrative tax records; a teacher counts as leaving only if not employed as a school teacher in any of the following three years (so temporary breaks are not attrition).
- "Between 5% and 6% of teachers leave the profession each year"; among working-age teachers (excluding retirement effects) attrition fell from 5.6 per cent in 2009 to 4.5 per cent in 2019; early-career attrition (first five years post university) "almost halved from 4% in 2009 to 2.3% in 2019".
- "School teachers have the lowest attrition rate among all 97 occupation minor groups", holding across sex, age groups and time periods, and "slightly lower rates of attrition than workers in occupations with similar characteristics, such as nurses, fire fighters and police, and medical professionals".
- "Not all attrition is harmful": over one third of occupation-changers move to education-related roles (teachers' aides, early childhood, education advisers); the note recommends policymakers "recognise that not all attrition is a policy failure - some exits reflect healthy labour market mobility".
- Prior Australian estimates had ranged from roughly 1 to 10 per cent a year (citing Weldon 2018), underlining the measurement problem.

### 4.3 Official AITSL data

AITSL, National Trends: Teacher Workforce, Australian Teacher Workforce Data, June 2025. https://www.aitsl.edu.au/research/australian-teacher-workforce-data/atwd-reports/national-trends-teacher-workforce [VERIFIED from source for the quotes below; the ATWD registration-based attrition tables themselves could not be retrieved this session.]

- Verified caveat on intentions versus behaviour: "reported intentions to leave the profession may not translate into actual attrition", with international evidence that stated intention-to-leave rates of 30 to 40 per cent "may result in actual turnover closer to 10-15% over 5 years".
- [SECONDARY, use with caution] Media and derivative summaries of ATWD registration data report about 95 per cent of graduates registered one year after graduating and about 91 per cent of the 2018 graduate cohort still registered five years on; verify against the ATWD Key Metrics Dashboard before quoting.

## 5. Other countries

### 5.1 OECD cross-country attrition (new indicator, no cross-profession benchmark)

OECD (2025), Education at a Glance 2025: OECD Indicators, Indicator D8 ("How severe are teacher shortages across countries?"), OECD Publishing, Paris. https://doi.org/10.1787/1c0d9c79-en (chapter page: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-at-a-glance-2025_1c0d9c79-en/full-report/how-severe-are-teacher-shortages-across-countries_781f4a97.html) [VERIFIED from source: full report PDF retrieved; quotes from Indicator D8.]

- "Across 19 OECD countries and economies with available data, on average 6.5% of fully qualified teachers from pre-primary to upper secondary education left the profession in 2022/23" (definition includes both resignations and retirements). Range: below 3 per cent in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel; above 10 per cent in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania. Pre-primary 7.3 per cent; primary 5.8 per cent; secondary 5.9 per cent.
- "Over the past decade, rates have remained relatively stable in most countries and economies" (exceptions: up more than 2 percentage points in Austria and Estonia; down by a similar margin in Denmark). On average 51 per cent of leavers resigned (the rest retired).
- Reportable absence: the OECD does not benchmark teacher attrition against other professions; no official international comparison of teacher versus other-profession exit rates exists.

### 5.2 Norway (register data)

Mari Lande With (2017), "Are Teachers Increasingly Leaving the Profession?", Professions and Professionalism, 7(2), e1723. https://doi.org/10.7577/pp.1723 [VERIFIED from source: full open-access PDF retrieved.]

- Norwegian administrative registers across three decades (about 97,000 teachers): "early career attrition has declined over time, whereas the incidence of early retirement increased". Contradicts the intensification narrative of rising exits; no formal cross-profession benchmark, but confirms the retirement-timing pattern found by Harris and Adams for the US.

### 5.3 Sweden

Per Lindqvist, Ulla Karin Nordanger and Rickard Carlsson (2014), "Teacher attrition the first five years - A multifaceted image", Teaching and Teacher Education, 40, 94-103. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2014.02.005 [ABSTRACT-ONLY verification: publisher blocked; metadata verified via Semantic Scholar.] A 23-year Swedish cohort study; finds attrition is lower and less permanent than commonly assumed, with many leavers returning, and argues crude leaving percentages overstate the loss (see also Carlsson, Lindqvist and Nordanger, 2019, European Journal of Teacher Education, https://doi.org/10.1080/02619768.2019.1566315, open access, same conclusion on measurement).

### 5.4 Netherlands, Germany, Canada: reportable absence

The Dutch education ministry's official Trendrapportage Arbeidsmarkt Leraren (e.g. 2023 edition, https://www.ocwincijfers.nl/themas/arbeidsmarkt-en-leraren/trendrapportage-arbeidsmarkt/overzicht) tracks sector outflow (about 9.5 per cent a year in secondary education, roughly constant for years, with 22 per cent annual outflow among the over-60s, i.e. largely retirement) but publishes no comparison with other professions. [Figures from the official report series located via search; PDF not independently parsed: treat as SECONDARY until checked.] No official or peer-reviewed cross-profession comparison of teacher exit rates was found for Germany or Canada. The absence of such comparisons outside the US, England and Australia is itself a finding: most national debates assert exceptional teacher attrition without an occupational benchmark.

## 6. Is some attrition healthy?

### 6.1 Evidence that selective attrition raises quality

- Melinda Adnot, Thomas Dee, Veronica Katz and James Wyckoff (2017), "Teacher Turnover, Teacher Quality, and Student Achievement in DCPS", Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 39(1), 54-76. https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373716663646 ; NBER WP 21922: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21922 [VERIFIED from NBER abstract.] Under the IMPACT evaluation system in Washington DC, "on average, DCPS replaced teachers who left with teachers who increased student achievement by 0.08 SD in math"; where low-performing teachers were induced to leave, "student achievement improves by larger and statistically significant amounts (i.e., 0.14 SD in reading and 0.21 SD in math)". Turnover of low performers, in a high-attrition urban district, improved outcomes.
- Eric A. Hanushek (2011), "The economic value of higher teacher quality", Economics of Education Review, 30(3), 466-479. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2010.12.006 (author PDF: https://hanushek.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Hanushek%202011%20EER%2030(3).pdf) [VERIFIED from source: full PDF retrieved.] "Replacing the bottom 5-8 percent of teachers with average teachers could move the U.S. near the top of international math and science rankings with a present value of $100 trillion"; deselecting the least effective 5 to 8 per cent would raise achievement by about 0.4 standard deviations (to Canada's level). See also Hanushek (2009), "Teacher deselection", in Goldhaber and Hannaway (eds), Creating a New Teaching Profession, Urban Institute Press.
- Jason A. Grissom and Brendan Bartanen (2019), "Strategic Retention: Principal Effectiveness and Teacher Turnover in Multiple-Measure Teacher Evaluation Systems", American Educational Research Journal, 56(2), 514-555. https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831218797931 ; ERIC: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1209526 [ABSTRACT-ONLY verification.] In Tennessee, more effective principals have lower turnover overall but higher turnover among the lowest-performing teachers: deliberate "strategic retention", i.e. functional turnover in practice.
- The functional versus dysfunctional turnover distinction originates in the management literature (Dalton, Todor and Krackhardt, 1982, "Turnover overstated: The functional taxonomy", Academy of Management Review, 7(1), 117-123. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1982.4285499) [concept citation; not verified this session]. The e61 note (Section 4.2, verified) applies the same logic: over a third of Australian leavers stay in education-related work, and some exits "reflect healthy labour market mobility".

### 6.2 The counterpoint: turnover has real costs

- Matthew Ronfeldt, Susanna Loeb and James Wyckoff (2013), "How Teacher Turnover Harms Student Achievement", American Educational Research Journal, 50(1), 4-36. https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831212463813 ; NBER WP 17176: https://www.nber.org/papers/w17176 [VERIFIED from ERIC/NBER abstract.] Using 850,000 NYC pupil observations over eight years: pupils in grade levels with higher turnover score lower in maths and English; moving from no turnover to 100 per cent turnover implies losses of about 8 to 10 per cent of a standard deviation in maths and 5 to 6 per cent in English, largest in schools with many low-performing and Black pupils; there is a disruptive effect "beyond changing the distribution in teacher quality".
- Replacement costs: widely cited estimates (about USD 20,000 or more per urban leaver) come from advocacy-adjacent sources (Learning Policy Institute; NCTAF). Under the paper's sourcing rules do not rest the cost point on these alone; if a cost figure is needed use the peer-reviewed estimate in Watlington, Shockley, Guglielmino and Felsher (2010), "The high cost of leaving: An analysis of the cost of teacher turnover", Journal of Education Finance, 36(1), 22-37 [not verified this session], or drop the monetary figure and rely on Ronfeldt et al. for the harm.

## 7. Verdict and suggested framing for the paragraph

1. Level: on the best available evidence, teacher attrition is not distinctively high. US: about 7.6 per cent a year, about the same as nurses, near accountants, below social workers (Aldeman and Yi 2025; Harris and Adams 2007); public education quit rates run at roughly a third of private-sector rates (BLS JOLTS). Australia: 5 to 6 per cent a year and the lowest of 97 occupation groups (e61 2025). England is the partial exception: 12.3 per cent versus 9.9 (nurses) and 7.7 (police), though only the police gap is statistically significant (NFER 2018), and England sits near the top of the OECD range (OECD average 6.5 per cent including retirements).
2. By country and career stage: the answer varies more across countries than the rhetoric admits (below 3 per cent to above 10 per cent across the OECD), and the early-career horror figures are myths: 17 per cent in five years in the US (NCES), early-career attrition of 2 to 3 per cent a year and falling in Australia (e61; Weldon), declining early-career attrition in Norway (With 2017). A distinctive genuine feature of teaching is earlier retirement, not mid-career flight (Harris and Adams; With).
3. Healthy rate: zero attrition is neither attainable nor desirable. Exits of low performers raise achievement (Adnot et al. 2017: +0.14 SD reading, +0.21 SD maths where low performers left; Hanushek 2011; Grissom and Bartanen 2019), and a share of exits is ordinary, welfare-enhancing labour-market mobility (e61 2025). But turnover is not free (Ronfeldt et al. 2013), so the policy object is not minimising the headline rate; it is shaping the composition of who leaves and protecting the specific settings (disadvantaged schools, remote areas, shortage subjects) where attrition is genuinely concentrated and harmful.
