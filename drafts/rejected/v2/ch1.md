# 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Why former teachers merit their own analysis

Every education system employs fewer teachers than it has trained. The difference is not a statistical residual but a population of people, and a distinctive one. It includes the head of mathematics who retired last summer, the primary teacher who stepped out of paid work to raise children, and the science graduate who taught for six years before moving into industry. This paper is about that population, what it carries, and what governments have actually done to bring parts of it back into schools.

Former teachers are worth studying in their own right because of what they bring to the profession. Alone among the recruitment pools open to education systems, they arrive with classroom craft already formed, with subject and curriculum knowledge acquired in service, and often with the accumulated judgement of a long career. The overlap with veteran teachers is substantial. Retirees are veterans by definition, and even among teachers who resign rather than retire, two out of three had more than five years of experience across the seven OECD countries with seniority data (OECD, 2025a). That experience matches a documented scarcity inside schools. On average across OECD countries, only 22% of novice teachers have an assigned mentor (OECD, 2019), and some systems now explicitly consider bringing former teachers back into mentoring rather than classroom roles (OECD, 2024).

None of this is a new discovery. *Teachers Matter* identified former teachers as "a potentially important source of recruits" two decades ago, noting that returners made up about 25% of the flow of full-time teachers into England's maintained sector in 2000/01, and more than half of the part-time inflow, and recommending that systems maintain contact with former teachers and offer targeted retraining (OECD, 2005). The *Education Policy Outlook 2024* returns to the same ground, listing support for re-entry among four levers for expanding teacher supply and observing that returners bring diverse career experiences that can enhance teaching quality and drive innovation (OECD, 2024). What the flagship literature has not yet provided is a systematic account of the programmes and rules through which countries act on this advice. That is the gap this paper addresses.

## 1.2. Shortages, and the honest size of the re-entry margin

The policy context is a broad tightening of teacher supply. On average across OECD countries, the share of students in schools whose principals reported that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff rose from 26% in 2018 to 47% in 2022, an increase of 21 percentage points (OECD, 2023b; OECD, 2025a). Globally, UNESCO estimates that 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers will be needed by 2030 (UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024). The outflow feeding these gaps is steady rather than dramatic. Across 19 OECD countries and economies with data, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23 (OECD, 2025a), and 17% of teachers who are not near retirement intend to leave within five years (OECD, 2025b). Chapter 2 examines these figures, and their national variation, in detail.

Re-entry is one part of the response to that tightening, and a modest part. That deserves saying plainly at the outset, because reserve arithmetic can suggest otherwise. Dutch register data identify over 62 000 qualified people outside education, against a combined shortage of 13 600 full-time equivalents, and the researchers note that if 22% of this reserve returned full time the shortage would disappear (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b). The calculation is useful context, not a promise. No system has come close to activation on that scale. In the United States, only around a third of teachers who leave ever return (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), and the share appears to have fallen to roughly one fifth for the 2000-2019 cohort (Moyer, 2022). The Dutch education agency itself, publishing its own reserve estimates, warns that the reserve "does not, however, solve the teacher shortage" (DUO, 2026).

Return is nonetheless a routine supply channel, one that most systems already depend on without managing it deliberately. Re-entrants have accounted for 37-49% of new hires in recent United States data (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), and in England returners increased in 2024/25 while every other category of entrant fell (Department for Education, 2025a). The case for policy attention rests on this combination. The channel is real, it flows anyway, and what governments do shapes who comes back and on what terms, even though it will never close shortages on its own.

## 1.3. Who former teachers are

The population behind the aggregate numbers falls into three broad groups, which recur throughout this paper because programmes and rules tend to address them separately (Table 1.1).

The first group is retirees. They are numerous, visible in pension registers, and the default target where no other data exist. Germany counted some 457 000 retired teachers in 2021, of whom just over 5 000 were teaching (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023); in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel, retirements account for more than half of all departures from the profession (OECD, 2025a). The second group comprises qualified people currently outside the labour market, much of it reflecting care work at home. In the classic United States cohort evidence, about two-thirds of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely rather than switching employer, with the arrival of a child the single strongest exit determinant (Stinebrickner, 2002); in recent linked administrative data, about a fifth of leavers exit the labour force (Brummet et al., 2024). The third group is job switchers working in other sectors. They attract the most political attention but are the smallest destination in the best United States evidence, where fewer than a quarter of leavers take jobs outside education (Brummet et al., 2024); in the Netherlands, roughly a fifth of primary-qualified and a quarter of secondary-qualified people work in other sectors (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b).

**Table 1.1. Three populations of former teachers**

| Group | Typical route out of the classroom | Indicative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Retirees | Statutory or early retirement at the end of a full career | 457 000 retired teachers in Germany (2021); retirements exceed half of departures in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023; OECD, 2025a) |
| Qualified people outside the labour market | Exit from paid work, frequently linked to childbirth and care responsibilities | About two-thirds of exiting female teachers in the NLS-72 cohort left the labour force entirely; about a fifth of recent leavers exit the labour force (Stinebrickner, 2002; Brummet et al., 2024) |
| Job switchers in other sectors | Move to another employer or occupation | Fewer than a quarter of United States leavers work outside education; about a fifth to a quarter of Dutch qualification holders work in other sectors (Brummet et al., 2024; Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b) |

Note: The groups overlap over time; a leaver may pass through more than one state before returning or retiring definitively.

## 1.4. What this paper does and adds

This is a policy paper. Its contribution is to compile, verify and make salient what many countries have actually tried in order to bring former teachers back, from re-engagement campaigns and refresher courses to pension re-employment rules and salary top-ups for postponing retirement. The compilation matters because the field is documented unevenly. Announcements are plentiful; accounts of results are rare, and the OECD itself observes that many systems lack accurate data on the number, characteristics and motivations of potential returnees (OECD, 2024). Wherever possible, this paper therefore reports programme take-up as verified against official sources, including annual reports, audit institutions and parliamentary records, rather than against launch publicity.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the evidence on why teachers leave, where they go and what they say would bring them back, treating former teachers as a population rather than a statistic. Chapter 3 is the core of the paper. It presents the international experience with re-entry policy, organised into programmes that actively recruit and support returners (Section 3.1) and the regulatory settings, above all pension and re-employment rules, that permit or obstruct return (Section 3.2). Chapter 4 draws out lessons and recommendations for policymakers.
