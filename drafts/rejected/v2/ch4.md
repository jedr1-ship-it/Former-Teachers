# 4. Lessons and recommendations for policymakers

Two things stand out from the compilation in Chapter 3. The first is that former teachers reward policy attention in their own right. The people documented across these programmes arrive with classroom craft, curriculum knowledge and the capacity to support colleagues that no initial teacher education programme can supply, and where their skills have been tested in randomised trials, in structured tutoring roles, they have delivered learning gains at scale (Gersten et al., 2015). The OECD reached this position two decades ago, when *Teachers Matter* identified former teachers as a potentially important source of recruits and recommended that education systems keep in contact with them (OECD, 2005), and it has restated it recently, listing re-entry support among the levers for expanding teacher supply (OECD, 2024). What had been missing between those two statements was a record of what governments actually did and what happened next. That record, assembled and verified here, is thin, recent and, with one exception, unevaluated, but it is now sufficient to support positions.

The recommendations that follow are justified first by what former teachers bring back to the profession, and second by a supply margin that is real but modest. Nothing in the compiled record suggests that re-entry can close a shortage; the best-documented programmes returned tens or low hundreds of teachers to systems short by thousands. A policy strand can be worth designing well without being decisive, and this one is. Each recommendation states the evidence it rests on; where a recommendation holds only under stated conditions, the conditions are named.

## 4.1. Recommendations

**1. Build the returner data infrastructure before legislating for the reserve.** Systems that maintain teacher registers can see their inactive pool and size it against the shortage; the Netherlands counts some 62 000 former teachers against a combined shortage of 13 600 full-time equivalents (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b; DUO, 2026), England counted 260 000 qualified teachers out of service in 2018 (Worth, 2023), Michigan 61 252 certificate holders not teaching (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021) and Australia around 19% of 553 300 registrants outside schools (AITSL, 2025). Systems without such registers, among them Germany, Austria and France, conduct their entire re-entry debate around the one pool they can see, retirees. The OECD has already identified missing returner data as a strategic constraint (OECD, 2024). Counting comes before recruiting.

**2. Diagnose the exit margin first; re-entry policy inherits the exit's cause.** Across 19 OECD systems, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23, and on average 51% of those leavers resigned rather than retired, with resignations above 80% of departures in Denmark, England and Estonia but retirements the majority in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a). A resignation-heavy system is recruiting back working-age professionals who walked away from the job's conditions, in England accepting roughly 10% lower pay to do so (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015) and in Australia reporting higher job satisfaction after leaving despite a wage penalty (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); a retirement-heavy system faces pension arithmetic instead. Michigan's inactive certificate holders, by contrast, put salary first (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The same incentive will not travel between these cases; the condition for transferring any design below is that the exit profile matches.

**3. Recruit from the employer's own records, and bundle the paperwork into one contact.** Direct, personalised outreach to known former employees is the cheapest instrument in the compilation and the only one with a fully published funnel. New South Wales contacted more than 1 500 recent leavers from departmental records, packaged accreditation, approval to teach and background checks into a single process, and placed 145 returners doing casual and temporary work across more than 600 schools, around 60% of them from retirement (NSW Government, 2024; see Table 3.1). Vienna and Salzburg wrote directly to their retirees (APA/ORF, 2023; Box 3.3). This is *Teachers Matter*'s advice to maintain contact with former teachers (OECD, 2005) put into operation at near-zero marginal cost, and it should be the default first move everywhere.

**4. Interest is cheap and conversion is the constraint, so fund conversion inside the hiring calendar, tied to real vacancies.** England's Return to Teaching pilot registered 5 729 expressions of interest, supported 354 people and placed 27 in permanent posts in its target subjects, at GBP 37 400 per additional returner (Buchanan et al., 2018; Box 3.1); the Netherlands budgeted subsidies for 500 returners and had paid out fewer than 200 by its last published count (Box 3.2). The corrective was identified by England's own successor scheme, which tied funded support to named vacancies in the live hiring round (NCTL, 2017). Standing adviser services are far cheaper per recorded returner, but their additionality has never been measured (UK Parliament, 2022), and support without a vacancy attached buys registrations, not teachers.

**5. Remove the pension penalty on return, and control cost by targeting need rather than stacking eligibility screens.** The revealed-preference evidence is consistent. North Carolina's 1999-2009 window, full salary on top of full pension, drew around 3 500 retirees back, disproportionately into high-need schools, and returns collapsed when it closed (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024); Georgia's stacked conditions of 30 years' service, a one-year wait and subject and region caps yield roughly 350 full-time rehires a year against some 6 000 unfilled or under-certified posts, by the state auditor's own count (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025); New Mexico's no-caps regime drew 154 users in its first year (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023; see Table 3.2). Deadweight is better controlled by conditioning on need, as China does by tying its Silver Age allowance to poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority and border counties (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023; Box 3.4), as Florida did by shortage subject (Feng and Sass, 2018) and as Portugal did in narrowing its supplement to shortage zones (RTP, 2026). Re-employment caps, where they exist, are two-way dials that need equity guardrails against crowding out new graduates, as Ontario's three decades of raising and cutting its 50-day limit demonstrate (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Alphonso, 2024; Box 3.5). One caution on the form of the money applies throughout, since teachers value current cash roughly five times as highly as deferred pension wealth (Fitzpatrick, 2015).

**6. Make streamlined re-accreditation a standing rule, not a crisis waiver.** New Zealand shows the end state. A waiver of the Teacher Education Refresh requirement for returners holding a relief-teaching offer was extended repeatedly and then absorbed into the Teaching Council's ordinary registration policy, alongside a statutory allowance to relief teach 20 half-days a year without a practising certificate and government-paid certification fees (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026; New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026). Ireland's substitution concession for career-break teachers, renewed one year at a time since 2021/22, is the pattern to avoid (Department of Education and Youth, 2025); a returner weighing a career decision cannot plan around a rule with an expiry date.

**7. Part-time and flexible posts are the actual price of return; systems unwilling to pay it should not expect returners.** Career breakers, the most return-committed segment of England's leavers, came back at double the rate of other former teachers and wanted part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). Portugal's union leaders attributed weak returner uptake to accumulated fatigue and an uninviting working environment rather than to the pay terms (SÁBADO, 2024). *Teachers Matter* made both halves of the point in 2005, that part-time opportunities raise return rates and that induction support belongs to returners as well as beginners (OECD, 2005). Advertising full classroom loads on unchanged conditions to people who left those conditions is not a re-entry policy.

**8. Value retirees' craft in tutoring roles, where the trial evidence supports them, and stay modest on mentoring, where it does not.** Retired and substitute teachers delivering structured small-group mathematics tutoring reproduced, across 994 first graders in 76 schools, an effect size of +0.34 first obtained with graduate students (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012), and older volunteers tutoring reading produced gains of 0.13-0.16 for students together with randomised evidence of health benefits for the volunteers themselves (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; What Works Clearinghouse, 2010; Fried et al., 2004; Carlson et al., 2015). The one randomised trial of retirees as new-teacher mentors, by contrast, found no significant effect on student achievement or teacher retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; What Works Clearinghouse, 2019). Tutoring programmes can be funded on evidence; mentoring programmes are still a hypothesis.

**9. Distinguish the stay-on margin from the come-back margin, and expect the first to dominate at equal money.** Portugal is the one system to have run both side by side, and the result was 63 accepted returnees against a target of 200 under its pension-plus-salary return route, while roughly 2 200 teachers took the EUR 750 monthly supplement to postpone retirement (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024; Diário de Notícias, 2024; RTP, 2026; see Section 3.1). The asymmetry is consistent with causal evidence that pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4-10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). Retention windows should be exhausted first; re-entry is the second channel, and budgets should be split accordingly.

**10. Attach a sunset date, a mandated uptake count and an arm's-length review to every new measure.** The systems that know what their re-entry measures achieved are the systems that legislated the accounting. Georgia wrote the audit requirement into the statute and received one (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025); Austria's Court of Audit produced the only audited national take-up figure in Europe, 1 062 returners over 2018/19 to 2023/24 (Rechnungshof, 2025); New Mexico's retirement board reports participation annually (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023); Louisiana's Act 344 mandates an actuarial report before 2034 (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). Everywhere the accounting was voluntary, it stopped at the launch announcement (see Table 4.1).

## 4.2. What the evidence does not show

No quasi-experimental study anywhere prices a re-entry incentive. The causal literature that exists concerns retention and initial entry (Fleck et al., 2025; Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026), and its elasticities cannot be borrowed unexamined for people who have already revealed a preference against prevailing conditions. The recommendations above therefore rest on administrative and audited take-up under different rule designs, on one independent programme evaluation, on randomised trials of retirees in instructional roles rather than of re-entry incentives, and on OECD comparative indicators. They identify which designs move take-up and which suppress it; none of them establishes the causal return on a euro of re-entry spending, because no government has yet generated the data that would allow it.

The reason is visible in Table 4.1. Across the initiatives compiled for this paper, the modal re-entry measure published a launch announcement and nothing afterwards. The OECD has named the underlying weakness, observing that "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically" (OECD, 2024). The pattern below indicates that the gap extends past potential returnees to actual ones. Several governments do not appear to know, or at least do not publish, how many teachers their own measures brought back.

## 4.3. What this paper establishes

This is a policy paper, and its contribution is the record itself. Before this compilation, the international experience with teacher re-entry existed as scattered announcements, single-country evaluations and pension-board minutes; it can now be read as one body of evidence, verified to primary sources. Four programme funnels from outreach to confirmed returns are on the record together for the first time, from England, New South Wales, the Netherlands and Portugal, and they support a generalisation that no re-entry programme with a published target has met it. One audited national count exists, Austria's 1 062 returners over six school years, and it exists because a court of audit went looking (Rechnungshof, 2025). The full design spectrum of pension re-employment rules, from North Carolina's neutrality to Georgia's stacked screens, is documented alongside the take-up each produced. The randomised record on retirees is now stated precisely, strong for structured tutoring, null so far for mentoring. And the absence of follow-up data is itself established as a finding, system by system, in Table 4.1.

The paper also fixes the terms of the argument. Former teachers merit analysis as a population, for the experience they carry and can transmit, not merely as a residual on a vacancy spreadsheet; and re-entry is a modest margin of the response to shortages, worth engineering well precisely because none of its cost need be wasted on the illusion that it is the whole answer. The design lessons above are actionable now. What remains for governments is the cheapest recommendation on the list and the one with the highest return for the next paper on this subject, which is to count who comes back.
