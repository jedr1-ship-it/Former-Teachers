# 2. Why teachers leave, where they go, and what would bring them back

Most teachers who leave the profession do so voluntarily and at working age, and most end up employed close to education on pay no better than they earned in the classroom. That single regularity, documented below across administrative registers, panel surveys and follow-up studies, defines the population that re-entry programmes address. This chapter establishes who leaves and how much of the outflow is chosen rather than imposed, why teachers go, where they end up and what they retain, and what they themselves say would bring them back. The programme evidence in Chapter 3 can only be judged against this baseline.

## 2.1. Who leaves, when in their career, and how far exit is a choice

Attrition in most OECD systems is moderate in level and dominated by resignation rather than retirement. In 2022/23, across the 19 OECD countries and economies with comparable data, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers from pre-primary to upper secondary education left the profession on average; France, Greece, Ireland and Israel sat under 3%, Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania above 10%, and rates were somewhat higher in pre-primary education (7.3%) than in primary (5.8%) or secondary (5.9%) (OECD, 2025a). An average of 51% of these leavers resigned rather than retired. That split varies sharply by system, and it matters for everything that follows in this paper. In Denmark, Estonia and England (United Kingdom), more than 80% of leavers resigned, so the departing cohort there is overwhelmingly of working age; retirement was instead the majority exit route in Argentina (primary education), France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel, where the exit margin is largely actuarial (OECD, 2025a). Resignation is also an early-career phenomenon. In the seven OECD countries reporting seniority, the share of resigning teachers with under five years of experience ran from 16% to 68%, one in three on average, and the same source notes that most countries maintain routes back for teachers who wish to return (OECD, 2025a).

England illustrates what these rates look like inside one workforce over time. Around 10% of teachers have left the state-funded sector each year for most of the past decade, a strikingly stable share when set against survey headlines about intended departures (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). In 2023/24, 40 800 full-time-equivalent teachers left, 9.0% of the qualified workforce, while 41 700 entered in 2024/25, of whom 17 000 (41%) were newly qualified; the Department for Education notes that returners rose while every other entrant category fell (Department for Education, 2025a). Exit and re-entry are thus not exceptional events in England but large, recurring flows through a workforce of roughly 450 000 full-time equivalents. The United States supplies the second well-documented case. National turnover between 2020/21 and 2021/22 reached 15.1% of public school teachers, split between 8.0% who moved schools and 7.1% who left teaching, and roughly three in four departures were voluntary and not retirements (Tan et al., 2026). The leaving is heavily concentrated rather than general. Uncertified teachers turned over at 20.1% against 14.7% for the certified, and rates ran 35-37% higher where low-income students and students of colour are most concentrated (Tan et al., 2026). Over the long run the American level is flat. Pooled labour-force data for 2015-2024 put annual occupational leaving at 7.6%; the pandemic lifted the rate from 6.5% in 2019 to 9.8% in 2020, but by 2023/24 it was back at 7.7%, within a series that has stayed broadly stable across four decades (Aldeman and Yi, 2025).

Set against comparable occupations, teaching does not haemorrhage staff. A decade of United States labour-force data showed 7.7% of teachers leaving their profession each year, compared with 6.1% of nurses, 8.0% of accountants and 14.9% of social workers, and the differences shrink further when the comparison is restricted to public school teachers; early-career teachers behaved much like early-career workers elsewhere (Harris and Adams, 2007). The same ordering holds in 2015-2024 data (Aldeman and Yi, 2025). What is genuinely distinctive about teaching is the timing of retirement. Teachers retire considerably earlier than other professionals (Harris and Adams, 2007), a pattern consistent with pension systems whose wealth accrual spikes in the mid-to-late fifties and then flattens or turns negative, pulling teachers out at ages that would be mid-career in most graduate occupations (Costrell and Podgursky, 2009). A material share of the "former teacher" population is therefore not disaffected but pension-timed, a distinction that Section 2.4 shows carries directly into what would bring each group back.

## 2.2. Why they leave

Workload, working conditions and school leadership outweigh salary as exit motives in almost every system with direct evidence; the United States is the documented exception. Meta-analytic work sorts the correlates of turnover into three clusters, namely personal characteristics, school-level factors (above all leadership and working conditions) and external policy factors such as accountability regimes and pay structures (Nguyen et al., 2020). Within that frame, the Australian record is the most complete. Twenty-two waves of household panel data tie exits to high stress, heavy workloads and poor work-life balance rather than to pay (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025), and when 256 former Australian teachers who left between 2016 and 2022 were asked directly, school leadership issues came first (17.7%), followed by workload (16.6%), the workplace environment (9.7%) and student behaviour (7.4%), with reasons typically multiple and the decision protracted (Brandenburg et al., 2024).

The stress channel is visible OECD-wide. TALIS 2018 found 18% of lower secondary teachers experiencing a lot of stress in their work, and such teachers were about twice as likely to expect to stop teaching within five years (OECD, 2020). TALIS 2024 puts high stress at 19% of teachers and adds a refinement, in that stress from student discipline, intimidation and verbal abuse predicts the wish to leave more strongly than workload, accountability or reform pressures do; overall, 17% of non-retiring teachers intend to leave within five years (OECD, 2025b). Intentions overstate behaviour several-fold and should be read as a dissatisfaction gauge, not a forecast (Nguyen et al., 2025). The workload behind the survey answers is nonetheless concrete. English teachers and leaders averaged a 48.1-hour working week in 2024, down from 49.4 hours the year before, and 43% judged their workload unacceptable, an improvement on 57% in 2022 but still more than two in five (Department for Education, 2025b).

In the United States, by contrast, pay is a first-order correlate of leaving. An extra USD 1 000 of cost-of-living-adjusted salary goes with turnover 0.34 percentage points lower, and predicted turnover drops by roughly 40% between salaries of USD 50 000 and USD 70 000; gradients on perceived leadership effectiveness (turnover of 18.7% against 9.0% between the lowest and highest ratings) and job satisfaction (22.0% against 8.0%) are steeper still (Tan et al., 2026). The estimates rest on a linear probability model and carry no causal identification, but stated preferences point the same way, since a third of Michigan's certified non-teachers ranked better pay among their top three reasons for staying out of the classroom (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The revealed preferences of European and Australian leavers run opposite. English leavers took wages roughly 10% below those of comparable stayers, gaining shorter hours and greater satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015), and Australian leavers earned 6.2% less per week one year after exit yet reported markedly higher job satisfaction (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The evidence is genuinely split, and the split is between one country and most of the others.

Life events form the third stream of exits, and historically the largest for women. The classic hazard analysis of certified female teachers from the 1972 United States high-school cohort found the arrival of a newborn child to be the dominant exit factor, well ahead of pay or alternative jobs, and about 67% of the exiting women left the labour force altogether rather than changing occupation (Stinebrickner, 2002). Parental leave and female labour supply have changed profoundly since, so the share should be read as historical, but the mechanism has not disappeared. It survives in the career-break returners documented below, for whom the exit was never a verdict on teaching.

## 2.3. Where they go, and who former teachers are

Contemporary leavers overwhelmingly remain employed, most of them close to education, and few improve their pay. In Australia, within a year of exit, 22.1% of former teachers hold non-teaching jobs inside school education and another 21.8% are found in the wider education, health care and social assistance category, while 28.0% are out of the labour force and 13.9% have retired; job satisfaction sits 0.13 standard deviations higher one year after leaving and 0.19 higher after three, alongside gains in leisure satisfaction, even though weekly wages are 6.2% lower at one year and 9.4% lower at three (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). In England over half of leavers other than retirees stay within the education sector, at the roughly 10% wage discount already noted (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). United States tax records tell the same story, with more than half of leavers remaining in education broadly defined, about a fifth exiting the labour force, fewer than a quarter taking jobs outside education, and mean earnings still slightly below pre-exit levels eight years on (Brummet et al., 2024); Georgia administrative data had earlier shown that very few leavers move to jobs paying more than teaching did (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006). The old pattern in which most exiting women left employment entirely (Stinebrickner, 2002) has given way to one in which the typical former teacher is working, reachable and not priced out of return.

How many such people exist can be counted wherever qualification registers can be linked to employment records, and the counts are consistently large (Table 2.1). The Dutch registers identify just over 62 000 people who qualified as teachers after 2000 and now work outside education, whereas the combined shortage stood at 13 600 full-time-equivalent posts at the end of 2023, a ratio of roughly 4.5 to 1; on the authors' arithmetic, the shortage would disappear if 22% of this reserve returned full time, and the count excludes anyone who qualified before 2000, so it understates the true pool (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b). As Chapter 1 argued, this arithmetic is context rather than promise, and the stricter Dutch measure shows why. Counting only those at least two years out and excluding retirees, the education executive agency finds 34 000 former primary and 64 000 former secondary or vocational teachers, 22% and 43% of the respective qualified stocks, and both pools shrank by 17-18% between 2015 and 2024; the agency states plainly that the reserve does not solve the teacher shortage (DUO, 2026). England's qualification and pension databases recorded, in 2018, some 260 000 working-age holders of qualified teacher status who had previously served and another 106 000 who had never served (Worth, 2023). Australia's registration data show about 19% of 553 300 registered teachers outside schools in 2023 (AITSL, 2025). Michigan's certification registry, examined in Box 2.1, counts 43% of the certified stock outside the public school classroom. Where no register exists the pool is invisible, and the debate contracts to the only group anyone can see. Germany publishes no count of qualified non-teachers; what can be seen is 457 000 retired teachers as of 2021, of whom just over 5 000 were back teaching in 2022/23, below 1% of a workforce of some 800 000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).

Two features of this population deserve emphasis, because they explain why this paper treats former teachers as worth attention in their own right. The first is composition. The measured reserves are mostly of working age, not retired, as Michigan's 93% under 60, England's 260 000 working-age leavers and the OECD-wide finding that half of departures are resignations all attest; retirees form a distinct, older segment that is large where the register countries cannot see past it, as in Germany and Austria. The second is what these people carry. Former teachers hold full qualifications, classroom craft and, in the case of mid- and late-career leavers, precisely the accumulated experience and subject knowledge that systems struggle to replace, together with whatever they have learned outside schools since. The OECD has recognised this for two decades. *Teachers Matter* described former teachers as "a potentially important source of recruits" and observed that, in 2000/01, returners already supplied about one quarter of the full-time entrants to the United Kingdom's maintained sector and over half of the part-time entrants, a returner population in which women outnumbered men and 40% were over 45 (OECD, 2005). *Education Policy Outlook 2024* adds that returners bring diverse career experiences that can enhance teaching quality and drive innovation (OECD, 2024). There is also an obvious complementarity with the mentoring gap, since only 22% of novice teachers across the OECD have an assigned mentor (OECD, 2019), and experienced returners and recent retirees are a natural source of that capacity, a role several of the programmes in Chapter 3 exploit.

## 2.4. What former teachers say would bring them back

Asked directly, former teachers name a redesigned job, not a better-paid version of the old one, in every surveyed system except the United States. Inactive Dutch teachers score several aspects of their present jobs above what active teachers report for theirs, and the levers they say would draw them back are less work pressure, more say over working hours and more autonomy, with salary one consideration among several rather than the decisive one (Somers et al., 2024a). In Australia, over half of registered teachers not working in schools would consider a return "under the right circumstances" and 5% are actively seeking one; more than half of the non-teaching respondents not on leave had been gone three years or longer yet kept their registration current (AITSL, 2025). England's evidence identifies which leavers mean it. Among the returner types identified around the national pilot, "career breakers" came back at roughly double the rate of the other groups and were drawn above all to part-time posts, which schools seldom advertise; under a third of supported returners had any classroom experience in the previous decade, so a credible refresher route is a second binding requirement (Worth and Sharp, 2018; Buchanan et al., 2018). The Michigan survey is the American counterpoint, and it leads with money. Higher salary topped the list of return incentives, followed by letting retirees keep their retirement benefits, improved benefits and student-loan forgiveness (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The 30% response rate cautions against precision, but the ordering matches the exit evidence in Section 2.2, in that people tend to name the lever whose absence pushed them out.

The weight of money in the return decision should therefore be read off the exit record, not assumed. Where leavers accepted a pay cut on the way out, as in England and Australia, and report higher satisfaction on lower earnings, an offer that merely restores or slightly betters teaching pay misreads the original decision, which was about hours, pressure and control over time. Where pay drove the exit, as the United States evidence suggests, financial offers are at least aimed at the right margin.

Motivation also differs systematically by why and when the person left. Career breakers, the descendants of Stinebrickner's life-event leavers, never rejected teaching; they need part-time and flexible posts, timing aligned with the school hiring cycle and a route to refresh confidence, and they return at the highest observed rates when those are present (Worth and Sharp, 2018; Buchanan et al., 2018). Dissatisfied leavers are the hardest segment, because exit made them measurably happier (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); for them the stated price of return is the removal of the conditions that pushed them out, and nothing cheaper. Retirees respond to a third set of levers entirely, led by pension neutrality. North Carolina's 1999-2009 window let retirees combine a full salary with a full pension; by 2009 more than 35% of the previously retirement-eligible had come back to full-time posts, the 2 694 returnees averaged 31 years of experience and were hired disproportionately by high-need schools, and returns fell to zero when the provision lapsed in 2010 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Michigan's respondents rank keeping retirement benefits among the top return incentives (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021), and the finding that teachers value deferred pension wealth at roughly 20 cents per present-value dollar (Fitzpatrick, 2015) implies that simple, current, salient money beats actuarial adjustments for this group. Austrian interview evidence adds that retiree returns are biographically rooted decisions shaped by individual, family, school-related and system-level factors more than by pay, and that keeping professional contact with the former school is what converts availability into an actual return (Resch, 2025a).

What genuinely tips the decision is thus narrower than the willingness surveys imply, and different from what policymakers tend to assume. Stated willingness is broad, with roughly half of United States leavers historically saying they would consider returning (NCES data as reported in Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021) and over half of Australia's non-teaching registrants saying the same (AITSL, 2025), yet realised return runs far behind. Between a quarter and a half of earlier American leaver cohorts eventually came back, and only around one fifth of the 2000-2019 exit cohort has done so (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022). The causal literature, meanwhile, prices the wrong margin for present purposes. The most systematic synthesis of why people enter and exit teaching finds financial incentives consistently effective for retention in quasi-experimental studies, supportive school leadership the most important non-pay factor, and professional development tested as a lever in only one such study (Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026). The parallel systematic review of causal interventions in education and healthcare employment reaches the same shape of conclusion, with financial incentives dominating an evidence base drawn overwhelmingly from the United States (72 of roughly 86 studies), and with pension enhancements that reward later retirement reducing retirement rates by between 4 and 10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). Neither synthesis contains a quasi-experimental estimate of a re-entry incentive for former teachers. That absence is a finding of this paper, not a footnote. Every elasticity policymakers currently borrow when designing return schemes was estimated on people who had not yet left, whereas the people the schemes target have already revealed, once, that the job on offer was not worth staying for. The programmes reviewed in Chapter 3 should be read with that asymmetry in view.
