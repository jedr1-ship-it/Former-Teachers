## 4.2. Regulation: pension rules, licensure and re-entry rights

Most of the regulatory action on re-entry concerns one group and one instrument: retirees, and the rules that determine whether a pension can be combined with a teaching salary. For teachers who resigned mid-career, the barriers to return are comparatively light – a lapsed licence, a re-accreditation requirement – and can be lowered by administrative means. For retired teachers, pension compatibility rules decide whether return is financially rational at all, and the evidence assembled below points one way: where the rules are permissive, retirees return in the thousands; where they are restrictive, in the hundreds, whatever the size of the shortage. Regulation, in this domain, is not the fine print of re-entry policy. It largely is the policy.

### Pension compatibility rules

Every pension scheme examined for this paper draws a line between retirement income and teaching income, and re-entry policy consists mainly of moving that line. Four dimensions recur across systems: a waiting period between retirement and re-employment; a cap on earnings, hours or days worked while the pension is paid; eligibility restrictions by subject, school or service record; and the treatment of contributions during re-employment. The six United States examples discussed here are illustrative rather than exhaustive; Georgia's state audit records that eight other southern states passed comparable return-to-work laws, with Georgia's the most restrictive among them (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025).

The fullest evidence on what happens when the line is removed altogether comes from North Carolina. Between 1999 and autumn 2009, the state allowed retired teachers to return to full-time classroom work while drawing both a full salary and a full pension, suspending the usual rule that capped part-time earnings at half the previous salary. Using administrative data on roughly 169 000 teachers in 2 900 schools, Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024) count about 3 500 returners over the decade, with more than half of all schools (52%) hiring at least one. The returners went disproportionately where they were needed: their schools had 3.7 percentage points more economically disadvantaged students than average, they were more likely than other teachers to teach mathematics, and 54% returned to the school in which they had taught before retiring, which suggests that retiree re-employment runs through existing relationships between schools and their former staff rather than through open labour-market matching. The authors could not estimate effects on vacancies or student achievement, but argue that the marginal cost of a rehired retiree is modest, because the pension is paid whether or not the retiree returns.

Georgia and New Mexico, which liberalised within months of each other in 2022, mark the two ends of the current design spectrum. Georgia's House Bill 385 lets retired members of the Teachers Retirement System teach full-time on full pension, but only after at least one year in retirement, only with 30 years of creditable service, and only in one of the three highest-need subject areas of the region; employers must pay both employee and employer contributions (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). The state audit mandated by the law found that 635 retired teachers worked full-time under it for at least a school year between FY2023 and FY2025, roughly 350 in any given year, or under 1% of a workforce of about 125 000 facing some 6 000 vacant or not-fully-certified positions. The audit judged the law's effect on both the shortage and the pension fund "minimal", and 45% of surveyed school systems reported that the statutory conditions had blocked a hire they would otherwise have made (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). The programme was nonetheless extended in 2026 to 2030, keeping the 30-year requirement but letting school systems define their own high-need areas (Teachers Retirement System of Georgia, 2026).

New Mexico's House Bill 73, by contrast, imposed no limit on salary or hours. Retirees may return for up to 36 months after a 90-day layout period, provided there is no pre-arranged return agreement and the pension board approves (New Mexico Legislature, 2022). In FY2023, the first year, 154 retirees used the new provision, and 2 792 retirees of the Educational Retirement Board were working in some capacity across all return-to-work tiers, out of roughly 48 000 potentially eligible (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023). The state then liberalised further in 2025, extending the window to 60 months and raising the low-earnings threshold to USD 25 000 (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025). The comparison is directional rather than causal, since the two systems differ in size and context, but it is consistent with the audit's own evidence from Georgia: restrictiveness maps onto uptake, and the binding restrictions are the ones legislatures write in to protect pension funds and ration access.

Other states have moved the same way, usually with sunset clauses. Louisiana extended its no-suspension critical-shortage route to all retirees of the teachers' retirement system in 2024, after a 12-month wait, and consolidated its rules in 2026, adding an unrestricted route for retirees aged 65 and over and raising the earnings cap for others from 25% of the annual benefit to 50% of final average compensation; no uptake figures have been published, and the only mandated review is an actuarial report due before 2034 (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024; 2026). Michigan replaced its patchwork of exceptions in 2022 with a simple rule, a bona fide termination plus nine months in retirement, after which a retiree may work without earnings limit, and in 2023 cut the wait to six months, with the loosening due to lapse in October 2028 unless renewed (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022; 2023). Neither state publishes counts of returning retirees, a gap taken up in Chapter 6.

Ontario shows the same instrument operated deliberately in both directions, and is discussed in Box 4.4. Its 50-day annual re-employment cap has been raised in shortages and cut in surpluses over three decades, and the March 2024 refusal of the teachers' federation to accept a further increase illustrates a constraint that United States legislatures do not face: where the pension plan is co-sponsored, the cap moves only with union consent (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026; Alphonso, 2024).

:::box
**Box 4.4. Ontario: a re-employment cap moved in both directions**

Under Schedule 1 to Ontario's Teachers' Pension Act, 1990, a retired member of the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan may work up to 50 days per school year for a participating employer without pension suspension; exceeding the limit stops the pension for every further month worked in that year (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). The ceiling has served as a two-way labour-market valve. In 1990, amid a severe shortage, it was raised from 20 to 95 days per year for the first three post-retirement years. In 2012 it was cut to a flat 50 days after a newspaper investigation found Ontario's ten largest boards spending CAD 108.3 million a year on retired supply teachers, paid nearly double the daily rate of new teachers (CAD 432.69 against CAD 227.36), while some 12 774 newly certified teachers competed for around 6 500 openings (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; OSSTF, n.d.).

During the pandemic the cap was temporarily restored to 95 days for the 2020/21 to 2022/23 school years, by agreement between the government and the Ontario Teachers' Federation (OTF), with boards required to report retiree hiring monthly as an OTF condition (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; OTF, 2023). The OTF cautioned that demand was thin: only about 60 of 142 000 retired members had expressed interest since September 2021, and the average age of retirees was 72 (Global News, 2022). In March 2024 the federation refused a fourth extension, arguing that "the Pension Plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers; it is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024; OCEOTA, 2024). Re-employment caps carry equity stakes on both sides, between unfilled classes and crowded-out new graduates, and in co-sponsored plans the dial cannot be turned by government alone.

France offers the barrier case. The cumul emploi-retraite framework allows pensions and earnings to be combined, but a retired public agent returning to their last employer, which is what returning to the Éducation nationale entails for most teachers, must wait six months from the effective date of the pension, a rule dating from the 2003 pension reform (Assemblée nationale, 2023). No official figures on retired teachers returning under the framework have been published. A 2023 parliamentary question asking which regulatory obstacles should be lifted so that willing retired teachers could cover unfilled absences was never answered and lapsed at the end of the legislature, while a 2026 exchange turned on income caps that penalise teachers who retired with pre-2015 early pension rights (Assemblée nationale, 2024; 2026).

England's rules are milder than any of the above, and are softening by scheme succession rather than by decision. Under the Teachers' Pension Scheme, abatement applies only to final-salary Age or Premature retirement benefits: a retiree may earn, in any tax year, up to an index-linked "salary of reference" minus the annual pension before the pension is suspended for the remainder of the scheme year. Benefits drawn early with actuarial reduction are never abated, and pensions accrued in the career-average scheme that covers all service since 2015 are not abated at all, so the constraint is confined to a shrinking cohort of final-salary entitlements and will disappear as career-average membership matures; compliance runs through employers' monthly data returns or a certificate of re-employment (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.).

Table 4.2 summarises the rules across these systems. Two patterns stand out. First, the direction of travel since 2020 has been liberalisation, usually time-limited: Georgia's law sunsets in 2030, Michigan's six-month rule in 2028, Virginia's provisions in 2028. Tying the loosening to the shortage that motivated it is defensible design, provided somebody is counting who returns, which in most of these systems nobody is. Second, uptake is documented in only three of the nine systems, and it is largest precisely where the penalty on return was removed most completely and for longest, in North Carolina.

### Licensure and re-accreditation

The second regulatory layer is the licence, and here the barriers are lower and the fixes cheaper. Virginia's Executive Directive Three of September 2022 instructed the Superintendent of Public Instruction to expedite renewal licences for retired and out-of-state teachers whose licences had lapsed, and to co-ordinate with the retirement system so that all vacant school positions were classified to allow retired teachers to fill them; the underlying pension mechanism, full pension plus salary in critical-shortage posts, has been statutory since 2001, and 2023 legislation halved the required break in service to six months (Will, 2022; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Virginia Retirement System, n.d.). The directive created no new entitlement, which is the point, since much of what deters returners is process, and process can be changed by instruction rather than statute.

New South Wales made the same move administratively. Its Teachers Re-Engage outreach of November 2023 paired direct contact of recent leavers with departmental support for regaining accreditation with the state regulator, approval to teach and the Working with Children Check, and the standing "returning teachers" service now routes returners into accreditation reactivation, including a dedicated regulator contact for teachers who retired before October 2004 (NSW Government, 2024; NSW Department of Education, 2026). New Zealand has gone furthest in dismantling re-certification requirements. A registered teacher whose practising certificate has expired may relief teach up to 20 half-days per calendar year without one, under a standing statutory allowance (New Zealand Legislation, 2020). Returners with a relief-teaching job offer are exempt from the Teacher Education Refresh programme that formerly applied after five or more years out, an exemption introduced as a time-limited measure and effectively mainstreamed by 2026, and the government pays practising-certificate fees until mid-2028 (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026; Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026). No uptake figures or evaluation have been published for any element of the package, so New Zealand stands as the clearest example of regulatory barrier removal as design, not yet as evidence.

### Career breaks and re-entry rights

A third, quieter instrument keeps leavers formally attached so that re-entry never becomes a regulatory event. Ireland's Career Break Scheme gives registered teachers unpaid leave of up to five years at a time, and ten over a career, with a right of return to teaching afterwards (Department of Education, 2019). Since at least 2021/22 the Department has annually suspended the scheme's bar on substitute work, most recently for 2025/26, so that teachers on career break can be employed as substitutes without restriction while retaining their return rights (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). This is retention-of-attachment rather than re-entry legislation, and no uptake figures or evaluation have been published, but it addresses the moment when most regulatory barriers are created, the moment of exit.

The pattern across the three layers is consistent with this section's opening claim. Pension rules are where the money is and where the movement has been; licensure fixes are cheap and administratively quick; re-entry rights prevent the barrier from arising. What no system yet demonstrates is that regulatory permission alone fills classrooms. Georgia's audited "minimal effect", Ontario's 60 interested retirees out of 142 000, and the thin uptake reporting almost everywhere else suggest that liberalisation raises take-up without, by itself, closing shortages, which is why Chapter 5 treats regulatory permission as a necessary condition and asks what has to sit alongside it.
