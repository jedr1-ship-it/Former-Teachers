# 3. What would bring them back

Asked what would bring them back, former teachers give a consistent answer, and it is rarely money. Across the systems where the inactive reserve has been surveyed, the conditions that drove the exit, above all workload, control over working hours and autonomy, dominate the stated price of return; pay leads the list only in the United States. Retired teachers respond to a different set of levers again, namely reduced and purposeful roles and pension rules that do not tax re-employment, and they matter in their own right because retirements account for the majority of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025). The causal literature on financial incentives, reviewed below, is robust but sits almost entirely on the retention and entry margins; no study anywhere yet identifies the causal effect of a re-entry incentive, and this chapter treats that gap as a finding rather than a footnote.

## 3.1. Working-age leavers would return for a different job, not a better-paid one

Willingness to return is broad. Over half of Australia's non-teaching registered teachers say they would consider returning "under the right circumstances", and 5% were actively looking to do so (AITSL, 2025); in the last United States national follow-up survey to ask, about half of those who had left teaching said they would consider returning (NCES, 2013, as cited in Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The operative question is what the right circumstances are, and the systems with the best data answer it in strikingly similar terms.

Inactive Dutch teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs, and the levers they say would bring them back are lower work pressure, more control over working hours and greater autonomy, with salary one lever among several rather than the dominant one (Somers et al., 2024). The Australian panel record explains why money sits so low. Teachers who left report job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations higher than comparable stayers one year after exit and 0.19 standard deviations higher after three years, even though their weekly wages are 6.2% lower at one year and 9.4% lower at three (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). A decision that left people more satisfied on less money was not about money, and money alone is unlikely to reverse it. England adds the operational detail. The returner segment most committed to coming back, those on career breaks, returned at about twice the rate of other leaver types and is particularly attracted by part-time work, which schools rarely advertise; fewer than a third of the returners supported by England's pilot had taught in the previous decade, making recent classroom experience a second binding constraint (Worth and Sharp, 2018). The pilot evaluation lists limited part-time availability and recruitment mistimed against the hiring cycle among the main barriers to placement (Buchanan et al., 2018).

The United States is the documented exception, and the split should be stated in numbers rather than smoothed over. Among Michigan's certified non-teachers, 33% placed a higher salary among their three most important reasons for not teaching, a salary increase was the incentive to return selected most often, and financial items, including allowing retirees to keep retirement benefits and student-loan forgiveness, dominate the rest of the incentive list (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The survey had a 30% response rate and its authors caution that the sample is not representative, but the ordering is the reverse of the Dutch one and matches the salary gradient in United States turnover discussed in Chapter 2.

For most systems, then, the offer that matches stated preferences is a reconstructed job, with advertised part-time posts, credible control over working hours, a lighter administrative load and a refresher route back to the classroom, rather than a premium on the job the returner already rejected once. Chapter 4 examines how far existing programmes deliver that offer.

## 3.2. What financial incentives can and cannot be expected to do

The most systematic reading of what teachers and potential teachers value comes from Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026), who build a single interdisciplinary model of the decision to enter or leave teaching and test it against a systematic review of survey experiments on job preferences, restricting the evaluation evidence to causal or quasi-experimental designs. The synthesis quantifies the relative weight attached to pay, workload, flexibility, pension arrangements and development opportunities in entry and exit choices. Across quasi-experimental evaluations it finds financial incentives consistently effective for retention, with supportive school leadership the most important non-pay factor; professional development, by contrast, has been tested as a recruitment or retention lever in only one quasi-experimental study.

Fleck et al. (2025) reach a compatible conclusion from a wider base, reviewing causal and quasi-experimental evaluations of interventions to raise employment in education and healthcare. Financial incentives dominate the evaluated literature and broadly work. A wage premium of about 10% in Norwegian shortage regions raised entry rates by 5.7 percentage points, one-time bonuses targeted at effective teachers produced positive effects in six of nine studies, and pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement reduced retirements by 4-10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). Causal evidence on working conditions is far thinner, though directionally consistent with the survey evidence, as a curriculum reform that raised workload increased turnover by around 4.3% (Fleck et al., 2025). The review's geography is itself a caveat, with 72 of its roughly 86 included studies coming from the United States.

The individual estimates behind these conclusions are sizeable. In England, retention payments of GBP 2 000 a year after tax, about 8% of gross salary, made eligible early-career mathematics and physics teachers 23% less likely to leave state-funded teaching in eligible years (Sims and Benhenda, 2022). In North Carolina, an annual USD 1 800 bonus for certified mathematics, science and special-education teachers in high-poverty and low-performing secondary schools reduced target-group turnover by about 17%, with the strongest response among experienced teachers, although implementation suffered from many teachers not knowing they qualified (Clotfelter et al., 2008). In Florida, loan forgiveness cut the attrition of middle and high-school mathematics and science teachers by 10.4% and 8.9% respectively, and a one-time bonus reduced exit by up to about 32% in the short run (Feng and Sass, 2018).

The gap in this literature matters as much as its content. Not one of the studies above, nor any study identified in either synthesis, estimates the causal effect of an incentive on the return of former teachers. The elasticities are estimated on serving teachers deciding whether to stay, or on graduates deciding whether to start. Former teachers have already revealed a preference against the job at its current price and conditions, and the Australian evidence shows them more satisfied on lower wages after acting on that preference (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). Borrowing retention elasticities for re-entry policy therefore assumes a symmetry of response that the stated-preference evidence contradicts. This paper records the absence of direct causal evidence on re-entry incentives as a finding in its own right. Until such evidence exists, retention and entry elasticities are best read as an upper bound on what an equivalent re-entry payment could achieve, and any new re-entry incentive should have an evaluation designed into it from the start.

## 3.3. Retirees respond to reduced hours, purpose and pension neutrality

Interest among retirees is not the constraint. In the United States, more than half of retiring baby-boomer teachers expressed interest in further employment, and about 70% of those were interested in new roles in education (Foster, 2010, as cited in DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). What converts interest into return is more selective. Nine episodic interviews with retired Austrian teachers, six returners and three non-returners within a broader 14-interview study including experts, find that the decision to return after retirement is a biographically rooted process shaped by individual, family, school-related and system-level factors rather than primarily by pay, with continuing contact with the former school reported as a key facilitator (Resch, 2025; Resch, 2025b). The effective pool, in other words, is largely those who never fully disengaged.

Pension rules then decide whether returning pays at all, and behaviour tracks the rules closely. North Carolina's 1999-2009 return-to-work window, which let retirees draw a full salary on top of a full pension, produced the strongest revealed-preference evidence available that pension neutrality re-activates retirees at scale (see Box 3.1). Georgia sits at the restrictive end of the design spectrum, requiring 30 years of service, a one-year wait and limits by subject and region, and the state's own auditors count roughly 350 full-time rehired retirees a year against some 6 000 vacant or not fully certified posts (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). Austria shows a subtler friction. Returning retirees keep their full pension, but those who retired as civil servants, the large majority of the retiring cohort, are excluded by law from prior-service crediting for any period already generating a pension and are therefore re-employed at the teacher starting salary, whereas former contract-scheme teachers have their service credited and broadly recover their pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023). Take-up has been modest but not trivial: the Austrian Court of Audit counted 1 062 retired teachers returning nationwide over the school years 2018/19 to 2023/24, peaking in 2021/22 and 2022/23 and declining since, against a teaching workforce of roughly 123 000 (Rechnungshof, 2025). Michigan's survey respondents make the same point in stated form, ranking the ability to keep retirement benefits among the leading incentives to return (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). A related warning concerns the form of the money. Teachers appear to value deferred pension wealth at about 20 cents per dollar of present value, preferring current wages roughly five to one (Fitzpatrick, 2015), so offers built on extra pension accrual, or bonuses layered over unresolved pension penalties, misprice the retiree market.

:::box
**Box 3.1. Pension neutrality at scale: North Carolina's return-to-work window (United States)**

**Design and incentives:** Between 1999 and 2009, North Carolina allowed retired teachers to return to full-time classroom work on a full salary while continuing to draw their full pension, a combination worth up to about 50% more income than a typical full-time teacher earned. Before and after the window, returning retirees were limited to part-time work at half salary.

**Take-up and results:** Take-up rose throughout the window until, by 2009, just over 35% of previously retirement-eligible teachers had returned to full-time work. The returners were experienced, with 31 years of service on average; 81% were women and they were disproportionately Black. High-need schools hired them disproportionately even though the policy contained no targeting provision. When the window closed in 2010, returns fell to zero.

**Lessons:** The episode is the clearest revealed-preference evidence available that pension rules, rather than willingness, are the binding constraint on retiree re-entry, and that an untargeted offer can nonetheless flow towards the schools that need it most. It also shows how policy-contingent the flow is, since what a rule change created, its expiry removed entirely.

Portugal ran the retention and re-entry margins side by side and produced the cleanest comparison on record. Decree-Law 51/2024 of 28 August 2024 created two distinct instruments that press coverage often conflated. Under Article 5, teachers retired for five years or less may return on fixed-term contracts in shortage subjects or understaffed schools, keeping their full pension and receiving additional compensation indexed to the first step of the career salary scale, pro-rated by teaching hours; under Article 6, teachers who have reached retirement conditions but stay in the classroom until the end of the school year receive a monthly supplement of EUR 750. Against a government target of 200 returnees, 79-80 retirees applied and 63 were accepted in 2024/25, at an average age of about 68, whereas roughly 2 200 teachers used the supplement to postpone retirement in 2025/26 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024; Diário de Notícias, 2024; Renascença, 2024b; RTP, 2026). Union leaders attributed the weak returner uptake to accumulated fatigue and an uninviting school environment rather than to the terms on offer (SÁBADO, 2024). The asymmetry suggests that, at comparable money, preventing an exit is far easier than reversing one, consistent with the causal finding that pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4-10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). In July 2026 the government restricted the supplement to shortage zones for 2026/27, judging that the blanket version was in many cases not justified (RTP, 2026).

What retirees are asked to do matters as much as what they are paid. The programmes that recruit older educators most readily do not offer the old job back; they offer structured, part-time, purposeful roles. Experience Corps, which places trained volunteers aged 50 and over, many of them former educators, in high-poverty elementary schools as reading tutors, works through fixed weekly sessions and modest stipends of up to about USD 278 per month (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009). Randomised evidence from the same programme documents benefits flowing to the volunteers themselves, including gains in physical activity, strength and cognitive activity (Fried et al., 2004) and, in the Baltimore trial, measured increases in hippocampal and cortical volumes among men (Carlson et al., 2015). Purpose is therefore not soft rhetoric but a compensable part of the offer, and one that allows re-entry policy to borrow a healthy-ageing rationale. The Portuguese and Austrian records suggest the converse also holds, in that a full classroom timetable on unchanged conditions is the least attractive offer that can be made to a retired teacher. | Strongest evidence type | Register-linked surveys and panel data (stated preference); no causal pricing of re-entry | Revealed preference under rule changes (North Carolina, Georgia, Austria, Portugal) |

Three implications carry forward. Offers to working-age leavers should trade on the redesigned job, with advertised part-time posts, control over hours and a credible refresher route, because that is the stated price of return in every surveyed system outside the United States. Offers to retirees should start from pension neutrality and reduced, purposeful roles, the only levers with revealed-preference support. And where a system chooses to spend money, the causal evidence indicates it buys more retention than re-entry. Chapter 4 examines how the programmes now in the field embody, or ignore, these regularities.
