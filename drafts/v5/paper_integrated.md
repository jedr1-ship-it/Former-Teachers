# Abstract

Education systems in much of the OECD are short of teachers, and their workforces are ageing towards retirement. Governments have responded, as they have for decades, with active policies to bring former teachers back, from recruitment campaigns to changes in pension and licensing rules. This policy paper compiles that international record and asks what actually moved people. The answer divides cleanly. Campaigns returned teachers by the dozen, while every large return followed a repair to the terms the state itself sets, because in most systems the price of a return is written in pension and civil-service law and no ministry has ever totalled it. Former teachers left to buy back their time, and they return for offers shaped like that trade, hours rather than posts. What returns with them is experience that lifts the colleagues around them, capability the system itself paid to build. The lesson for policy is to repair the deal rather than advertise it, and to judge every instrument by who returned and who stayed.

# Résumé

Dans une grande partie de la zone OCDE, les systèmes éducatifs manquent d'enseignants et leurs effectifs vieillissent vers la retraite. Les gouvernements y répondent, comme depuis des décennies, par des politiques actives visant à faire revenir les anciens enseignants, des campagnes de recrutement aux modifications des règles de pension et de certification. Ce document d'orientation compile ce bilan international et demande ce qui a réellement fait bouger les intéressés. La réponse est nette. Les campagnes ramènent des enseignants par dizaines, tandis que chaque retour massif a suivi une réparation des conditions que l'État fixe lui-même, car dans la plupart des systèmes le prix d'un retour est inscrit dans le droit des pensions et de la fonction publique, et aucun ministère n'en a jamais établi le total. Les anciens enseignants sont partis pour racheter leur temps, et ils reviennent pour des offres à la mesure de cet échange, des heures plutôt que des postes. Avec eux revient une expérience qui élève leurs collègues, une capacité que le système a lui-même payée. La leçon pour l'action publique est de réparer le contrat plutôt que de l'annoncer, et de juger chaque instrument à l'aune de qui est revenu et de qui est resté.

# Key messages

- **Every system examined in this paper is sitting on a reserve of former teachers it has never honestly counted, and retirement waves are feeding it.** The headline figures in circulation count rows in a register, where a veteran who left twenty years ago sits beside last year's resigner. A count netted for licence status and recency of practice would be smaller and far more useful, and every register already holds the data to produce one within a year.

- **Leaving was a purchase of time, paid for in earnings, and former teachers return for offers that let them keep what they bought.** Every system that recruits them in numbers sells days and part-time contracts rather than full-time posts, and the part-time work they want is precisely what schools rarely advertise. The offer that works has been identified for years; stocking it costs the price of an advertisement.

- **Campaigns bring teachers back by the dozen; the rules governments already own bring them back by the thousand.** England's national return pilot placed twenty-seven teachers. North Carolina, which for a decade stopped docking pensions for returning to work, drew more than three thousand retirees back through ordinary hiring with no campaign attached. Re-entry alone will not close a shortage, and the record's honest lesson is cheaper than the campaigns that promise it will.

- **The price of a return is set by the state, mostly by accident, in laws education ministries do not write.** The salary step a returner lands on, and whether pension and salary can be drawn together, sit in pension and civil-service law with departments that have never answered for a classroom. In Austria a crediting rule written for the whole federal service leaves two identical teaching careers with offers most of a salary scale apart. A ministry could total what its own state charges a former teacher to come back, and publish the bill within weeks; none has.

- **Recovering an experienced teacher recovers capability the system already paid to build, and the proven way to buy it is as a defined job at a specified dose.** Experienced colleagues measurably lift the teachers around them, so a returning veteran is more than a filled vacancy. Where former teachers tutored to a protocol with a set number of sessions, children learned; where systems bought seniority and goodwill, what the school gained went unmeasured. The premium is plausible, and a system finds out by specifying the job and writing the evaluation into the contract of purchase.

- **A re-entry policy earns belief when somebody other than its sponsor counts what it did, and the count outlives the press release.** Placements are the friendliest number a programme owns; the one returner cohort anyone could follow lost close to a third of its members again within four years. Each instrument that produced usable evidence carried a counting duty lodged outside the sponsoring ministry, with a sunset timed so the numbers could still change the decision. That duty costs a single clause at the drafting stage.

# 1. Introduction

Every year teachers hand back their classroom keys and keep their licences. Some go home, to a newborn or an ageing parent. Others resign mid-career and take the degree somewhere that makes fewer claims on their evenings. The rest, in time, retire, on a date fixed by pension rules rather than by anything that happened in a classroom. Together they form one of the largest movements of qualified professionals out of any public service, and they leave at a costly moment. Across OECD countries the share of students in schools whose principals report that a lack of teaching staff hinders instruction rose from 26% in 2018 to 47% in 2022, a perception measure; counted vacancies are rarer, averaging 1.6% of positions in the systems that measure them, though above 4% in Austria and Sweden (OECD, 2025a). Behind the gap stands a retirement wave. The serving workforce is ageing, a concern from the Baltic states to Portugal, and in Italy teachers aged 55 and over substantially outnumber those under 30 (European Commission, 2023). A gap in front and a wave behind it are why governments are knocking on former teachers' doors.

They have been knocking for decades. England and Wales were already sponsoring around a hundred refresher courses a year for returners in the early 2000s, when OECD teacher policy first named former teachers a potentially important source of recruits (OECD, 2005), and supporting the re-entry of teachers who left the profession appears among the supply levers in the Organisation's current policy stocktake (OECD, 2024). The knocking is partly shortage cover, and this paper says so plainly; systems reach for their former teachers because vacancies hurt. That motive is respectable without being sufficient, since re-entry alone cannot close a shortage, and nothing in what follows sells it as the fix. The premise is rather that former teachers matter in their own right. Each arrives already trained at public expense, already licensed or relicensable, and fluent in how schools actually run, so recovering one is recovering capability the system itself paid to build. Meanwhile some of them simply come back unasked. Wherever anyone measures the inflow, returners supply a third or more of new entrants in a given year (Department for Education, 2023; Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). Every government already operates a re-entry system. Few operate it on purpose.

The people beyond the doors stay close and stay qualified. In the United States, where linked records make it possible to follow leavers, more than half remain in education broadly defined and fewer than a quarter take jobs outside it (Brummet et al., 2024); earlier cohort evidence points the same way (Stinebrickner, 2002). The licence survives the resignation, and with it, in most systems, the service history that would set a returning salary. Ministries have taken to calling this population a reserve, and the word circulates in budget debates as if it named a measured quantity. It names a register rule, and the rules differ enough to change the answer entirely. In England, nearly a third of the headline pool of qualified teachers outside state service has never been in service at all, a group the department already holds as a separate series (Worth, 2023, citing DfE). In Michigan, whose register links certificates to employment, seven in ten certified non-teachers had not taught in a public school at any point in the previous five years, and close to half held expired certificates (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The Netherlands managed to hold two reserves at once, a cohort-truncated count of just over 62 000 (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b) alongside a register series near 98 000 (DUO, 2026). The gap between the figures is the gap between the rules. Yet every register examined in this paper already holds the data for an honest count, since service histories separate the served from the never served, certification records separate valid credentials from lapsed ones, and last-taught dates separate recent leavers from those a decade away. Each cut moves the count downward, so the headline figures are known to overstate. The OECD has already named the underlying gap, observing that many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees (OECD, 2024). The reserve is real. It has simply never been honestly counted, and a ministry quoting one is a definition and a query away from quoting a measurement.

Who are these people? Teachers leave by three different doors, and the door decides most of what a return would take. The first leads out of paid work altogether, most often into care and family responsibilities, and the people who took it hold a live qualification and no current employer. The second leads into another sector, taken by mid-career resigners who carried the qualification with them, a group the linked records suggest is smaller than commonly assumed. The third is retirement, and it opens on actuarial terms. The split is knowable and varies more than the debate assumes. Across OECD countries with data, about half (51%) of fully qualified leavers in the most recent year measured resigned rather than retired; resignations exceed 80% of departures in Denmark, England and Estonia, while retirements dominate in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a). The door also decides which body of law prices the return, pension and service-crediting rules past pensionable age, salary scales and certification rules before it. A system of working-age resigners faces a different repair job from a system of pensioners, which is why the exit split is the first diagnostic to run.

This paper compiles what governments have actually done to bring former teachers back, and reads that record for what worked. Drawing on programme evaluations, audit findings, legislation and administrative statistics from some twenty education systems, it asks what governments offered former teachers, what price their own rules attached to return, and what verifiably happened. The compilation is the contribution, because the international record of these attempts has not previously been set side by side. Read together, it divides sharply. Recruitment campaigns returned teachers by the dozen; when a rule changed and the price of return fell, teachers came back by the thousand through ordinary hiring. The decisive re-entry policy of a state is therefore the price its own laws put on a return, a price written mostly by accident in pension, certification and salary rules that sit largely outside education ministries, and a bill no ministry has ever totalled. Repairing that deal, rather than advertising it, is what the record rewards. Section 2 stays with the people, asking why they left, where they went and what a returner can bring back to schools; Section 3, the body of the paper, then describes the policies themselves, family by family in order of the returns they produced, and closes with the operational lessons the record supports. The examples illustrate types of instrument rather than representing the national systems they are drawn from, and nothing in what follows categorises countries.

None of it matters unless the people themselves would come. The first question therefore belongs to them. What did they give up teaching to get, and what would make a return worth the price?

# 2. Why teachers leave, where they go and what they can bring back

A government that invites its former teachers back is asking them to reverse a decision most of them do not regret. Followed into their new jobs, leavers report themselves more satisfied and are paid less than the colleagues they left behind, and that combination is what a purchase looks like. They gave up earnings and received time. In Australian panel data spanning two decades, job satisfaction rises after the exit while wages fall and stay lower for years (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025), and English leavers accept roughly a tenth less pay in exchange for shorter hours (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). This section reads the exit as that purchase and every re-entry offer as a bid against it. It first sorts the documented motives into four categories and names the family of policy that answers each. It then follows leavers into their next jobs and asks what a return could bring back, ending with the specification Section 3 will test.

## 2.1. Why they leave

**The motives on record sort into four categories, intrinsic, environmental, economic and life-cycle, and each category points at a different family of re-entry policy.** The taxonomy tracks the field's largest meta-analysis, which groups the correlates of leaving into personal characteristics, school factors and external or policy factors (Nguyen et al., 2020); this paper keeps that architecture while separating life-cycle motives into a category of their own, because they answer to different instruments than the rest of the personal column.

The most-documented motives carry one conclusion. Most of the recorded weight of leaving rests on rules and conditions a government already owns, and every motive on the list is answered by at least one of the five policy families of Section 3 (Table 2.1).

The intrinsic motives are the ones a returner would carry back through the door. Stress works through the organisational channel, roughly doubling the stated intention to leave (OECD, 2020), yet which stressor dominates refuses to travel across borders (Table 2.1), and the accountability climate is a policy possession wherever it bites. The remedy it points to is the family that changes the job itself, a defined role rather than the post that was left.

The environmental motives lead the table because behaviour, not testimony, put them there. The English leavers gave up a tenth of their wages to escape the hours, and the motive binds hardest where teaching is sold only in full-time units. A thin part-time market, as in England and the Netherlands, makes hours both the reason for going and the obstacle to coming back, and there this category shades into the demand for command of one's own time. Leadership is what leavers name first, ahead of workload, in the one national survey of former teachers that asked (Table 2.1).

The economic motives weigh most where an alternative career is genuinely bidding. The American evidence is the most salary-responsive in the base, and financial incentives reliably retain serving teachers in the quasi-experimental record (Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026). Return rates run highest in subjects with the fewest opportunities elsewhere (OECD, 2005), and in Australia and England, where leavers demonstrably accept lower pay, the same dial turns little. A genuine split runs through this category. Asked directly, Michigan's certificate-holders chose a salary increase most often among incentives to return; watched rather than asked, leavers keep paying for conditions. The survey stands on a response rate below one in three and a menu dominated by financial items (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021), while the conditions reading stands on observed trajectories in three labour markets (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025; Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015; Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006). The honest use of the split is directional; post-exit earnings are a product registers already generate, so a ministry can establish which reading fits its own leavers before calibrating a programme to a misread preference. Pension design sits here because retirement only feels personal; pension wealth is legislated to peak and then fall, and the decision arrives with a birthday written by a fund (Table 2.1).

The life-cycle motives are transversal, and they define the three exit profiles of Section 1. Life-event leavers return when circumstances change rather than when advertising improves, and many in the classic cohort eventually did return (Stinebrickner, 2002); for them the price of return moves with childcare and the part-time market, and the salary scale is consulted last. Young explorers dominate the leaver pool in some systems and barely register in others (Table 2.1), so one country's reserve is deep in accumulated craft while another's is largely early-career. Retirees are priced by pension law, whatever they tell a surveyor about salary.

## 2.2. Where they go

**Linked records show most leavers staying close to education and keeping their qualifications.** United States administrative data that follow individual leavers find more than half still working in education broadly defined, with mean earnings slightly below pre-exit levels years later (Brummet et al., 2024). Over a quarter of recent Australian leavers are outside the labour force a year after exit, before a single retirement is counted (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The licence tends to survive the exit as well. Australia's national register carries a population of qualified teachers outside schools; asked, over half say they would consider returning under the right circumstances, and roughly one in twenty is actively looking (AITSL, 2025). A costless signal at unspecified terms estimates nothing about supply; the registrations themselves are the fact that matters, and they are the population Section 3's policies address.

One qualification is too important for a footnote. Panel estimates of this kind are computed on people still observable after the exit, so they are strongest as evidence about temporary leavers, those who kept a foot in the door. The limitation is an asset, because the segment for whom exit was explicitly temporary is precisely the segment that converts best when an offer arrives.

What that population holds is neither a grievance nor spare cash. It is employed and no richer than it was, most of it still close to education. It has time.

## 2.3. What they can bring back

**Returners supply hours on the terms of their original trade, and they carry a capability the system itself paid to build.** Wherever former teachers return in numbers, what they are being sold is hours, because an offer of days lets a returner keep the deal they made. The standing regulatory routes converge on this product from starting points that share nothing else. New Zealand's statutory on-ramp is twenty half-days of relief teaching a year without a current practising certificate (New Zealand Legislation, 2020), and Ontario lets a retired teacher work fifty days a year before pension consequences begin (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). Ireland reached the same product by suspending its substitute-work limit for teachers on career break (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). No preference study produced any of these rules. Each defines a small, divisible quantity of teaching, and that is the quantity the market clears; in the one Japanese prefecture studied in depth, retirees supply roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024), and at the turn of the century returners already supplied more than half of the part-time inflow into UK maintained schools (OECD, 2005).

The demand side matches with unusual precision. When registrants for England's returner pilot were segmented by motivation, the career breakers, the most return-committed group, returned at roughly double the rate of the other types, and what they wanted was part-time work, which schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). Interest was never the binding constraint in that market; the advertised part-time post was. The proposition has a boundary worth naming. No system examined here has ever offered a full-time post on genuinely changed conditions, so the revealed preference for small quantities is partly a preference expressed on the only menu ever printed. The one system that filled establishment posts with former teachers at scale did so by letting a full pension stack on a full salary, a repair priced in Section 3 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

What the hours can carry is more than cover. Teachers improve measurably when the quality of the colleagues around them rises, with the gains largest for the least experienced (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009). An experienced returner is therefore capability the system paid two decades to build, and placing them beside novices is the nearest thing a school system has to growing from within. The mechanism works through sustained proximity, and dose is what decides it; the largest randomised trial of comprehensive teacher induction found a positive achievement effect only in the third year, among teachers who had received two full years of support (Glazerman et al., 2010). The doses that matter in this literature run to a few dozen hours a year (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009), while the caps on retiree work run to hundreds, so a system that buys a real dose is buying well inside what its own rules already permit.

One deployment of this workforce has been shown to move pupil outcomes, and it was a defined job at a specified dose. A structured mathematics protocol for at-risk first-graders, taught three times a week in forty-minute small-group sessions across seventeen weeks, was delivered by more than eighty tutors recruited from local pools of retired and substitute teachers, and across nearly a thousand pupils in four urban districts it reproduced an effect first obtained with graduate students, at +0.34 standard deviations in early mathematics (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012; Fuchs et al., 2005). What the districts bought was fidelity to a protocol, and what they bought is exactly what the trial measured. The trial that specified nothing settled nothing. Retired master educators averaging two decades in high-need schools were randomly assigned as mentors to probationary teachers, in a trial that meets What Works Clearinghouse standards without reservations and whose effects are indeterminate in every reviewed domain (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; What Works Clearinghouse, 2019). How much mentoring the pairs did had been left largely to the pairs, and the one encouraging signal, exploratory and non-causal, concerns dose. The pair seems to hand down a verdict, that structure works and craft fails, and it will carry a narrower reading. An indeterminate rating under trial standards is a statement about evidential power, and the deployment someone bothered to define is also the only one anyone has managed to price.

Even the positive trial resists a clean attribution, since its tutors were retired and substitute teachers with no published split between the two, and the wider tutoring meta-analysis never isolates retired educators as a class (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024). No study in this evidence base connects a returner to the outcomes of a class or a colleague, and even the authors of the largest documented return episode state that they could not attempt the estimate (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Twenty years of craft may well deserve a premium; only a specified purchase has ever been priced; the distance between those two sentences measures effort, not value. The spillover evidence keeps the premium a live hypothesis rather than a sentiment, and a hypothesis that plausible earns the linkage study.

The specification is now in hand. Former teachers sold earnings for time and show every sign of considering the price fair, so the offer that respects the trade is denominated in hours, attached to an identified vacancy and, for the designed roles, delivered at a dose someone chose on purpose, with evaluation bought at the same moment. Section 3 reads the policy record against that specification, ordered by what each family verifiably moved.

# 3. The policies: what governments have tried and what it delivered

In 2015, England went looking for its former teachers. A national registration opened to any qualified teacher out of post, personal support and placements in partner schools stood behind it, and the whole apparatus pointed at the secondary subjects hardest to staff. The response was immediate; 5 729 people registered, close to double the target. Then the machinery began to grind. Of those thousands, 354 went on to receive the pilot's support, and by the workforce census of November 2016 that closed the account, 27 were teaching in the permanent shortage-subject posts the pilot existed to fill (Buchanan et al., 2018; Table 3.3).

The largest return of teachers in this record was never asked for. North Carolina ran no campaign and sent no advisers; in 1999 it changed a pension rule, so that a retired teacher could hold a full-time post at full salary without giving up the pension, and then let ordinary hiring do the rest. Roughly 3 500 retirees came back over the decade the rule stood, through the same vacancy lists as everyone else, and more than half the schools observed hired at least one (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; Box 3.1).

Dozens where governments advertised, thousands where a rule moved. That contrast is the international record in miniature, and the record is long. It opens with a statute of 1999 and closes with programmes still recruiting in the mid-2020s, and across those decades systems reached for their former teachers in the years when classrooms were going unstaffed. Campaigns and statutes alike were built, mostly and quite openly, to cover vacancies, a motive this paper takes at face value. The contrast also forces the question this section is built around. If interest was never the scarce resource, what does an invitation buy, and where is the price of accepting one actually written?

In country after country, efforts have been made to re-attract former and retired teachers to the classroom, seeking two things above all, cover for lessons that would otherwise go untaught and the recovery of experience the system itself once paid to build. The ambition is canonical; the OECD recommended maintaining contact with former teachers and flexible re-entry pathways two decades ago (OECD, 2005) and today lists support for re-entry among the standing levers of teacher supply (OECD, 2024). To that end, several routes have been enabled, and they sort into five recognisable families of instrument. Family A rewrites the pension and employment rules that set the price of a return. Family B repairs the certificate, so that a licence lapsed in a drawer stops being a barrier. Family C attaches money to the return, paid to the school, to the returner or against the costs of a named posting. Family D goes out and asks, with campaigns, letters from the employer's own records and advisers. Family E buys a defined role, a specified job rather than generic cover. The letters are the ones the motives table of Section 2 points at (Table 2.1), and the families appear here in order of the scale of return each can document, because that hierarchy is itself the section's principal finding.

**Where the state's own rules lowered the price of a return, teachers came back in the thousands; where governments advertised over an unchanged price, they came back by the dozen, and only the family that defined the role ever measured what students received.** Table 3.1 is the map of the terrain this section covers, family by family, with the beneficiary each family principally serves, its flagship scheme and the headline lesson its record supports. ## 3.1. Family A. Pension and employment rules set the price of a return

**Statutory relief on pension and salary is the family that has produced returns in the thousands, and its instruments are written by legislatures and pension boards rather than by education ministries.** What governments sought from this family was rarely framed as recruitment at all. Its route is permission, a law letting a retired teacher hold a paid post without forfeiting the pension, on conditions the statute sets, with returns travelling through ordinary hiring. No adviser, registration drive or marketing accompanies it; the instrument is the rule itself, and the rule decides what a return costs the person making it.

North Carolina's window, already met above, remains the family's landmark, and its returners averaged more than thirty years of experience (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Box 3.1 traces the design, its reach into high-need schools and the questions it left unanswered.

**Box 3.1. Removing the pension-salary trade-off entirely: North Carolina's return-to-work window, 1999-2009**

For a decade, one American state suspended the ordinary trade-off between a teacher's pension and a teacher's salary. Before 1999, a retired teacher in North Carolina who returned full-time forfeited pension and retiree health benefits, and part-time work was capped at earnings of half the previous salary. The return-to-work window lifted the cap, allowing a retiree to hold a full-time post at full salary while drawing the full pension, an income on the order of half as much again as a regular full-time teacher's. In its first year the window was confined to low-performing schools with a certification match, as interim instructors or substitutes; those restrictions were lifted in June 2000, after which no targeting mandate applied.

About 3 500 teachers returned under the window, some 2.1% of the roughly 169 000 teachers observed, and over 1 500 schools, more than half the sample, hired at least one. By 2009, just over 35% of teachers who had been retirement-eligible in the previous year were returning to full-time work. Returning teachers averaged 31 years of experience, were more likely than other teachers to be Black and to teach mathematics, and worked in schools with 3.71 percentage points more economically disadvantaged and 4.86 percentage points more Black students than other teachers' schools; 30% taught in the most disadvantaged quartile of schools against 21% in the least. The gradient arose without steering, though 54% returned to the school they had left, so part of it reflects where those teachers had always worked.

The study's cost framing matters for any imitator: the pension annuity is paid whether or not the retiree returns, so the marginal cost of the rehire is the salary difference against a novice, less the novice's benefits. What the authors could not do is also instructive. With no vacancy data and no counterfactual, no effect on student outcomes or on the shortage itself could be estimated, and the window expired in autumn 2009 with the question unanswered.

Source: Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024).

The mechanism separating this setting from the family's smaller yields is not mysterious. Teachers have been shown to value current cash at roughly five times deferred pension wealth (Fitzpatrick, 2015), so an offer that protects the pension while resetting current pay to the entry step withholds exactly the currency returners respond to, and preserves the one they discount. Portugal wrote that low setting deliberately, and Section 3.3 tells what it bought, 63 returners against a quota of 200. In England the dial is moving with nobody's hand on it, since abatement attaches only to final-salary retirement benefits and will dissolve of its own accord as career-average membership matures (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.).

Few governments have ever set the dial on purpose, and Austria shows how it gets set instead. Consider two Austrian teachers with identical classroom careers, both retired, both invited back. One is offered the profession's starting salary on top of a full pension. The other broadly recovers pre-retirement pay. Nothing in their teaching separates them; what separates them is an employment status chosen decades earlier, because federal law excludes from prior-service crediting any period that already generates a pension, so a teacher who retired as a civil servant returns on full pension plus the starting salary while a former contract teacher, whose service is credited, comes back broadly at pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023). Two identical careers attract offers most of a salary scale apart, on the strength of a crediting rule written for the whole federal service that happens to fall on teachers too. Nobody designed it as re-entry policy. That is precisely the point.

The Austrian accident is one setting of one dial, and four dials together price a return in any system. The pay dial fixes the step a returner lands on and whether pension and salary may be drawn together. The exclusion dial decides who may return at all, through service thresholds, waiting periods and subject or region screens. The intensity dial caps how much teaching may be sold before the pension reprices. The licence dial governs lapse, refresh and waiver, and it is a family of policy in its own right, taken up in Section 3.2. Table 3.2 sets out where each dial sits in thirteen systems, and its most striking regularity is jurisdictional; almost none of these settings lives in education law.

Three cases carry the pay dial, and honesty requires saying what they cannot carry. They differ in pension regime, era, product and age profile as well as in the step a returner lands on, and nowhere has the step been varied while anything else was held constant. The discriminating test exists and is cheap. The Austrian audit could disaggregate its returners by former employment status, comparing the cohort forced onto the starting salary with the cohort whose service was credited. Even that comparison would price the pay rule together with everything else that distinguishes a career civil servant from a contract teacher, so a null result would be more informative than a positive one.

The exclusion dial belongs to Georgia, the one state that audited its own scheme and found its own rules blocking the hires it wanted. Eligibility conditions are additive in law and multiplicative in effect; a service threshold, a mandatory break and a subject-and-region test must each be cleared, each is set independently of the others, and the population that survives all three can be a small fraction of the population any one of them would pass. Georgia's rehire statute of 2022 stacked a 30-year service requirement, a one-year break and a centrally determined list of the three highest-need subjects per region, and full-time rehires stayed below one per cent of the workforce while almost half (45%) of surveyed school systems reported at least one hire the statutory provisions had blocked (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). The audit is also the record's only employer-side counterfactual, and what employers named as the obstacle was not money. The retirees they did rehire earned a veteran wage the systems were evidently willing to pay (Box 3.2); what stopped further hires were the service rule, the waiting year and the subject methodology (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). The pay dial and the exclusion dial are therefore genuinely distinct charges, and a system can set one generously while the other quietly does the rationing. Georgia has since renewed its rehire law with the audit on the table, extending the programme to the end of the decade, keeping the 30-year rule and devolving the high-need determination to school systems (Teachers Retirement System of Georgia, 2026). The screen that was relaxed is the locally held one; the screen that stayed is the centrally set one. The take-up series that follows will be the cleanest natural test of the exclusion dial the field is likely to get.

Waiting periods, the most widespread exclusion, sit in pension-integrity law rather than in education policy. The French six-month delay before a retired public servant may resume work with the last employer exists to stop employers re-hiring their own just-retired staff more cheaply (Assemblée nationale, 2020). The American waits, ninety days in New Mexico, six months in Michigan and Virginia after earlier longer periods, a year in Louisiana and Georgia, descend from tax-law tests of bona fide retirement (New Mexico Legislature, 2022; Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2023; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024). Each guards a pension system's integrity. Each is also, incidentally, a charge on teacher re-entry levied by a body with no teacher-supply mandate.

New Mexico layered routes instead of choosing one. Its educational retirees may work at a quarter of a post or less, or below an earnings ceiling, or, on the newest route, with no limits on salary or hours for a fixed span of months, subject to a 90-day layout, a ban on pre-arranged returns and pension-board approval, and the span was lengthened and the earnings ceiling raised in 2025 (New Mexico Legislature, 2022; New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025). The pension board's registry counted 2 792 working retirees across all routes in the newest route's first year, only 154 of them on the flagship no-limits route (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023; Table 3.3).

Michigan simplified. An act of 2022 swept away a patchwork of critical-shortage lists and replaced it with two tests, a bona fide termination and a waiting period, after which a retiree may take any school post with no earnings limit and no loss of pension or insurance subsidy; a further act of 2023 cut the wait from nine months to six and allowed capped earnings during it, with the loosening sunset in 2028 (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022; 2023). Virginia has run pension plus salary in critical-shortage positions since 2001; an executive directive of 2022 added fast-tracked renewal of lapsed licences and instructed that vacant teaching posts be classified so that retirees could fill them, and legislation of 2023 halved the required break in service to six months while extending the provisions to 2028 (Will, 2022; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Virginia Retirement System, n.d.). Louisiana attaches the relief to a certified shortage, a post qualifying when advertisement yields fewer than three certified applicants; an act of 2024 opened the route to all retirees after a twelve-month wait, and the consolidated law of 2026 added a route free of pension impact from age 65, raised the outside earnings cap and let general certification suffice, ordering an actuarial report before 2034 (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024; 2026). Neither state publishes a take-up count.

The intensity dial is the one dial anyone has openly fought over, and the fight shows who owns it. Ontario built the family as a valve rather than a window, a standing cap on the days a retired teacher may teach before the pension reprices. The cap has been nearly quintupled in a shortage, roughly halved after a newspaper investigation documented heavy board spending on retired supply teachers at nearly twice the new-graduate day rate while newly certified teachers went short of assignments, restored for the pandemic years, and cut back when the pandemic agreement expired (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026; Table 3.2). Then, in March 2024, the teachers' federation that co-sponsors the pension plan refused a further extension, in the record's plainest statement of where this dial lives: "the Pension Plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers; it is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (OCEOTA, 2024; Alphonso, 2024). The cap moves only by agreement of the plan's co-sponsors, one of which answers to serving and newly certified teachers as well as to pensioners.

What moving the cap achieved is, strikingly, unknown. No post-liberalisation take-up figure for the pandemic increase was ever published, and the number most often quoted against permissiveness is a union estimate of member interest released on the day the change was announced, which measures appetite for a forty-five-day increment rather than appetite for returning (Global News, 2022); this paper declines to borrow it. The fuller lesson comes from the system that removed intensity limits altogether. When New Mexico opened its route with no salary or hours caps, the flagship route drew barely a hundred and fifty users in its first year, within a total of fewer than three thousand retirees working across all four routes, against tens of thousands potentially eligible (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023; Table 3.3). Permission is not price. Liberalising a cap moves nobody by itself, and unprogrammed re-employment under long-standing rules dwarfs the flagship route built to showcase the liberalisation.

France, finally, enables without programming at all. Under its general framework for combining pension and earnings, the education authorities rehire retired teachers on contract for replacements and shortage subjects, behind a six-month delay before any return to the last employer, and since 2023 the resumed work accrues new pension rights (Assemblée nationale, 2020; 2023). A parliamentary question asking which obstacles should be lifted so that willing retirees could cover absences lapsed unanswered at the end of the legislature, and no aggregate count of returning retirees has ever been published (Assemblée nationale, 2024). Ireland, for its part, activates the employment rules of its career-break scheme, suspending the scheme's substitute-work limits so that a teacher on career break may substitute without restriction, at regular substitute rates and with increments accruing under the general rules (Department of Education, 2019; Department of Education and Youth, 2025). The suspension has been renewed annually for several successive school years, and several of the American instruments above carry sunsets, which poses a design choice the record leaves open. An instrument justified as barrier removal should be permanent and evaluated as regulation, while one justified as crisis cover should be explicitly sunset and counted. What no system should do is run a crisis instrument on annual renewal for five years while describing it as structural reform.

Read down the columns of Table 3.2 and a single finding emerges. Every dial that has demonstrably moved re-entry volume in this record sits outside the education ministry, the crediting rule in civil-service and pension law, pension-salary concurrency in state retirement statutes, the day cap with a pension plan's co-sponsors, the waiting period in social-security and tax law. A re-entry strategy written wholly inside an education ministry is a strategy over the weakest instruments in the system, which is much of why the campaigns of Family D moved so little while costing so little. What its waits, thresholds and screens jointly amount to is a price, and what a ministry can do about a price it does not own is the first lesson of Section 3.6.

## 3.2. Family B. Certification and licensing routes reopen the door cheaply

**Repairing the certificate is the cheapest re-entry policy a government can buy, and the least measured once bought.** What governments sought from this family follows from a fact established in Section 1, that licences survive the exit; the barrier is register-visible, since close to half of the certified non-teachers in the register examined there held expired certificates (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). A former teacher whose paperwork has lapsed faces a fee, a refresher course or a processing queue before any school can employ them, and every element of that barrier is owned by the education authorities themselves. The routes enabled are accordingly direct, waivers, fast tracks and fee relief, and no legislature or pension board needs to be persuaded first.

New Zealand keeps the lightest design in permanent law. A registered teacher whose practising certificate has expired may relief-teach up to 20 half-days a year without renewing it, the refresh requirement bites mainly when a returner lacks a job offer, and the government currently pays practising-certificate fees on teachers' behalf (New Zealand Legislation, 2020; Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026; New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026). The design's logic rewards attention. By tying the refresh requirement to the absence of a job offer, the rule ensures that a returner a school actually wants is never delayed by it, and the burden falls only where no employer is yet waiting.

Others have moved the same dial by decree or by folding. Virginia fast-tracked the renewal of lapsed licences by executive directive in 2022, alongside the pension provisions described in Section 3.1 (Will, 2022). Louisiana's consolidated law of 2026 let general certification suffice for its return-to-work routes, folding the licensing question into the pension statute itself (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). Michigan's simplification of its working-after-retirement rules removed certification-linked shortage lists along the way (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022). And the outreach drive in New South Wales, described in Section 3.4, bundled accreditation, approval to teach and background checks into a single conversation, treating the paperwork as something the employer clears rather than something the returner navigates (NSW Government, 2024).

The family's learning is an absence. No jurisdiction publishes take-up for a licence waiver, so the dial most often recommended in policy commentary is the one whose effect the record cannot yet show, and claims for it belong in the register of promise rather than of evidence. The designs are sensible, the marginal cost is close to zero, and the counting duty of Section 3.6 is nowhere cheaper to attach than here.

## 3.3. Family C. Money attached to the return

**Money has been attached to the return in three different places, to the receiving school, to the returner's own pay and to the costs of a named posting, and what separates the designs is the stage of the return the money actually touches.** Governments reached for this family when they wanted movement they could budget for, a subsidy line being easier to create than a statute is to amend, and the routes enabled were grants to employers, supplements to returners and allowances against the costs of serving where the need is greatest. Its documented yields run to the hundreds at most, and its most instructive episodes are underspends.

The Netherlands paid the school. Between 2017 and 2020 the government paid primary school boards EUR 2 500 for each qualified returner appointed for at least six months after at least twelve months out of teaching, the money covering the board's induction and guidance costs rather than pay (Ministerie van OCW, 2017). The budget provided for 500 returners against a silent reserve estimated at 31 000 in the primary sector alone. Just over 100 grants were paid in the first full year and nearly 200 cumulatively by late 2019, the annual ceilings were lowered on extension precisely because the budget was not being exhausted, and the scheme lapsed without a published evaluation (Ministerie van OCW, 2019a; 2019b; Tweede Kamer, 2019). Successor regional schemes made activation of the silent reserve a fundable goal behind a mandatory regional information desk; their evaluation found tailored support to returners among the least realised activities, and several regions reported more applications from side-entrants and returners than schools could place, a self-reported finding that supports the direction of an absorption constraint rather than its magnitude (De Jong et al., 2022). An instrument that pays for a stage downstream of the binding one is deadweight by construction, and this one paid for induction, the stage after the constraint.

Portugal paid the returner, at the price of a novice. A decree of August 2024 created an exceptional route under which a teacher retired five years or less may return on a fixed-term contract in shortage subjects or understaffed schools, keeping the full pension, with additional pay indexed to the first step of the career salary scale and pro-rated by teaching hours, under an annual quota fixed by the government (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Against a quota of 200, some 80 applied and 63 were accepted, most commonly in Portuguese, history and special education (Diário de Notícias, 2024; Renascença, 2024). Those who came spoke of love of teaching, of recovering frozen service time and of thin pensions (Diário de Notícias, 2024); the largest union's leader called the idea that the retired would return "an illusion" (SÁBADO, 2024). The reading cannot be entirely clean, because the application window lasted twelve days, placements began in January and the applicants averaged about 68 years of age, so process and demography share the explanation with pay. What can be said is that the offer asked veterans to return at the price of novices, and very few did. The same decree carries a second instrument, a monthly supplement of EUR 750 for teachers who reach pensionable age and stay on, and some 2 200 teachers used it in 2025/26 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 6; RTP, 2026). The two articles form the record's cleanest paired reading of where money moves teachers, and the answer is before the exit rather than after it.

Germany pays supplements on top of a return the Länder actively solicit. Thirteen of the sixteen Länder recruit pensioned teachers on offers of flexible hours, lifted earnings limits and salary supplements of around 10% (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Hesse, the documented extreme, wrote to some 7 000 retired teachers in a school year and attributes about 550 filled posts to its whole short-term package of retiree recruitment, service extensions and part-time top-ups, not to the letters alone (Hessisches Ministerium für Kultus, Bildung und Chancen, 2024); nationwide, just over 5 000 pensioners were teaching, under 1% of the workforce (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). The supplements are the offer; the letters that carry it belong with the outreach of Section 3.4.

China paid the posting. The silver-age schemes recruit retired principals, teaching-research staff and special-grade and senior teachers, generally aged 65 or under, for at least a school year in named categories of county, counties recently lifted out of poverty, ethnic-minority counties and border counties among them (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). The recruit keeps the full pension and receives an annual allowance of CNY 20 000, funded on a gradient that charges the central budget most where the counties are poorest, with housing, a return journey each semester and insurance supplied in kind (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). The role is wider than a timetable, since demonstration lessons, service as vice-principals and the mentoring of young local teachers are written into it, a defined-role dimension Section 3.5 returns to (MOE et al., 2023; State Council, 2023). The predecessor schemes report more than 20 000 recruits by mid-2023, and the current plan aims at a corps of some 120 000 across five education strands, though the counts are self-reported and no independent audit exists (State Council, 2023; MOE et al., 2023).

The family's learning sits in the contrast between its designs. An allowance that compensates the itemisable costs of a named posting is a different purchase from money offered flat against a revealed preference for staying away, and Section 2 set out why the preference usually wins. What almost nothing in this family offered was a change in the price of return itself, which stayed wherever the rules of Family A had set it.

## 3.4. Family D. Active recruitment and accompaniment

**Campaigns of re-attraction and direct outreach treat a return as a conversion problem, walking the interested from contact to contract, and wherever a funnel was published the losses happened in operations rather than in interest.** What governments sought from this family was speed and visibility, an instrument that could be launched in a school year and photographed. The routes enabled run from national registration with funded refresher training, through letters and calls from the employer's own personnel records, to standing adviser services that cost little and outlast the drives.

England has run the family's fullest experiment. The pilot of 2015 advertised nationally, registered the interested and funded the stage between interest and employment through partnerships of schools that had under-recruited in the target subjects, with subject-knowledge and refresher training, classroom experience and mentoring, free to the participant and with no payment to the returner (Buchanan et al., 2018). It is the one campaign in this record with a complete, independent account of itself. The evaluation found interest abundant at every stage and traced the collapse between registration and placement to operations, above all to the timing of support against the hiring cycle and to the scarcity of practical teaching opportunities in partner schools (Buchanan et al., 2018). What the programme could not manufacture was a vacancy at the moment the returner was ready. The successor programme drew the plain conclusion and tied every funded place to a school with an identified vacancy (NCTL, 2017), which remains the most direct fix in the record for the most commonly documented failure.

The campaign also taught the department who its returners are; the most committed of them, teachers on a career break, returned at double the rate of the rest and asked above all for the part-time posts that schools rarely advertise (Worth and Sharp, 2018). England then kept the cheapest layer of the apparatus. A standing adviser service gives any former teacher a named adviser, free, for application preparation and signposting to courses and vacancies; confined at first to mathematics, physics and modern foreign languages, it was later extended to every secondary subject and then to primary teaching (UK Parliament, 2022; Department for Education, 2023). In its two subject-restricted years the service recorded around 130 returns from more than 600 people advised (UK Parliament, 2022; Walker, 2022). It has never been independently evaluated; the department's own reporting has since folded into a statement that more teachers are returning than at any point in a decade (UK Parliament, 2026).

Direct outreach from the employer's own records is the variant that asks least of the returner, and it has run at scale in Austria. The route is a letter, or a call, from the system that already knows who left, with the paperwork of a return bundled behind it. Austria's regional education directorates turned to it as the retirement wave crested. Vienna wrote to 300 already retired teachers and instructed school principals to raise the question with staff approaching retirement; Styria encouraged the same conversations; Salzburg contacted every retired teacher up to age 67, offering public-transport tickets and higher premiums; Vorarlberg opened a dedicated service point, which now sends information on working in retirement out together with the retirement paperwork itself (APA/ORF, 2023). The one qualitative study of the people addressed suggests why such contact is worth the postage, post-retirement decisions to return are biographical, and staying in contact with the former school is among the things that tip them (Resch, 2025). Across six school years the country's Court of Audit counted 1 062 retired teachers who resumed teaching, in a workforce of roughly 123 000, with intakes that peaked in the pandemic years and then fell (Rechnungshof, 2025). Most German Länder work the same seam, with the letters carrying the offers of money and hours described in Section 3.3 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).

The cheapest documented variant ran in New South Wales. From November 2023 the education department contacted more than 1 500 teachers who had resigned from the public system in the previous five years, bundling accreditation, approval to teach and background checks into a single conversation; more than 260 expressed interest, and 145 were placed in casual and temporary work spread across more than 600 schools, about 60% of them returning from retirement (NSW Government, 2024). The letters went out weeks after a major pay settlement, and the department's standing page for returners still leads with the pay rise (NSW Department of Education, 2026). Outreach of this kind is a channel, transmitting a changed offer, rather than an instrument that creates one; it earns its place on cheapness, and its funnel was published once, in May 2024, then vanished from the following annual report (NSW Department of Education, 2025).

The family's learning is the one its fullest experiment wrote. Interest was never the scarce resource; conversion was, and conversion fails on the vacancy, its hours and its timing. A drive that cannot put an identified post with the right hours in front of a willing returner is advertising a product that is out of stock, and the corrections that worked, England's vacancy tie and the waiver New Zealand attaches to a job offer, both put the vacancy back at the centre of the transaction.

## 3.5. Family E. Return as a defined role

**A defined role is the only product in this record whose effect on students was ever measured, and the governments that obtained that measurement specified the job before they paid for it.** What systems sought from this family was never generic cover. They wanted a named product, tutoring for pupils at risk, mentoring for novice teachers, demonstration teaching for a rural school, and they recruited former teachers because the role demanded classroom fluency without consuming a place in initial teacher education. The routes enabled range from one-off procured programmes to a standing place in the staffing architecture.

The flagship is the tutoring deployment. Four American urban districts bought a structured early-mathematics protocol for first-graders at risk and staffed it from local pools of retired and substitute teachers, with the role, the group size and the dose fixed in advance; what the districts bought was fidelity to a protocol rather than professional judgement (Gersten et al., 2015). As Section 2 showed, the deployment reproduced at scale an effect first obtained with graduate students, and it remains the only engagement of former teachers ever measured against a comparison group. The contrast case bought craft instead. The mentoring scheme in Aurora, Colorado recruited recently retired master educators, screened by interview for a record of excellence and deliberately kept outside the evaluation hierarchy of the novice teachers they mentored, and attached a randomised trial to the purchase (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). As Section 2 set out, the trial was too small to settle what the craft adds, and how much mentoring the pairs actually did had been left largely to the pairs. The pairing of the two schemes is the family's charter. Specification turns an announcement into a purchase, and only a specified purchase has ever been priced.

Japan runs the family's fullest standing expression, since re-employment after mandatory retirement is the default staffing route there. Boards of education conventionally re-employ retirees until pension age, and in the prefecture studied in depth retirees supply roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024; see Section 2). The same study marks the ceiling of any retiree-based channel. Only about 35% of age-based elementary retirees there now seek full-time re-employment, every retiree pool ages on its own schedule, and this paper does not attribute to design what demography may explain.

China's silver-age postings, financed as Section 3.3 described, belong partly to this family too, because the role is written into the recruitment. Demonstration lessons, service as vice-principals and the mentoring of young local teachers are part of the job description rather than a hope attached to it (MOE et al., 2023; State Council, 2023). What is missing there is the other half of the charter, since no independent evaluation of the product exists.

The Bahamas, most recently, has institutionalised the reserve by name, announcing in mid-2026 a Teacher Reservist Program, a standing register of retired educators, trained teachers who built careers elsewhere and recent graduates that schools can call on, run on the platform of the existing supply-teacher programme, with a multi-agency task force to fast-track vetting and credential assessment as part of a drive to recruit up to 300 teachers (Cooper, 2026; ZNS Bahamas, 2026). It is announcement-stage and earns its place on design rather than results.

The family's learning is an instruction for buyers. A government that wants tutoring, induction support or mentoring from its former teachers can specify the role and the dose the way the tutoring districts did, and can make the evaluation a term of the purchase rather than a commission raised after the money is spent. Buying seniority and hoping is what the record mostly shows, and Section 3.6 turns the alternative into an operational rule.

## 3.6. What the record teaches

**The five families delivered what they were designed to touch, a contact, a stage of conversion, occasionally a destination, and their volumes stayed inside a narrow band unless something institutional moved behind them.** Where the band broke, in North Carolina's window and in Japan's standing re-employment, the thing that moved was a rule, owned far from any programme office. Judged as the vacancy cover they were mostly bought for, even the successes stayed modest. The mass return's effect on the shortage went unmeasured (Box 3.1), and the one audited scheme was judged to have touched its own minimally (Box 3.2); re-entry alone closes no shortage in this record. What repairing the price demonstrably delivered, in the thousands, was experienced teachers.

Across the families, the arithmetic is strikingly uniform. Wherever a full account of a campaign or an outreach drive exists, the instrument moved a fraction of one per cent of its eligible pool in a year, and near-universal outreach stayed inside the band. The band looks narrower still against the flow that runs with no campaign at all. Roughly 17 000 teachers return to English state schools in an ordinary year, about a third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023), and re-entrants have made up between roughly a third and a half of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). Return is a standing channel, produced by standing rules and ordinary hiring; a programme does not create the channel, it bids for a margin on top of it. Read against that ledger, the record's lessons are operational, and they run from strong evidence to thin.

**Total the bill your own state presents to a returner, and carry it to the departments that wrote it.** What a system charges a returner is a sum its own rules fix, and the salary step a returner is placed on is the dial that most plausibly separates the record's one mass return from its many token ones (Section 3.1). The difficulty is ownership. Most of the rules that set the price sit in pension and civil-service law, drafted by departments that never answer for teacher supply, so an education ministry can total the bill and publish it but usually cannot change it alone. That is an argument for the audit, not against it. The charges a state levies on its own former teachers are a finite list; it can be totalled, published and taken to the desks where the strongest dials are kept, beginning with the salary step. No system examined in this paper has published such an audit. Any of them could, in weeks rather than years.

**Publish an honest count of the people you could recall.** Every register examined in Section 1 already holds the fields such a count requires. Each deduction moves the figure downward, so a netted series is an honest upper bound on the recallable population, while a headline total describes the rule of the register that produced it. The OECD has observed that missing data on potential returners may limit systems' ability to use this channel strategically (OECD, 2024), and the remedy is administrative rather than scientific. A ministry that keeps quoting an unnetted figure is describing its register, and should say so.

**Sell the hours returners want to buy, and tie every funded offer to a named vacancy.** Former teachers bought shorter hours and more control when they left, and the segment most committed to returning wants the part-time posts schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). The repair is a retail one. Put the hours in the advertisement. Release conversion support only against an identified vacancy, the correction England's successor programme made after its pilot (NCTL, 2017). Spare returners who already hold a job offer any refresh requirement, the design New Zealand reached independently (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026). The scarce product has been identified for years. Stocking it costs the price of an advertisement.

**Buy defined work at a specified dose, and write the evaluation into the contract of purchase.** The one deployment of former teachers that stated its protocol and its dose was measured at scale and worked; the role left to the practitioner's discretion was measured once, in a small trial that set no dose, and settled nothing (Gersten et al., 2015; DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; Section 3.5). A government that wants tutoring, induction support or mentoring from its former teachers can specify the role and the dose the way the tutoring trials did, and can make the evaluation a term of the purchase rather than a commission raised after the money is spent. Buying seniority and hoping is what the record mostly shows. It is also why the next review of this field would otherwise be written on the same absence of evidence as this one.

**Report destination and survival, never placements alone, and price the programme on the years it kept.** A placement is the friendliest number a programme owns, and the least informative, because the goods being counted range from a casual day to a permanent post. Beyond volume, a programme can deliver a destination, and the record here is thin but consistent. Where the pension penalty was removed with no targeting mandate, returners went disproportionately to schools with more disadvantaged intakes, though the gradient was modest and more than half returned to the school they had left, so part of it is inherited attachment rather than steered movement (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; Box 3.1). Targeting by condition attaches money to the destination itself, as the Chinese county conditions do; targeting by narrowing came later elsewhere, as when the Portuguese government restricted its stay-on supplement to shortage zones, conceding that the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026); targeting by prohibition produced the record's one audited failure (Box 3.2). The last deliverable is survival, and only one system can currently report it. Linked English data show 32% of one returner cohort out of service again four years later, with a further 2% retired, against 29% of the same year's newly qualified cohort, and the odds of a returner leaving after the first year back run 60% above average (Buchanan et al., 2018). The gap is small and the cost ranges overlap, so the finding does not price returners against novices. What it settles is the denominator. A hire that does not survive its first year buys very little, and the honest unit of account for any programme in Table 3.3 is cost per retained teacher-year, at one and three years, rather than cost per placement.

**Know when the right expenditure is zero, or negative.** The record identifies three conditions under which spending nothing, or withdrawing an instrument, is the defensible budget. The first is a loose entry pipeline. Where newly qualified teachers outnumber the openings available to them, premium-priced re-employment of retirees operates as a transfer from new entrants to pensioners, and the harm lands in the casual market where new careers begin. That is the configuration reporters found in Ontario before the rule was tightened, with the largest school boards spending over a hundred million Canadian dollars a year on retired supply teachers at close to twice the new-graduate day rate, in years when newly certified teachers outnumbered annual openings roughly two to one (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Section 3.1). Commentary on the Chinese programmes points the same way, suggesting that some localities use retiree recruitment to defer creating formal posts, although this is secondary reporting rather than an official series (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025), and in the Bahamas the teachers' union reported retired educators already working full-time in supply roles before the reservist scheme gave the practice a name (Munnings, 2026). The second is an absorption constraint. Where schools cannot place the returners they already attract, further recruitment spending buys a queue, the configuration the Dutch regional partnerships reported in the same window in which the national returner subsidy went underspent (De Jong et al., 2022; Ministerie van OCW, 2019a); the finding establishes the constraint's direction rather than its size, and the direction alone is enough to move money from recruitment towards part-time posts, timetabling and hiring capacity. The third is a population consisting only of retirees. A retiree pool is old and self-limiting; in the system where re-employment is the default staffing route, only about a third of age-based leavers from primary schools now seek full-time re-employment, and the one audited national intake peaked and fell within its own audit window (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024; Rechnungshof, 2025). Part of what reads as programme failure in this record is demography. None of the three conditions is permanent, and each is observable in data a ministry already holds. Zero is a budget line to be revisited, never a verdict on the population.

**Write the duty to count into the instrument itself, lodge it with a body that does not answer to the sponsor, and match it with a sunset that falls while the count can still change the decision.** Wherever a re-entry measure produced recurring numbers, somebody outside the sponsoring ministry held a standing duty to count; wherever nobody did, no numbers exist, however loudly the measure was announced. Evidence in this field is created at the drafting stage or not at all. Three instruments in this record produce audited or recurring numbers, and in each the obligation is owed elsewhere, to a state auditor mandated in the statute, to a national court of audit, to a legislative oversight committee. Instruments of the same family in several other systems sit inside pension administrations that hold exactly the same records and publish nothing (Table 3.4). What predicts being counted is an independent duty; riding a register, which every one of these instruments does, predicts nothing.

The consequence is a selection effect that deserves to be stated as a finding about the literature. The most rigorous products in this record, an independent programme evaluation, a mandated state audit and a court of audit report, all return negative or modest verdicts, while the measures that report success publish no evaluation at all. A synthesis of published evidence in this field is therefore biased downward, a synthesis of published claims biased upward, and nothing in the record permits a correction; part of the scepticism re-entry attracts is an artefact of who was obliged to count. The full loop of sunset, mandated count, arms-length audit and informed renewal has closed exactly once in this record, in the statute that wrote its own audit (Box 3.2). The place the duty is needed next is the entry-level supply market, because that is where any harm would show, and the one wind-back in the record followed a newspaper investigation rather than an official series (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). Education systems should therefore publish new-graduate hiring rates for their casual and supply pools alongside retiree re-employment counts, and treat movement in the first as the trigger for reviewing the second.

Former teachers will keep returning through whatever channel exists, because unprogrammed return is already a routine part of every measured inflow. What a government decides is the deal that meets them, and that deal is written in its own law, administered by its own registers and priced, so far, by nobody. What the record asks of a ministry is modest. Total the bill and take it to its authors. Publish the honest count. Advertise the hours. Buy defined work and evaluate it at purchase. Follow the people, and be willing to spend nothing. None of this is a campaign, and almost all of it is cheap. The prize is larger than a staffed classroom, because experienced returners raise the quality of the teaching around them, as Section 2 showed, so a repaired deal recovers capability the system has already paid to build. The state wrote the terms on which its teachers left and the price they face on the way back. The deal is its own to repair.
