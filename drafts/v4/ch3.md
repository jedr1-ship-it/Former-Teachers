# 3. Re-entry policy in practice: international experience

In 2015, England went looking for its former teachers. A national registration opened to any qualified teacher out of post, personal support and placements in partner schools stood behind it, and the whole apparatus pointed at the secondary subjects hardest to staff. The response was immediate; 5 729 people registered, close to double the target. Then the machinery began to grind. Of those thousands, 354 went on to receive the pilot's support, and by the workforce census of November 2016 that closed the account, 27 were teaching in the permanent shortage-subject posts the pilot existed to fill (Buchanan et al., 2018; Table 3.1).

The largest return of teachers in this record was never asked for. North Carolina ran no campaign and sent no advisers; in 1999 it changed a pension rule, so that a retired teacher could hold a full-time post at full salary without giving up the pension, and then let ordinary hiring do the rest. Roughly 3 500 retirees came back over the decade the rule stood, through the same vacancy lists as everyone else, and more than half the schools observed hired at least one (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; Box 3.1).

Dozens where governments advertised, thousands where a rule moved. That contrast is the international record in miniature, and the record is long. It opens with a statute of 1999 and closes with programmes still recruiting in the mid-2020s, and across those decades systems reached for their former teachers in the years when classrooms were going unstaffed. Campaigns and statutes alike were built, mostly and quite openly, to cover vacancies, a motive this paper takes at face value. The contrast also forces the question this chapter is built around. If interest was never the scarce resource, what does an invitation buy, and where is the price of accepting one actually written? The record of what governments have actually built, mapped below family by family, answers the first question. The ledger of rules answers the second, and almost none of those rules turns out to be written inside an education ministry, or written on purpose.

## 3.1. The record of attempts: five families of re-entry policy

**Governments have pursued their former teachers through five recognisable families of policy, and every family found willing returners; the family that changed the price of a return found them in the thousands.**

In country after country, efforts have been made to re-attract former and retired teachers to the classroom, seeking two things above all, cover for lessons that would otherwise go untaught and the recovery of experience the system itself once paid to build. The ambition is canonical; the OECD recommended maintaining contact with former teachers and flexible re-entry pathways two decades ago (OECD, 2005) and today lists support for re-entry among the standing levers of teacher supply (OECD, 2024). To that end, several routes have been enabled. Some governments built campaigns, with national registration, advisers and funded refresher training. Some wrote directly to the people in their own personnel records. Some attached money to the return, paid to the returner, to the receiving school or to the costs of a named posting. Some rewrote the pension and employment rules that set the price of a return. And some built standing on-ramps designed to outlast any single drive. Table 3.1 holds the published take-up counts and Table 3.2 the rule settings; the prose carries each scheme's story, the tables its arithmetic.

**Campaigns of re-attraction with funded support treat a return as a conversion problem, and England has run the family's fullest experiment.** The pilot advertised nationally, registered the interested and funded the stage between interest and employment through partnerships of schools that had under-recruited in the target subjects, with subject-knowledge and refresher training, classroom experience and mentoring, free to the participant and with no payment to the returner (Buchanan et al., 2018). It is the one campaign in this record with a complete, independent account of itself. The evaluation found interest abundant at every stage and traced the collapse between registration and placement to operations, above all to the timing of support against the hiring cycle and to the scarcity of practical teaching opportunities in partner schools (Buchanan et al., 2018). What the programme could not manufacture was a vacancy at the moment the returner was ready. The successor programme drew the plain conclusion and tied every funded place to a school with an identified vacancy (NCTL, 2017), which remains the most direct fix in the record for the most commonly documented failure.

The campaign also taught the department who its returners are; the most committed of them, teachers on a career break, returned at double the rate of the rest and asked above all for the part-time posts that schools rarely advertise (Worth and Sharp, 2018). England then kept the cheapest layer of the apparatus. A standing adviser service gives any former teacher a named adviser, free, for application preparation and signposting to courses and vacancies; confined at first to mathematics, physics and modern foreign languages, it was later extended to every secondary subject and then to primary teaching (UK Parliament, 2022; Department for Education, 2023). In its two subject-restricted years the service recorded around 130 returns from more than 600 people advised (UK Parliament, 2022; Walker, 2022). It has never been independently evaluated; the department's own reporting has since folded into a statement that more teachers are returning than at any point in a decade (UK Parliament, 2026).

**Direct outreach from the employer's own records is the family that asks least of the returner, and it has run at scale in Austria, in most German Länder and in New South Wales.** The route is a letter, or a call, from the system that already knows who left, with the paperwork of a return bundled behind it. Austria's regional education directorates turned to it as the retirement wave crested. Vienna wrote to 300 already retired teachers and instructed school principals to raise the question with staff approaching retirement; Styria encouraged the same conversations; Salzburg contacted every retired teacher up to age 67, offering public-transport tickets and higher premiums; Vorarlberg opened a dedicated service point, which now sends information on working in retirement out together with the retirement paperwork itself (APA/ORF, 2023). The one qualitative study of the people addressed suggests why such contact is worth the postage, post-retirement decisions to return are biographical, and staying in contact with the former school is among the things that tip them (Resch, 2025a). Across six school years the country's Court of Audit counted 1 062 retired teachers who resumed teaching, in a workforce of roughly 123 000, with intakes that peaked in the pandemic years and then fell (Rechnungshof, 2025).

Most of Germany does the same, with thirteen of the sixteen Länder actively recruiting pensioned teachers on offers of flexible hours, lifted earnings limits and salary supplements of around 10% (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Hesse, the documented extreme, wrote to some 7 000 retired teachers in a school year and attributes about 550 filled posts to its whole short-term package of retiree recruitment, service extensions and part-time top-ups, not to the letters alone (Hessisches Ministerium für Kultus, Bildung und Chancen, 2024); nationwide, just over 5 000 pensioners were teaching, under 1% of the workforce (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).

The cheapest documented variant ran in New South Wales. From November 2023 the education department contacted more than 1 500 teachers who had resigned from the public system in the previous five years, bundling accreditation, approval to teach and background checks into a single conversation; more than 260 expressed interest, and 145 were placed in casual and temporary work spread across more than 600 schools, about 60% of them returning from retirement (NSW Government, 2024). The letters went out weeks after a major pay settlement, and the department's standing page for returners still leads with the pay rise (NSW Department of Education, 2026). Outreach of this kind is a channel, transmitting a changed offer, rather than an instrument that creates one; it earns its place on cheapness, and its funnel was published once, in May 2024, then vanished from the following annual report (NSW Department of Education, 2025).

**Money has been attached to the return in three different places, to the receiving school, to the returner's own pay and to the costs of a named posting, and what separates the designs is the stage of the return the money actually touches.**

The Netherlands paid the school. Between 2017 and 2020 the government paid primary school boards EUR 2 500 for each qualified returner appointed for at least six months after at least twelve months out of teaching, the money covering the board's induction and guidance costs rather than pay (Ministerie van OCW, 2017). The budget provided for 500 returners against a silent reserve estimated at 31 000 in the primary sector alone. Just over 100 grants were paid in the first full year and nearly 200 cumulatively by late 2019, the annual ceilings were lowered on extension precisely because the budget was not being exhausted, and the scheme lapsed without a published evaluation (Ministerie van OCW, 2019a; 2019b; Tweede Kamer, 2019). Successor regional schemes made activation of the silent reserve a fundable goal behind a mandatory regional information desk; their evaluation found tailored support to returners among the least realised activities, and several regions reported more applications from side-entrants and returners than schools could place, a self-reported finding that supports the direction of an absorption constraint rather than its magnitude (De Jong et al., 2022). An instrument that pays for a stage downstream of the binding one is deadweight by construction, and this one paid for induction, the stage after the constraint.

Portugal paid the returner, at the price of a novice. A decree of August 2024 created an exceptional route under which a teacher retired five years or less may return on a fixed-term contract in shortage subjects or understaffed schools, keeping the full pension, with additional pay indexed to the first step of the career salary scale and pro-rated by teaching hours, under an annual quota fixed by the government (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Against a quota of 200, some 80 applied and 63 were accepted, most commonly in Portuguese, history and special education (Diário de Notícias, 2024; Renascença, 2024). Those who came spoke of love of teaching, of recovering frozen service time and of thin pensions (Diário de Notícias, 2024); the largest union's leader called the idea that the retired would return "an illusion" (SÁBADO, 2024). The same decree carries a second instrument, a monthly supplement of EUR 750 for teachers who reach pensionable age and stay on, and some 2 200 teachers used it in 2025/26 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 6; RTP, 2026). The two articles form the record's cleanest paired reading of where money moves teachers, and the answer is before the exit rather than after it.

China paid the posting. The silver-age schemes recruit retired principals, teaching-research staff and special-grade and senior teachers, generally aged 65 or under, for at least a school year in named categories of county, counties recently lifted out of poverty, ethnic-minority counties and border counties among them (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). The recruit keeps the full pension and receives an annual allowance of CNY 20 000, funded on a gradient that charges the central budget most where the counties are poorest, with housing, a return journey each semester and insurance supplied in kind (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). The role is wider than a timetable, since demonstration lessons, service as vice-principals and the mentoring of young local teachers are written into it (MOE et al., 2023; State Council, 2023). The predecessor schemes report more than 20 000 recruits by mid-2023, and the current plan aims at a corps of some 120 000 across five education strands, though the counts are self-reported and no independent audit exists (State Council, 2023; MOE et al., 2023). An allowance that compensates the itemisable costs of a named posting is a different purchase from money offered flat against a revealed preference for staying away, and Chapter 2 set out why the preference usually wins. What almost nothing in this family offered was a change in the price of return itself, which stayed wherever the rules of the next family had set it.

**Statutory relief on pension and salary is the family that has produced returns in the thousands, and its instruments are written by legislatures and pension boards rather than by education ministries.** Its route is permission, a law letting a retired teacher hold a paid post without forfeiting the pension, on conditions the statute sets, with returns travelling through ordinary hiring. North Carolina's window, already met above, remains the family's landmark, and its returners averaged more than thirty years of experience (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Box 3.1 traces the design, its reach into high-need schools and the questions it left unanswered.

Georgia wrote the family's most restrictive statute and then audited it. Its rehire law of 2022 lets a retiree with 30 years of creditable service, retired for at least a year, teach full-time on full pension in the three highest-need subjects of the region, and the mandated audit counted 635 rehires over three fiscal years while surveyed employers named the statute's own screens as the reason wanted hires fell through; the renewal of 2026 kept the service rule, extended the programme to the end of the decade and devolved the high-need determination to school systems (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025; Teachers Retirement System of Georgia, 2026). Box 3.2 follows the loop from statute to audit to renewal.

New Mexico layered routes instead. Its educational retirees may work at a quarter of a post or less, or below an earnings ceiling, or, on the newest route, with no limits on salary or hours for a fixed span of months, subject to a 90-day layout, a ban on pre-arranged returns and pension-board approval, and the span was lengthened and the earnings ceiling raised in 2025 (New Mexico Legislature, 2022; New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025). The pension board's registry counted 2 792 working retirees across all routes in the newest route's first year, only 154 of them on the flagship no-limits route (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023).

Michigan simplified. An act of 2022 swept away a patchwork of critical-shortage lists and replaced it with two tests, a bona fide termination and a waiting period, after which a retiree may take any school post with no earnings limit and no loss of pension or insurance subsidy; a further act of 2023 cut the wait from nine months to six and allowed capped earnings during it, with the loosening sunset in 2028 (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022; 2023). Virginia has run pension plus salary in critical-shortage positions since 2001; an executive directive of 2022 added fast-tracked renewal of lapsed licences and instructed that vacant teaching posts be classified so that retirees could fill them, and legislation of 2023 halved the required break in service to six months while extending the provisions to 2028 (Will, 2022; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Virginia Retirement System, n.d.). Louisiana attaches the relief to a certified shortage, a post qualifying when advertisement yields fewer than three certified applicants; an act of 2024 opened the route to all retirees after a twelve-month wait, and the consolidated law of 2026 added a route free of pension impact from age 65, raised the outside earnings cap and let general certification suffice, ordering an actuarial report before 2034 (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024; 2026). Neither state publishes a take-up count.

Ontario shows the same family built as a valve rather than a window, a standing cap on the days a retired teacher may teach before the pension reprices, raised in a shortage, cut to protect newly certified teachers, raised again for the pandemic years and cut back when they ended (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). France, finally, enables without programming at all. Under its general framework for combining pension and earnings, the education authorities rehire retired teachers on contract for replacements and shortage subjects, behind a six-month delay before any return to the last employer, and since 2023 the resumed work accrues new pension rights (Assemblée nationale, 2020; 2023). A parliamentary question asking which obstacles should be lifted so that willing retirees could cover absences lapsed unanswered at the end of the legislature, and no aggregate count of returning retirees has ever been published (Assemblée nationale, 2024). The yields of this family, where counted at all, dwarf the campaigns'. What its waits, thresholds and screens jointly amount to, a price, is the argument of Section 3.2.

**Standing on-ramps hold a route open permanently, so that a return needs no campaign to travel through, and four systems show the design at different stages of maturity.** New Zealand keeps the lightest one in permanent law. A registered teacher whose practising certificate has expired may relief-teach up to 20 half-days a year without renewing it, the refresh requirement bites mainly when a returner lacks a job offer, and the government currently pays practising-certificate fees on teachers' behalf (New Zealand Legislation, 2020; Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026; New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026). Ireland activates its career-break teachers instead, suspending the scheme's substitute-work limits so that a teacher on career break may substitute without restriction, at regular substitute rates and with increments accruing under the general rules (Department of Education, 2019; Department of Education and Youth, 2025); one arrangement is permanent law, the other an annual renewal, a distinction Section 3.2 returns to. Japan runs the family's fullest expression, since re-employment after mandatory retirement is the default staffing route there. Boards of education conventionally re-employ retirees until pension age, and in the prefecture studied in depth retirees supply roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024; see Chapter 2). The same study marks the ceiling of any retiree-based channel. Only about 35% of age-based elementary retirees there now seek full-time re-employment, every retiree pool ages on its own schedule, and this paper does not attribute to design what demography may explain. The Bahamas, most recently, has institutionalised the reserve by name, announcing in mid-2026 a Teacher Reservist Program, a standing register of retired educators, trained teachers who built careers elsewhere and recent graduates that schools can call on, run on the platform of the existing supply-teacher programme, with a multi-agency task force to fast-track vetting and credential assessment as part of a drive to recruit up to 300 teachers (Cooper, 2026; ZNS Bahamas, 2026). It is announcement-stage and earns its place on design rather than results.

Across the families, the arithmetic is strikingly uniform. Wherever a full account of a campaign or an outreach drive exists, the instrument moved a fraction of one per cent of its eligible pool in a year, and near-universal outreach stayed inside the band. The band looks narrower still against the flow that runs with no campaign at all. Roughly 17 000 teachers return to English state schools in an ordinary year, about a third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023), and re-entrants have made up between roughly a third and a half of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). Return is a standing channel, produced by standing rules and ordinary hiring; a programme does not create the channel, it bids for a margin on top of it. The tutoring deployments of Chapter 2, which specified a role and a dose, remain the only programmes in this record whose product was ever measured against a comparison group, and specification is what turns an announcement into a purchase. Beyond volume, a programme can deliver a destination, and the record here is thin but consistent. Where the pension penalty was removed with no targeting mandate, returners went disproportionately to schools with more disadvantaged intakes, though the gradient was modest and more than half returned to the school they had left, so part of it is inherited attachment rather than steered movement (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; Box 3.1). Targeting by condition attaches money to the destination itself, as the Chinese county conditions do. Targeting by narrowing came later elsewhere, as when the Portuguese government restricted the stay-on supplement described above to shortage zones, conceding that the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026). Targeting by prohibition, screens meant to steer hires, produced the record's one audited failure, and it waits in the next section.

The last deliverable is survival, and only one system can currently report it. Linked English data show 32% of one returner cohort out of service again four years later, with a further 2% retired, against 29% of the same year's newly qualified cohort, and the odds of a returner leaving after the first year back run 60% above average (Buchanan et al., 2018). The gap is small and the cost ranges overlap, so the finding does not price returners against novices. What it settles is the denominator. A hire that does not survive its first year buys very little, and the honest unit of account for any programme in Table 3.1 is cost per retained teacher-year, at one and three years, rather than cost per placement.

The five families, in short, delivered what they were designed to touch, a contact, a stage of conversion, occasionally a destination, and the volumes of the first three stayed inside a narrow band unless something institutional moved behind them. Where the band broke, in North Carolina's window and in Japan's standing re-employment, the thing that moved was a rule, owned far from any programme office. Judged as the vacancy cover they were mostly bought for, even the successes stayed modest. The mass return's effect on the shortage went unmeasured (Box 3.1), and the one audited scheme was judged to have touched its own minimally (Box 3.2); re-entry alone closes no shortage in this record. What repairing the price demonstrably delivered, in the thousands, was experienced teachers. The rest of the chapter reads the rules of the fourth family as what they jointly are, a price list.

## 3.2. Regulation: where the price of a return is written

**A system's decisive re-entry policy is the price its own rules put on a return, and that price is written in four places, on pay, exclusion, intensity and licence, almost always by departments that do not answer for teacher supply and almost never on purpose.**

Consider two Austrian teachers with identical classroom careers, both retired, both invited back. One is offered the profession's starting salary on top of a full pension. The other broadly recovers pre-retirement pay. Nothing in their teaching separates them; what separates them is an employment status chosen decades earlier, because federal law excludes from prior-service crediting any period that already generates a pension, so a teacher who retired as a civil servant returns on full pension plus the starting salary while a former contract teacher, whose service is credited, comes back broadly at pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023). Two identical careers attract offers most of a salary scale apart, on the strength of a crediting rule written for the whole federal service that happens to fall on teachers too. Nobody designed it as re-entry policy. That is precisely the point.

The Austrian accident is one setting of one dial, and four dials together price a return in any system. The pay dial fixes the step a returner lands on and whether pension and salary may be drawn together. The exclusion dial decides who may return at all, through service thresholds, waiting periods and subject or region screens. The intensity dial caps how much teaching may be sold before the pension reprices. The licence dial governs lapse, refresh and waiver. Table 3.2 sets out where each dial sits in thirteen systems, and its most striking regularity is jurisdictional; almost none of these settings lives in education law.

**The pay dial separates the record's one mass return from its many small ones, and few governments have ever set it on purpose.** In England the dial is moving with nobody's hand on it, since abatement attaches only to final-salary retirement benefits and will dissolve of its own accord as career-average membership matures (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.). One government did write the low setting deliberately. Portugal's returner route lets a teacher retired five years or less come back on a fixed-term contract, keeping the pension, with additional pay indexed to the first step of the career scale and pro-rated by teaching hours (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Sixty-three people accepted against a quota of 200 (Diário de Notícias, 2024; Renascença, 2024). The reading cannot be entirely clean, because the application window lasted twelve days, placements began in January and the applicants averaged about 68 years of age, so process and demography share the explanation with pay. What can be said is that the offer asked veterans to return at the price of novices, and very few did.

The opposite setting produced the mass return. Where retirees could hold a full-time post at full salary while drawing a full pension, an income on the order of half as much again as a regular full-time teacher's, roughly 3 500 returned over a decade and the returners averaged over thirty years of experience (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; Box 3.1). The mechanism separating the settings is not mysterious. Teachers have been shown to value current cash at roughly five times deferred pension wealth (Fitzpatrick, 2015), so an offer that protects the pension while resetting current pay to the entry step withholds exactly the currency returners respond to, and preserves the one they discount.

Three cases carry the pay dial, and honesty requires saying what they cannot carry. They differ in pension regime, era, product and age profile as well as in the step a returner lands on, and nowhere has the step been varied while anything else was held constant. The discriminating test exists and is cheap. The Austrian audit could disaggregate its returners by former employment status, comparing the cohort forced onto the starting salary with the cohort whose service was credited. Even that comparison would price the pay rule together with everything else that distinguishes a career civil servant from a contract teacher, so a null result would be more informative than a positive one.

**The exclusion dial belongs to Georgia, the one state that audited its own scheme and found its own rules blocking the hires it wanted.** Eligibility conditions are additive in law and multiplicative in effect; a service threshold, a mandatory break and a subject-and-region test must each be cleared, each is set independently of the others, and the population that survives all three can be a small fraction of the population any one of them would pass. Georgia's rehire statute stacked a 30-year service requirement, a one-year break and a centrally determined list of the three highest-need subjects per region, and full-time rehires stayed below one per cent of the workforce while almost half (45%) of surveyed school systems reported at least one hire the statutory provisions had blocked (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). Box 3.2 traces the loop from statute to audit to renewal.

The audit is also the record's only employer-side counterfactual, and what employers named as the obstacle was not money. The retirees they did rehire earned a veteran wage the systems were evidently willing to pay (Box 3.2); what stopped further hires were the service rule, the waiting year and the subject methodology (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). The pay dial and the exclusion dial are therefore genuinely distinct charges, and a system can set one generously while the other quietly does the rationing.

Waiting periods, the most widespread exclusion, sit in pension-integrity law rather than in education policy. The French six-month delay before a retired public servant may resume work with the last employer exists to stop employers re-hiring their own just-retired staff more cheaply (Assemblée nationale, 2020). The American waits, ninety days in New Mexico, six months in Michigan and Virginia after earlier longer periods, a year in Louisiana and Georgia, descend from tax-law tests of bona fide retirement (New Mexico Legislature, 2022; Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2023; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024). Each guards a pension system's integrity. Each is also, incidentally, a charge on teacher re-entry levied by a body with no teacher-supply mandate.

Georgia has since renewed its rehire law with the audit on the table, extending the programme to the end of the decade, keeping the 30-year rule and devolving the high-need determination to school systems (Teachers Retirement System of Georgia, 2026). The screen that was relaxed is the locally held one; the screen that stayed is the centrally set one. The take-up series that follows will be the cleanest natural test of the exclusion dial the field is likely to get.

**Box 3.2. Writing the audit into the statute: Georgia's sunset, audit and renewal loop**

Georgia's House Bill 385 of 2022 allowed retirees with at least 30 years of creditable service, retired at least one year, to teach full-time on full pension in the three highest-need subject areas of their region, as determined annually by the state. The statute carried its own sunset, 30 June 2026, and its own audit requirement, and the mandated performance audit was delivered in April 2025.

The audit counted 635 retired teachers working full-time under the law for at least one school year across three fiscal years, roughly 350 in any given year, in a workforce of about 125 000 facing some 6 000 vacant or not-fully-certified posts. Related pension contributions totalled USD 11.1 million, under one per cent of contributions overall; the rehired retirees earned an average salary of USD 78 487. The auditors judged the effect on both the shortage and the pension system minimal. They also surveyed employers: 45% of 163 responding school systems said the statutory provisions had blocked a hire they wanted to make, naming the 30-year rule, the one-year break and the high-need-subject methodology, and about 65% planned to keep using the law. Eight other southern states had passed similar laws; Georgia's was the most restrictive.

The legislature then renewed with the audit on the table. Senate Bill 150, signed in May 2026, extended the programme to 2030, kept the 30-year service requirement, and let school systems determine their own high-need areas in place of the state methodology. Sunset, mandated count, arms-length audit and informed renewal closed as a loop exactly once in the record assembled for this paper, and this is that instance.

**The intensity dial is the one dial anyone has openly fought over, and the fight shows who owns it.** Ontario's cap on the days a retired teacher may teach before the pension reprices has been nearly quintupled in a shortage, roughly halved after a newspaper investigation documented heavy board spending on retired supply teachers at nearly twice the new-graduate day rate while newly certified teachers went short of assignments, restored for the pandemic years, and cut back when the pandemic agreement expired (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026; Table 3.2). Then, in March 2024, the teachers' federation that co-sponsors the pension plan refused a further extension, in the record's plainest statement of where this dial lives: "the Pension Plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers; it is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (OCEOTA, 2024; Alphonso, 2024). The cap moves only by agreement of the plan's co-sponsors, one of which answers to serving and newly certified teachers as well as to pensioners.

What moving the cap achieved is, strikingly, unknown. No post-liberalisation take-up figure for the pandemic increase was ever published, and the number most often quoted against permissiveness is a union estimate of member interest released on the day the change was announced, which measures appetite for a forty-five-day increment rather than appetite for returning (Global News, 2022); this paper declines to borrow it. The fuller lesson comes from the system that removed intensity limits altogether. When New Mexico opened a route with no salary or hours caps, the flagship route drew barely a hundred and fifty users in its first year, within a total of fewer than three thousand retirees working across all four routes, against tens of thousands potentially eligible (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023; Table 3.1). Permission is not price. Liberalising a cap moves nobody by itself, and unprogrammed re-employment under long-standing rules dwarfs the flagship route built to showcase the liberalisation.

The intensity dial also holds the standing on-ramps, and here the record poses a choice rather than answering one. The statutory allowance of relief teaching without a current certificate, noted in Chapter 2, is permanent law in one system (New Zealand Legislation, 2020). The annual suspension of substitute-work limits for teachers on career break has been renewed annually for several successive school years in another (Department of Education and Youth, 2025), and several of the American instruments carry sunsets. An instrument justified as barrier removal should be permanent and evaluated as regulation, while one justified as crisis cover should be explicitly sunset and counted. What no system should do is run a crisis instrument on annual renewal for five years while describing it as structural reform.

**The licence dial is the cheapest to move and, once moved, the least measured.** The scale of the barrier is register-visible, since close to half of the certified non-teachers in the register examined in Chapter 1 held expired certificates (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The designs on offer are sensible. One system ties its refresh requirement to the absence of a job offer, so a returner a school actually wants is never delayed by the rule, and currently pays practising-certificate fees on teachers' behalf (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026; New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026); another fast-tracked lapsed licences by executive directive (Will, 2022); others simplified their working-after-retirement rules outright (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022) or folded relicensing into them (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). Yet no jurisdiction publishes take-up for a licensure waiver, so the dial most often recommended in policy commentary is the one whose effect the record cannot yet show, and claims for it belong in the register of promise rather than of evidence.

Read down the columns of Table 3.2 and a single finding emerges. Every dial that has demonstrably moved re-entry volume in this record sits outside the education ministry, the crediting rule in civil-service and pension law, pension-salary concurrency in state retirement statutes, the day cap with a pension plan's co-sponsors, the waiting period in social-security and tax law. A re-entry strategy written wholly inside an education ministry is a strategy over the least powerful instruments in the system, which is much of why the campaigns of Section 3.1 moved so little while costing so little.

What an education ministry can do, on its own authority and within a week, is audit the price. The charges a state levies on its own former teachers are a finite list; it can be totalled, published and taken to the departments that own each dial, beginning with the salary step. No country in this record has published such an audit. Any of them could. If the price is the policy, the question becomes what a ministry should do first, and the next chapter answers it in order, together with the two outcomes a programme can genuinely deliver and the counting duty without which none of it can be judged.
