# Abstract

Education systems in much of the OECD are short of teachers, and their workforces are ageing towards retirement. Governments have responded, as they have for decades, with active policies to bring former teachers back, from recruitment campaigns to changes in pension and licensing rules. This policy paper compiles that international record and asks what actually moved people. The answer divides cleanly. Campaigns returned teachers by the dozen, while every large return followed a repair to the terms the state itself sets, because in most systems the price of a return is written in pension and civil-service law and no ministry has ever totalled it. Former teachers left to buy back their time, and they return for offers shaped like that trade, hours rather than posts. What returns with them is experience that lifts the colleagues around them, capability the system itself paid to build. The lesson for policy is to repair the deal rather than advertise it, and to judge every instrument by who returned and who stayed.

# Résumé

Dans une grande partie de la zone OCDE, les systèmes éducatifs manquent d'enseignants et leurs effectifs vieillissent vers la retraite. Les gouvernements y répondent, comme depuis des décennies, par des politiques actives visant à faire revenir les anciens enseignants, des campagnes de recrutement aux modifications des règles de pension et de certification. Ce document d'orientation compile ce bilan international et demande ce qui a réellement fait bouger les intéressés. La réponse est nette. Les campagnes ramènent des enseignants par dizaines, tandis que chaque retour massif a suivi une réparation des conditions que l'État fixe lui-même, car dans la plupart des systèmes le prix d'un retour est inscrit dans le droit des pensions et de la fonction publique, et aucun ministère n'en a jamais établi le total. Les anciens enseignants sont partis pour racheter leur temps, et ils reviennent pour des offres à la mesure de cet échange, des heures plutôt que des postes. Avec eux revient une expérience qui élève leurs collègues, une capacité que le système a lui-même payée. La leçon pour l'action publique est de réparer le contrat plutôt que de l'annoncer, et de juger chaque instrument à l'aune de qui est revenu et de qui est resté.

# Key take-aways

- **Every system examined in this paper is sitting on a reserve of former teachers it has never honestly counted, and retirement waves are feeding it.** The headline figures in circulation count rows in a register, where a veteran who left twenty years ago sits beside last year's resigner. A count netted for licence status and recency of practice would be smaller and far more useful, and every register already holds the data to produce one within a year.

- **Leaving was a purchase of time, paid for in earnings, and former teachers return for offers that let them keep what they bought.** Every system that recruits them in numbers sells days and part-time contracts rather than full-time posts, and the part-time work they want is precisely what schools rarely advertise. The offer that works has been identified for years; stocking it costs the price of an advertisement.

- **Campaigns bring teachers back by the dozen; the rules governments already own bring them back by the thousand.** England's national return pilot placed twenty-seven teachers. North Carolina, which for a decade stopped docking pensions for returning to work, drew more than three thousand retirees back through ordinary hiring with no campaign attached. Re-entry alone will not close a shortage, and the record's honest lesson is cheaper than the campaigns that promise it will.

- **The price of a return is set by the state, mostly by accident, in laws education ministries do not write.** The salary step a returner lands on, and whether pension and salary can be drawn together, sit in pension and civil-service law with departments that have never answered for a classroom. In Austria a crediting rule written for the whole federal service leaves two identical teaching careers with offers most of a salary scale apart. A ministry could total what its own state charges a former teacher to come back, and publish the bill within weeks; none has.

- **Recovering an experienced teacher recovers capability the system already paid to build, and the proven way to buy it is as a defined job at a specified dose.** Experienced colleagues measurably lift the teachers around them, so a returning veteran is more than a filled vacancy. Where former teachers tutored to a protocol with a set number of sessions, children learned; where systems bought seniority and goodwill, what the school gained went unmeasured. The premium is plausible, and a system finds out by specifying the job and writing the evaluation into the contract of purchase.

- **A re-entry policy earns belief when somebody other than its sponsor counts what it did, and the count outlives the press release.** Placements are the friendliest number a programme owns; the one returner cohort anyone could follow lost close to a third of its members again within four years. Each instrument that produced usable evidence carried a counting duty lodged outside the sponsoring ministry, with a sunset timed so the numbers could still change the decision. That duty costs a single clause at the drafting stage.
