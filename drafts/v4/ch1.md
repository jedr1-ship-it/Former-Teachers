# 1. Introduction: the people who left

Every year teachers hand back their classroom keys and keep their licences. Some go home, to a newborn or an ageing parent. Others resign mid-career and take the degree somewhere that makes fewer claims on their evenings. The rest, in time, retire, on a date fixed by pension rules rather than by anything that happened in a classroom. Together they form one of the largest movements of qualified professionals out of any public service, and they are the subject of this paper.

They do not go far. In the United States, where linked records make it possible to follow leavers, more than half remain in education broadly defined, about a fifth withdraw from paid work altogether, and fewer than a quarter take jobs outside education (Brummet et al., 2024). Earlier cohort evidence points the same way, with most exiting women leaving the labour force rather than changing employer (Stinebrickner, 2002). Nor do they stop being teachers in law. The licence survives the resignation, and with it, in most systems, the service history that would set a returning salary. Recruitment, for its part, has grown harder. Across OECD countries the share of students in schools whose principals report that a lack of teaching staff hinders instruction rose from 26% in 2018 to 47% in 2022, a perception measure; counted vacancies are rarer, averaging 1.6% of positions in the systems that measure them, though above 4% in Austria and Sweden (OECD, 2025a). The workforce still serving is ageing towards retirement, a concern from the Baltic states to Portugal, and in Italy teachers aged 55 and over substantially outnumber those under 30 (European Commission, 2023). A gap in front and a retirement wave behind it are why governments are knocking on former teachers' doors now. Ministries have taken to calling the people beyond those doors a reserve, and the word circulates in budget debates as if it named a measured quantity. Meanwhile some of the people it names simply come back. Wherever anyone measures the inflow, returners supply a third or more of new entrants in a given year (Department for Education, 2023; Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), an unprogrammed flow that Chapter 3 treats as the baseline against which any programme must be judged. Every government already operates a re-entry system. Few operate it on purpose.

This paper is about these people, what governments offered when they wanted them back, and what the state's own rules charged them for returning. The courtship is partly shortage cover, and the paper says so plainly; systems reach for their former teachers because vacancies hurt. That motive is respectable without being sufficient, since re-entry alone cannot close a shortage, and nothing in what follows sells it as the fix. The premise is rather that former teachers matter in their own right, for who they are and what they carry, and the paper states it here rather than discovering it at the end. The barriers that now separate a former teacher from a school were mostly written by the state itself, in pension law, certification rules and salary scales, which is why the record of re-entry policy is above all a record of what governments have done to their own rulebooks.

## 1.1. Three ways of leaving

**Teachers leave by three different doors, and the door decides most of what a return would take.**

The first door leads out of paid work altogether, most often into care or family responsibilities. The people who took it hold a live qualification and no current employer, and their exit was timed by their lives rather than by the school calendar. The second door leads into another sector, taken by mid-career resigners who carried the qualification with them; the linked records cited above suggest this group is smaller than commonly assumed. The third door is retirement, and it opens on actuarial terms.

How a system's leavers divide among the doors is knowable, and it varies more than the debate assumes. Across OECD countries with data, about half (51%) of the fully qualified teachers who left the profession in the most recent year measured resigned rather than retired. Resignations exceed 80% of departures in some systems, notably Denmark, England and Estonia, while retirements account for most departures in others, including France, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a). A system whose former teachers are mostly working-age resigners faces a different repair job from one whose former teachers are mostly pensioners, which is why the exit split is the first diagnostic to run, before any programme is designed.

## 1.2. What they carry, and what prices them

**A former teacher arrives already trained at public expense, already licensed or relicensable, and fluent in how schools actually run.**

That is the case for treating them as more than a staffing convenience. They can be deployed without consuming an initial training place, and much of what they would bring back overlaps with what systems already prize in veteran staff, knowledge of the curriculum as it is actually taught and the standing to support colleagues newer than themselves. Recovering them is recovering capability the system itself paid to build, and Chapter 2 reviews evidence that experienced colleagues raise the quality of the teachers around them. The claim must stop there. No study in this evidence base links a returner to the outcomes of the classes they teach or the colleagues they work beside, so the classroom value of accumulated craft stands unmeasured rather than demonstrated. The paper treats that as a gap in what has been measured, and returns in the final chapter to the linkage that would settle it.

Beyond the doors, two facts about an individual do most of the policy work, and both sit in the registers. The first is whether the person ever served. Someone qualified who never entered a classroom has no classroom experience to bring back, and under the premise of this paper is not a former teacher at all, whatever they represent for a supply model. The confusion is not hypothetical; in one system it reached the statute book. The Dutch returner subsidy defined its target as a teacher who had interrupted, or had not begun, a career in primary education (Ministerie van OCW, 2017), so the instrument's own legal definition of a returner covered people with nothing to return from. Supply planners may reasonably count the never served as convertible recruits, and for their purposes they are; the two series should at the very least be published apart.

The second fact is where the person stands relative to pensionable age, because that boundary decides which body of law prices the return. Past it, pension and service-crediting rules set the offer. Before it, salary scales and certification rules do.

## 1.3. The reserve, honestly counted

**The reserve is real, and every register examined in this paper already holds the data to count it honestly within a year.**

What no ministry has done is publish that count. The figures in circulation are accurate counts of whatever rule the register applied, and the rules differ enough to change the answer entirely (Table 1.1). In England, nearly a third of the headline pool of qualified teachers outside state service has never been in service at all, a group the department already holds as a separate series (Worth, 2023, citing DfE), so a substantial share of the quoted number has nothing to return to. Michigan shows the same picture from the inside, through a register that links certificates to employment. Seven in ten of the certified non-teachers had not taught in a public school at any point in the previous five years, and close to half held expired certificates, against a small minority of serving teachers (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The Netherlands managed to hold two reserves at once. A count restricted to people qualified since the turn of the century put the figure at just over 62 000 (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b); a series counting every qualification holder at least two years out of the sector, retirees excluded, put it near 98 000 (DUO, 2026). The gap between the figures is the gap between the rules. Even under a single rule, the shares of the qualified stock differ so sharply between primary and secondary education (Table 1.1) that no one percentage describes a national reserve.

The repair is a definitional exercise, and a short one. A register that records service history can separate the served from the never served; certification records distinguish valid from restorable from lapsed; last-taught dates separate recent leavers from those a decade or more away; and subject and phase can be set against vacancies, age against pensionable age, residence against region. Each cut moves the count downward and none moves it up, so the direction of the error in the headline figures is known even where its magnitude is not. No system examined here publishes the resulting series. Any of them could. A ministry that has been quoting a reserve is a definition and a query away from quoting a measurement, and the OECD has already named the underlying gap, observing that many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees (OECD, 2024).

An honest upper bound is all the netted series would be, and the paper claims nothing more for it. Willingness, geography, subject fit and the price of return all bind after the register does. But a defensible upper bound beats an indefensible one, and it changes what a minister can honestly promise.

## 1.4. What this paper does

**This paper compiles what governments have actually done to bring former teachers back, and reads that record for what worked.**

The strand is an established one, and it is a record of action, not of commentary. Governments have run active re-attraction policies for decades; England and Wales were sponsoring around a hundred refresher courses a year for returners in the early 2000s, when OECD teacher policy first named former teachers a potentially important source of recruits (OECD, 2005), and supporting the re-entry of teachers who left the profession appears among the supply levers in the Organisation's current policy stocktake (OECD, 2024). What this paper adds is the compilation itself, read for what moved people; the international record of these attempts has not previously been set side by side. Drawing on programme evaluations, audit findings, legislation and administrative statistics from some twenty education systems, it asks what governments offered former teachers, what price their own rules attached to return, and what verifiably happened.

Chapter 2 stays with the people, asking what those who left actually bought when they went and what a returner can be asked to do. Chapter 3, the centre of the paper, examines the international experience with re-entry programmes and with the pension, licensure and re-employment rules that govern return. Chapter 4 converts the record into lessons and recommendations, ending with the two measurements on which better policy most depends, a reserve netted to a stated specification and a returner linked to the classes and colleagues they work with. Throughout, cases appear as evidence for propositions, and the argument is meant to be legible without any single one of them. The examples illustrate types of instrument rather than representing the national systems they are drawn from, and nothing in what follows categorises countries or systems.

None of it matters unless the people themselves would come. The first question therefore belongs to them, and Chapter 2 opens with it. What did they give up teaching to get, and what would make a return worth the price?
