# 2. The deal they made

A government that invites its former teachers back is asking them to reverse a decision most of them do not regret. Followed into their new jobs, leavers report themselves more satisfied and are paid less than the colleagues they left behind, and that combination is not what misfortune looks like. It is what a purchase looks like. They gave up earnings and received time, fewer hours and more say over when to work them.

This chapter treats the exit as that purchase and reads every re-entry offer as a bid against it, sorting the documented motives for the exit by who owns them. The reading explains why the only offers that recruit former teachers in numbers are denominated in days rather than posts, and it exposes, without resolving it cheaply, a genuine split between what leavers say would bring them back and what they observably accept. The chapter then turns to the return itself and asks what a returner can be asked to do, a question on which the trial record has so far priced exactly one product. Chapter 3 will ask which governments, if any, ever put the matching offer on the table.

## 2.1. What leaving bought

**Leavers paid for their exit in earnings and were repaid in time, and on the evidence of their later behaviour most of them consider the price fair.**

The cleanest view of the trade comes from household panel data that follow the same individuals across the exit. In Australia, where such data span two decades, former teachers report job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations above stayers one year after leaving and 0.19 above after three, while their weekly wages run 6.2% and then 9.4% below their final year in post, and their hourly wages remain lower for a decade (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). Satisfaction that rises as earnings fall is hard to read as anything but a trade concluded on acceptable terms. Most leavers got what they paid for.

Nor is the Australian ledger a local curiosity. English leavers accept a wage cut of roughly a tenth against comparable stayers, in exchange for shorter hours and higher job satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). Very few American leavers move to jobs paying more than teaching did (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006), and linked administrative records in the United States find more than half of them still working in education broadly defined, with mean earnings still slightly below pre-exit levels years later (Brummet et al., 2024). Asked rather than observed, Dutch teachers outside the classroom rate several conditions of their current jobs more favourably than serving teachers rate theirs, and put lower work pressure, more control over working hours and more autonomy ahead of salary when naming what would bring them back (Somers et al., 2024a). In three quite different labour markets the ledger of leaving reads the same, time gained and money knowingly given up.

For a substantial minority the purchase was not a better job but a stage of life. In the classic cohort evidence, around two thirds of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely, and the presence of a newborn child predicted exit more strongly than pay did (Stinebrickner, 2002). That cohort is historical, and parental leave and female labour supply have both changed since, yet recent panel data still place more than a quarter of leavers outside the labour force a year after exit, before a single retirement is counted (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). For this group the price of a return moves with childcare, the school calendar and the availability of part-time posts, and the salary scale is consulted last, if at all.

One qualification is too important for a footnote. Panel estimates of this kind are computed on people still observable after the exit, which excludes those who left permanently and disappeared from view, so they are strongest as evidence about temporary exit, about leavers who kept a foot in the door. The limitation turns out to be an asset. The segment for whom exit was explicitly temporary is precisely the segment that converts best when an offer arrives, as Section 2.4 will show, so the population these estimates describe is the population a re-entry offer actually addresses.

What that population holds, then, is neither a grievance nor spare cash. It is employed, mostly still close to education, and no richer than it was. It has time.

## 2.2. Why they left

**The motives on record for leaving teaching sort into those personal to the teacher and those that belong to the system, and the sorting decides who holds the remedy.**

The literature supplies the sorting. Its largest meta-analysis groups the correlates of leaving into personal characteristics, school factors such as leadership and working conditions, and external or policy factors such as accountability and pay (Nguyen et al., 2020); the entry literature speaks instead of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Watt et al., 2012). This paper pools school and policy factors as system motives, since an employer or a government wrote each and could rewrite it. Against them stand the motives personal to a life, which no ministry drafts and none can amend.

The most-documented motives carry one conclusion, that most of the recorded weight of leaving rests on the system side of the ledger, in rules and conditions a government already owns (Table 2.1).

Workload leads the system column because behaviour, not testimony, put it there; the leavers of Section 2.1 gave up earnings to escape the hours, a tenth of their wages in the English case. The motive binds hardest where teaching is sold only in full-time units, and there it shades into the personal demand for command of one's own time. A thin part-time market, as in England and the Netherlands (Table 2.1), makes hours both the reason for going and the obstacle to coming back.

Salary weighs most where an alternative career is genuinely bidding. The American evidence is the most salary-responsive in the base (Table 2.1), financial incentives reliably retain serving teachers in the quasi-experimental record (Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026), and return rates run highest in subjects with the fewest opportunities elsewhere (OECD, 2005). In Australia and England, where leavers demonstrably accept lower pay, the same dial turns little.

Leadership is what leavers name first, ahead of workload, in the one national survey of former teachers that asked (Table 2.1), and stress works through the same organisational channel, roughly doubling the stated intention to leave (OECD, 2020). Which stressor dominates is a national matter. Pooled TALIS 2024 data put student behaviour ahead of workload and accountability pressures as a predictor of intended exit, a ranking the Australian and English evidence reverses in workload's favour (OECD, 2025b); the accountability climate is a policy possession wherever it bites, and how hard it bites refuses to travel across borders.

The personal motives run on a different calendar. A newborn child predicted exit better than pay in the classic cohort (Table 2.1), and the motive's grip depends on the institutions around it, since part-time opportunities, leave schemes and family-friendly support raise the rate at which women return (OECD, 2005). Retirement, by contrast, only feels personal; teachers leave earlier than comparable professionals because pension wealth is legislated to peak and then fall (Table 2.1). The decision arrives with a birthday, and the timing was written by a fund.

Every system motive in Table 2.1 has an owner with the power of amendment, from school leadership to pension law, and Chapters 3 and 4 accordingly read re-entry policy as repair. The personal motives have no owner and need none; they are the motives the purchase of time was made to serve, and the offer that meets them is denominated in hours, as Section 2.4 will show. Both theses of this paper are consequences of the same table.

## 2.3. What would reverse the trade?

**Asked directly, former teachers name salary as the price of return; watched rather than asked, they keep paying for conditions, and the two answers cannot both be taken at face value.**

The survey arm deserves to be stated at full strength before it is examined. In the one American state to survey its non-teaching certificate-holders at scale, a third (33%) placed a higher salary among their three most important reasons for not teaching, and a salary increase was the incentive to return chosen most often, ahead of pension, benefit and loan-forgiveness measures that were themselves financial (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Nothing in this paper's evidence base observes revealed preferences at comparable scale, so the finding cannot be discounted merely for pointing away from the panel data of Section 2.1.

It can be examined with the scepticism applied to everything else. The conditions-first reading rests on observed post-exit trajectories in three separate labour markets. The salary-first reading rests on a single survey with a response rate below one in three, which its own authors describe as not representative, answered from a menu of incentives dominated by financial items (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Stated willingness is also cheap in every direction. In the one national registration survey that asks, over half of registered teachers not working in schools say they would consider returning under the right circumstances, while roughly one in twenty is actively looking (AITSL, 2025). A costless signal at unspecified terms estimates nothing about supply, in either currency.

Part of the split dissolves once the population is subdivided, because how people left shapes what would bring them back. Leavers whose exit was a life event return when circumstances change, not when advertising improves, and many in the classic cohort eventually did return (Stinebrickner, 2002). Mid-career resigners are priced by workload and control. Retirees are priced by pension law, whatever they tell a surveyor about salary. The exit split introduced in Chapter 1 thus selects the relevant dial before any questionnaire is fielded.

What remains of the split is genuinely unresolved, and its honest use is directional. Post-exit earnings trajectories are a product registers already generate, so a ministry can establish which reading fits its own leavers from data the state already holds, at a cost far below that of a programme calibrated to a misread preference. A system whose leavers demonstrably paid for conditions buys little by bidding money against that record; a system whose former teachers have stated a price has at least been told which dial to test first. Chapter 3 returns to both dials with the programme record in hand.

## 2.4. Selling days rather than posts

**Wherever former teachers return in numbers, what they are being sold is hours, because an offer of days lets a returner keep the deal they made.**

The standing regulatory routes converge on this product from starting points that share nothing else. New Zealand's statutory on-ramp is twenty half-days of relief teaching a year without a current practising certificate (New Zealand Legislation, 2020). Ontario lets a retired teacher work fifty days a year before pension consequences begin (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). Ireland's annual supply measure suspends the limit on substitute work by teachers already on career break (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). No preference study produced any of these rules. Each simply defines a small, divisible quantity of teaching, and that is the quantity the market clears.

The convergence has a seller as well as a buyer. A day of relief teaching is a transaction a principal can complete alone. It creates no establishment post and no placement on the salary scale, it commits nobody for a year, and the thresholds above sit exactly where pension or certification consequences would otherwise begin. Each instrument, in effect, marks out the largest amount of teaching a former teacher can supply without re-engaging the machinery they left. What happens when that machinery is engaged anyway is the business of Chapter 3.

Where returners actually land confirms what the rules sell. In the one Japanese prefecture studied in depth, retirees supply roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024). The returners recruited through direct outreach in New South Wales went into casual and temporary engagements (NSW Government, 2024), and the Portuguese returner statute goes further still and pro-rates its pay by teaching hours (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Nor is any of this an invention of the current generation of programmes. At the turn of the century returners already made up about a quarter of the full-time inflow into UK maintained schools, and more than half of the part-time inflow (OECD, 2005). Hours are the channel through which return has always run.

The demand side matches the supply side with unusual precision. When registrants for England's returner pilot were segmented by motivation, the most return-committed group, the career breakers, made up 11% of registrants and returned at roughly double the rate of the other four types (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). What that group wanted was part-time work, and part-time posts are what schools rarely advertise. Interest was never the binding constraint in that market. The advertised part-time post was.

Two systems reached the operational conclusion independently, which is some evidence that it is the right one. England's successor programme tied funded returner places to schools with identified vacancies, correcting a pilot that had prepared people for posts that did not exist (NCTL, 2017). New Zealand waives its refresher requirement for returners who already hold an offer of work, so that the rule never delays a returner a school actually wants (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026). Both treat the identified vacancy, with the hours attached to it, as the unit in which re-entry policy deals. Neither disguises the motive. Systems knock on former teachers' doors because vacancies hurt, and what secures the returner is the offer shaped to the deal they made, not the knocking.

The proposition has a boundary, and the salary survey of Section 2.3 patrols it. No system examined here has ever offered a full-time post on genuinely changed conditions, with a protected timetable and a capped administrative load, so the revealed preference for small quantities is partly a preference expressed on the only menu ever printed. The obvious test, advertised part-time contracts with protected timetables, has been named in the literature since 2018 and tried nowhere (Worth and Sharp, 2018).

There is one true exception to the rule that scale comes in days, and it is instructive about what full posts cost. A single system has filled establishment posts with former teachers at scale. It did so by letting a full pension stack on top of a full salary, an institutional change whose price and consequences belong to Chapter 3 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

## 2.5. What can a returner be asked to do?

**The one deployment of this workforce ever shown to move pupil outcomes was a defined job at a specified dose, and that is a fact about what has been measured, not a ceiling on what former teachers can do.**

The question matters because the channel that absorbs most returners asks least of what they carry. Relief and casual work buys presence in a classroom. It buys no continuity with a class and no part in induction or mentoring, and nothing in the record says what is gained or lost by deploying twenty years of experience as interchangeable cover. The comparison is unattempted rather than expensive; any system that linked returner records to the classes they teach and the colleagues they work beside could make it.

Nor is what returners carry a constant across systems. Among the seven OECD countries with seniority data on resigning teachers, one in three resigners on average had served less than five years, but the share runs from about one in six in Denmark to around two thirds in Israel (OECD, 2025a; Table 2.2). One country's leaver pool is deep in accumulated craft; another's is largely early-career. A role for returners has to be designed against the pool a system actually has, which is one more reason the netted, subdivided count of Chapter 1 comes first.

At one end of the design spectrum sits a role scripted to the minute, and it is the closest the trial literature has come to pricing this workforce. A structured mathematics protocol for at-risk first-graders, taught in small groups for forty minutes, three times a week, over seventeen weeks, was delivered by more than eighty tutors recruited from local pools of retired and substitute teachers. Across nearly a thousand first-graders in dozens of schools in four urban districts, it reproduced an effect first obtained with graduate students, at +0.34 standard deviations on a standardised early-mathematics test (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012; Fuchs et al., 2005). What the districts bought was fidelity to a protocol rather than professional judgement, and what they bought is exactly what the trial measured.

At the other end sits the trial that specified nothing and settled nothing. Recently retired master educators, screened by interview for a record of excellence and deliberately kept out of evaluating the teachers they mentored, averaged more than two decades in their district's high-need schools. The 77 probationary teachers in 11 schools of one district were assigned at random to receive one for two years, with both arms keeping the district's business-as-usual buddy mentoring, so the trial estimates the added value of the retired mentor over standard support (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). It meets What Works Clearinghouse standards without reservations, and under those standards its effects are indeterminate in all three reviewed domains, reading, mathematics and teacher retention (What Works Clearinghouse, 2019). Its first-year mathematics result did not survive the report's own multiple-comparison adjustment, and its retention point estimates ran the wrong way. The one encouraging signal concerns dose, a doubling of the odds that a mentee stayed with each additional ten hours of mentoring, and it is exploratory and non-causal by the report's own account. How much mentoring the pairs actually did had been left largely to the pairs.

Set side by side, the two trials seem to hand down a verdict, that structure works and craft does not. They will not carry that reading. One trial specified a job and a dose and found an effect. The other specified neither, and an indeterminate rating under trial standards is a statement about evidential power, not a negative result; a small trial that set no dose is no verdict on twenty years of practice. What the pair actually demonstrates is narrower and more useful. The deployment someone bothered to define is also the only one anyone has managed to price.

Even the positive trial resists a clean attribution to former teachers. Its tutors were retired and substitute teachers, with no published split between the two, and the wider tutoring meta-analysis finds the largest effects for teacher and paraprofessional tutors without ever isolating retired educators as a class (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024). The longest-running deployment of older adults as school reading tutors, for its part, is evidence about older volunteers, since being a former teacher is not among its eligibility conditions (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009). That programme still earns its place here, because its randomised volunteer arm found health gains for the tutors themselves, in physical activity in a pilot trial and in brain volume within a neuroimaging subsample (Fried et al., 2004; Carlson et al., 2015). A finding of that kind invites a health ministry's budget into a conversation education ministries mostly conduct alone.

None of this leaves the case for former teachers resting on faith. A former teacher arrives with a valid or restorable licence and can be deployed without consuming a place in initial teacher education. They combine fluency in how schools run with a position outside the school's evaluation hierarchy, a trust advantage the mentoring trial's own report identifies and that neither a new graduate nor a serving head of department can offer (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The mechanism a designed role would harness is documented too, and it is the reason this population is worth recovering in itself. Teachers improve measurably when the quality of the colleagues around them rises, with the gains largest for the least experienced (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009). An experienced returner is therefore more than a filled vacancy. They are capability the system itself paid two decades to build, and placing them beside novices is the nearest thing a school system has to growing from within. But that mechanism works through sustained proximity, and dose is not a detail. The largest randomised trial of comprehensive teacher induction found no effects after one year of support, and a positive achievement effect only in the third year, among teachers who had received two full years of it (Glazerman et al., 2010).

Availability is no obstacle to buying a serious dose. The doses that matter in this literature run to a few dozen hours or sessions a year, with student gains concentrated among pupils who received at least that much (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009), while the caps that constrain retiree work run to hundreds of hours. A system that engages former teachers for token hours should expect no measured effect, and a system that buys a real dose is buying well inside what its own rules already permit.

What is still missing is the linkage that would settle the craft question, in either direction. No study in this evidence base connects a returner to the outcomes of a class or of a colleague. Even the authors of the largest documented return episode, several thousand experienced retirees back in full-time posts, state that they could not attempt the estimate for want of vacancy data and a counterfactual (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Twenty years of craft may well deserve a premium. Only a specified purchase has ever been priced, and the distance between those two sentences measures effort, not value. The spillover evidence is what keeps the premium a live hypothesis rather than a sentiment, because a mechanism shown to lift nearby colleagues could be operating unpriced in every staffroom a returner joins, and a hypothesis that plausible earns the linkage study. Linked data could show returners outperforming novices in comparable posts, which would strengthen a premise this paper has declined to assert, or underperforming them, which would narrow the rationale to the licence and the deployability. Chapter 4 converts the linkage study, and the trial of a designed role at a specified dose, into requests.

The chapter closes where it opened, on the trade. Former teachers sold earnings for time and have shown no wish to unwind the sale, so the offer that respects the trade is denominated in hours, attached wherever possible to an identified vacancy and, for the designed roles, delivered at a dose someone chose on purpose. That specification is now in hand. Chapter 3 reads two decades of programmes and regulation against it, asking which states, if any, ever put the matching offer on the table, what price their own rules attached to a return, and who set that price.
