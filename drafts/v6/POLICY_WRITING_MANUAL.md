# A DRAFTING STANDARD FOR POLICY PAPERS
58 mechanically checkable rules, with authority and example

---

## 1. HOW A PARAGRAPH IS BUILT

**1. Put old information in the topic position and new information in the stress position.**
*Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (1990).* — "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information."
✓ "This gap in coverage has a fiscal cost. **That cost fell mainly on municipalities.**" ✗ "Municipalities bore the cost, which is a fiscal consequence of the coverage gap."

**2. Every unit has a topic and a stress position — sentence, paragraph, section.** Check the last sentence of each paragraph as hard as the first.
*Gopen & Swan.* Paragraphs have topic sentences and stress sentences; the emphatic material must land at the point of closure.
✗ A paragraph that ends on a caveat or a citation instead of on the finding.

**3. State the point of the paragraph in the last sentence of its opening segment.**
*Williams & Bizup, Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, Lesson 8.* "Put the point sentence at the end of the short opening segment; make it the last sentence your reader reads before starting the longer, more complex segment that follows."
✓ Sentence 1 frames; sentence 2 states the claim; sentences 3–6 evidence it.

**4. Name, in the point sentence, the key terms that thread through the rest of the paragraph.**
*Williams & Bizup.* "Toward the end of that point sentence, name the key themes that thread through what follows."
✗ Point sentence says "results were mixed"; body then introduces *inconsistent*, *no measurable effect* — terms the reader was never given.

**5. One idea per paragraph; a new topic starts a new paragraph.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.), "Maintain clarity, consistency and cohesion".* "Keep paragraphs down to single ideas… Discuss new topics in new paragraphs." Reinforced by *UK Analysis Function*: "one concept per paragraph."

**6. Start strong: the main point of the paragraph goes up front, not at the end.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* "Start strong: place the main point of your paragraphs up front. Your opening sentence and paragraph should tell the reader what you are writing about and why it is significant."

**7. The paragraph is a unit of thought, not of length; it must be homogeneous in subject matter and sequential in treatment.**
*Fowler, quoted in The Economist Style Guide (12th ed.).* One-sentence paragraphs "should be used only occasionally."

**8. Evidence sits in the middle of the paragraph, between the claim and its implication — never first, never last.**
*Williams & Bizup* (point sentence opens; readers must see how everything is relevant to the point) + *OECD 3rd ed.* ("Subsequent sentences in the paragraph should contain details the reader needs").
✗ Opening a paragraph with "Figure 3 shows…"; ✓ "Coverage stalled after 2015. Enrolment rose by 1.2 points a year to 2015 and by 0.1 points after (Figure 3). The stall coincides with the freeze in per-pupil grants."

**9. Keep topic strings short and consistent across consecutive sentences.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 5.* "Through a series of sentences, keep your topics short and reasonably consistent."
✗ Sentence subjects running: *competition… labour costs… a plan…* ✓ subjects running: *we… we… this study…*

**10. Cap paragraph length: aim ~70 words / 1–3 sentences for online-first text; never more than 5 sentences.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.), "keep it short" table* (words in a paragraph: 70 or less; sentences: 1–3); *GOV.UK writing guidelines* ("Paragraphs should contain no more than 5 sentences"); *LSE Impact Blog* (4–5 sentence paragraphs).

**11. Use transitions, but sparingly and with varied placement.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* Transition words are "important tools for clarity and continuity, to be used but not over-used. Vary their placement in the sentence in order to avoid repetition."

## 2. HOW CLAIMS ARE PHRASED WITH AUTHORITY WITHOUT OVERCLAIMING

**12. Separate the *strength of evidence* from the *size of the effect*. Never let one word do both jobs.**
*Winton Centre for Risk and Evidence Communication, cited in Bennett Institute/CSaP (2020).* "Keep your expressions of the magnitude of uncertainty clearly separate from the magnitude of any evidence you are trying to communicate."
✗ "There is strong evidence of a large effect" as one undifferentiated claim; ✓ "The effect is large (8–11 points); the evidence base is three RCTs in one country."

**13. Use a two-dimensional calibration: evidence (limited / medium / robust) × agreement (low / medium / high).**
*IPCC AR5 Guidance Note (Mastrandrea et al., 2010), ¶8.* "Generally, evidence is most robust when there are multiple, consistent independent lines of high-quality evidence."
✓ "robust evidence, medium agreement" is a legitimate published formulation.

**14. Reserve quantified likelihood language for quantified uncertainty, and fix the terms in a glossary.**
*IPCC AR5 Guidance Note, ¶10, Table 1.* *virtually certain* 99–100%; *very likely* 90–100%; *likely* 66–100%; *about as likely as not* 33–66%; *unlikely* 0–33%.
✗ "likely" used loosely to mean "we think so."

**15. When evidence and understanding are overwhelming, drop the hedge and state the fact.**
*IPCC AR5 Guidance Note, ¶5.* "In some cases, it may be appropriate to describe findings for which evidence and understanding are overwhelming as statements of fact without using uncertainty qualifiers."
✓ "Population ageing will raise pension spending." (no "may," no "could")

**16. Provide a traceable account: for every headline claim, the text must say what the claim rests on.**
*IPCC AR5 Guidance Note, ¶2.* A description "of your evaluation of the type, amount, quality, and consistency of evidence and the degree of agreement."
✓ "This rests on two national panel studies and one quasi-experiment; none covers rural districts."

**17. Say whether a recommendation comes from one study or a synthesis.**
*ODI, "Policy briefs as a communication tool for development research" (2008), Table 1.* "Transparency of evidence underpinning policy recommendations (e.g. a single study, a synthesis of available evidence, etc.)."

**18. Mark opinion as opinion — explicitly, not by tone.**
*ODI (2008), Table 1.* Present "evidence-informed opinions" but with "clear identification of argument components that are opinion-based."
✓ "The evidence does not settle sequencing; in our judgement, the fee waiver should come first, because…"

**19. Match your verb to your evidence design: causal, associational, or projective.**
*UK Government Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics".* Causality: *affect, cause, consequence, effect, impact.* Relationship only: *association, correlation, corresponding, equivalent, parallel.* Provisional: *expect, believe, think, predict, envisage, forecast.*
✗ "The reform impacted attainment" (from a cross-section); ✓ "Attainment was higher in reform districts."

**20. Hedge with verbs, not with adverb pile-ups. *Suggest* and *indicate* are the calibrated workhorses.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 9.* "The verbs *suggest* and *indicate* let you state a claim about which you are less than 100-percent certain, but confident enough to propose."
✗ "There seems to be some evidence to suggest that certain differences could possibly derive from…"

**21. Delete the intensifiers before you delete the hedges. The absence of a hedge is itself the strongest intensifier.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 9.* "The most common intensifier, however, is the absence of a hedge." "Confident writers use intensifiers less often than they use hedges."
✗ *clearly, obviously, undoubtedly, of course, inevitably, key, crucial, fundamental.*

**22. Never open a claim with *obviously*, *it is clear that*, *there is no question that*.**
*Williams & Bizup, Quick Tip.* "When most readers read a sentence that begins with something like *obviously, undoubtedly, it is clear that*… they reflexively think the opposite."

**23. Cut compulsive hedging; keep only hedges that carry information.**
*Pinker, The Sense of Style / "Why Academics Stink at Writing" (2014).* Pinker distinguishes "compulsive hedging" from genuine qualifying: "sometimes a writer has no choice but to hedge a statement."
✗ "It may arguably be the case that, in some respects, X could perhaps tend to…"

**24. Do not assert; argue. Go easy on the *oughts* and *shoulds*.**
*The Economist Style Guide (12th ed.), Introduction.* "When you express opinions, do not simply make assertions. The aim is not just to tell readers what you think, but to persuade them; if you use arguments, reasoning and evidence, you may succeed. Go easy on the oughts and shoulds."

**25. Check the framing of every risk statement; consider stating the reciprocal.**
*IPCC AR5 Guidance Note, ¶4.* "A 10% chance of dying is interpreted more negatively than a 90% chance of surviving… Consider reciprocal statements to avoid value-laden interpretations."

**26. State the limits in the text, not only in a footnote.**
*Bennett Institute / CSaP, How to communicate effectively to policy makers (2020).* "Don't forget to be explicit about any caveats, methodological limitations or sources of information and be upfront about whether there is any scientific uncertainty and what the nature of that uncertainty is."

## 3. HOW EVIDENCE AND EXAMPLES ARE DEPLOYED

**27. Do not overload a sentence with numbers.**
*UK Government Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics".* "Do not overload sentences with lots of numbers."
✓ One comparison per sentence.

**28. Cap the digits in a paragraph — roughly eight, and no more than twelve including years.**
*Sarah Cohen, Numbers in the Newsroom (IRE).* "Limit yourself to no more than 12 digits, including dates such as 2012, in a single paragraph"; the tighter working rule she gives is eight, which fits "a year and two substantive numbers."
✗ "Spending rose from EUR 4,318m in 2011 to EUR 6,742m in 2019, or 2.4% to 3.1% of GDP, against an OECD average of 3.6%." (24 digits)

**29. Round to the precision your data support.**
*UK Government Analysis Function.* Use "3.5 million" rather than "3,546,882"; "Users find it difficult to process long, complex numbers."

**30. Never report a change without an anchor.**
*UK Government Analysis Function.* "Do not report on changes without discussing the context. For example, if you report a 2% rise, help the user understand whether this is typical or unusual in comparison to previous statistics, to other countries, or in respect to policy targets." *Cohen* names the four anchors: a standard or goal, history, portion of the whole, other places.

**31. Interpret the number for the reader; do not leave the inference to them.**
*IDRC / policy-brief practice and UK Analysis Function.* "Good commentary should help users to understand and interpret the messages from the statistics."
✓ "…a gap of 11 points — roughly one full year of schooling."

**32. If a paragraph is all numbers and no people, shorten it drastically or rewrite it.**
*Sarah Cohen.* "If you have a story filled with numbers — and not people — it needs to be really, really short." Consistent with *Billig*: populate the text with people doing things.

**33. Introduce a country case with the mechanism, not the label.**
*Bardach & Patashnik, A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis* — "smart practices," not "best practices": establish whether a practice worked exceptionally well **and why**, and identify its vulnerabilities.
✗ "Estonia is a good example." ✓ "Estonia cut processing time from 18 days to 3 **because** a single identifier let agencies pre-fill forms — a precondition Country X does not yet meet."

**34. Every example must carry a *because*. If the example can be deleted without losing a causal claim, it was decoration.**
*Derived from Bardach's smart-practice test* + *Economist Style Guide*: a good article is "a series of paragraphs that follow logically in order and, ideally, will suffer if even one sentence is cut out."

**35. When citing a policy tried elsewhere, state its key design elements and its local applicability.**
*Lavis et al., SUPPORT Tools 13, "Preparing and using policy briefs" (2009).* Assess "key elements of the policy option if it has been tried elsewhere," and comment on "the local applicability of the findings."

**36. Do not run lists of countries. Group them by mechanism, and name only the cases you will explain.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.), "Commonly misused words":* "*Across* is ambiguous in such expressions as 'data on unemployment across countries'. Make clear whether you mean data on regions within countries, on each of several countries, or on a group of countries."
✗ "Reforms were adopted in Chile, Estonia, Korea, Portugal and Spain." ✓ "Five countries used the same instrument — a capped voucher — but only where an inspectorate already existed (Chile, Estonia, Korea)."

**37. Collect only data that become information that become evidence for *this* problem.**
*Bardach & Patashnik, Step 2 "Assemble some evidence".* Data are worth collecting only insofar as they can be turned into information and then into evidence relevant to the problem.

**38. Use a figure where a figure earns its place — policy readers respond to them.**
*ODI (2008).* Over 80% of surveyed policymakers found graphs or explanatory diagrams helpful, and visually stimulating briefs were rated more highly. Reciprocally, *OECD 3rd ed. drafting checklist*: "Are the tables and figures useful? Are they properly sourced?"

**39. Write to a 30–60 minute reading budget, and make the document stand alone.**
*ODI (2008):* most policymakers "spend just 30 to 60 minutes reading information on a particular issue." *Bennett Institute/CSaP:* "the brief must stand on its own" — assume appendices are not read.

**40. Give figure and table titles that stand alone.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* Titles should be "clear and concise… enough information to enable the table or chart to stand on its own"; unique; jargon-free; and, in general, under 55 characters.

## 4. SENTENCE-LEVEL CRAFT

**41. Make main characters the subjects of your verbs; make their important actions the verbs.**
*Williams & Bizup, Principles of Clarity 1 and 2.*
✗ "The Federalists' argument in regard to the destabilization of government by popular democracy was based on…" ✓ "The Federalists argued that popular democracy destabilized government because…"

**42. If clarity principles conflict, favour old-before-new.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 5.* "If you have to choose among them, favor the third… for readers, a passage's overall cohesion trumps the clarity of individual sentences."

**43. Turn *-ion* and *-ment* nouns back into verbs.**
*European Commission, How to write clearly, Hint 6* ("Cut out excess nouns — verb forms are livelier"): *carry out an evaluation of* → *evaluate*; *conduct a review of* → *review*. *OECD 3rd ed.*: "A rise in unemployment was recorded last month" → "Unemployment rose last month."

**44. Run a nominalisation audit: if 5% or more of your words are nominalisations, rewrite.**
*Helen Sword, "Zombie Nouns" and The Writer's Diet.* Nominalisations "cannibalize active verbs, suck the lifeblood from adjectives and substitute abstract entities for human beings." Where a technical abstraction must stay, "surround them with concrete nouns and active verbs."

**45. Populate the prose with people and institutions that act.**
*Michael Billig, Learn to Write Badly (2013).* Nominalising lets a writer "rhetorically sidestep… the work of demonstrating that it exists"; social scientists should "populate their texts — to write about people rather than things."
✗ "Marginalisation persists." ✓ "Employers still refuse to interview applicants with foreign surnames."

**46. Prefer the active voice and name the agent — especially in "it is considered that" constructions.**
*European Commission, Hint 8.* "It is considered that tobacco advertising should be banned in the EU. **Who considers?** The writer, the Commission, the public, the medical profession?" *OECD 3rd ed.*: the active voice "is more forceful and convincing."

**47. But do not apply a passive ban mechanically — know what a passive actually is.**
*Geoffrey Pullum, "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice" (Chronicle, 2009):* of Strunk and White's four illustrative pairs, "three out of the four are mistaken diagnoses." A passive is legitimate when the agent is obvious or when it puts old information first (Williams, Lesson 3).

**48. Average 15–20 words a sentence, and vary the length.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.):* "A good average sentence length is 15 to 20 words… too many short phrases in quick succession can be distracting, so vary your sentence length." *EC:* "1 sentence = 20 words on average (but sprinkle in a few short sentences!)." *GOV.UK:* "Check sentences with more than 25 words to see if you can split them."

**49. Give sentences strong endings; do not bury the point mid-sentence.**
*European Commission, Hint 5.* "Try to give your sentences strong endings — that's the bit readers will remember." *OECD 3rd ed.:* "Place emphatic words at the end of the sentence. In the middle, they will be swallowed up."
✗ "The problem must be immediately solved." ✓ "The problem must be solved immediately."

**50. Keep subject and verb together; do not open with a long subordinate clause or bury dependent clauses mid-sentence.**
*Gopen & Swan* (minimise material between subject and verb); *OECD 3rd ed.* ("Do not start a sentence with a subordinate clause"; "Do not bury long dependent clauses in mid-sentence").
✗ "The Delegation reserves the right, in the event of the data not being available in time, to postpone the workshop." ✓ "The Delegation reserves the right to postpone the workshop if the data are not available in time."

**51. Apply the six concision cuts to every paragraph.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 9, "Six Principles of Concision".* Delete meaningless words; delete doubled words; delete what readers infer; replace a phrase with a word; change negatives to affirmatives; delete useless adjectives and adverbs.
Meaningless-word list: *kind of, actually, particular, really, certain, various, virtually, basically, generally, given, practically.*

**52. Put statements in positive form.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* "Negative constructions are often wordy… they oblige readers to imagine the positive alternative and then cancel it out."
✗ "The economy did not experience as substantial a negative impact as had been anticipated." ✓ "The economy suffered less than expected."

**53. Convert throat-clearing phrases into content, and delete false starts.**
*OECD 3rd ed.:* avoid *it should be noted that, it goes without saying that, at the outset, the fact of the matter*; *There is a need for extra study* → *The government must study this further.* *EC, Hint 4:* cut *as is well known, it is generally accepted that.*

## 5. WHAT TO AVOID (NAMED BAD PRACTICES)

**54. Throat-clearing openings.** *The Economist Style Guide (12th ed.), Introduction:* "Do not spend several sentences clearing your throat, setting the scene or sketching in the background. Introduce the facts as you tell the story."
✗ "This paper seeks to explore some of the many complex issues surrounding…"

**55. Signposting excess and metadiscourse.** *Pinker, The Sense of Style:* signposting is the habit of "say what you're going to say, say it, and then say what you've said"; metadiscourse is "writing about the writing." *Williams:* "Everything you write needs metadiscourse, but too much buries" the substance.
✗ "In this section, we will first review the literature, before turning to…" ✓ "Three mechanisms explain the gap. The first is enrolment."

**56. Elegant variation.** *Fowler, The King's English (1906) / Modern English Usage (1926)* — vary a word only "when there is some awkwardness, such as ambiguity or noticeable monotony." *EC, Hint 4:* "not changing words just for 'style'… you could confuse your reader." *OECD 3rd ed.:* "Repeat a word if it is the best word."
✗ *the reform… the measure… the initiative… the intervention* for one thing.

**57. False parallelism, including rhetorical "X rather than Y".** *Williams & Bizup, Lesson 10:* "Coordinate elements that are parallel both in grammar **and in sense**." A contrast is not an argument (*Economist*: "do not simply make assertions").
Mechanical check: for every *rather than / not X but Y*, confirm (i) X and Y are the same kind of thing, and (ii) someone actually holds Y.
✗ "The problem is structural rather than technical." ✓ "Two-thirds of delays occur before a technical decision is taken; the binding constraint is staffing."

**58. Over-qualification and adjective inflation.** *OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.):* "Think twice before using adjectives such as: *relevant, suitable, appropriate, serious, broad, effective, positive, meaningful, significant, major, main, particularly, obvious*." "Remove weak intensifiers and qualifiers such as: *very, quite, rather, actually, completely, definitely, so*."

**59. Buzzwords and business speak.** *GOV.UK A–Z style guide, "words to avoid":* *deliver, drive, foster, key, robust, leverage, tackle, transform, utilise, facilitate, empower, overarching, streamline, dialogue, impact (as verb)* — "we lose trust from our users if we write government 'buzzwords' and jargon." *OECD 3rd ed.* independently bans *impact* as a verb, and *drive, deliver, foster, leverage* in their vogue senses.

**60. Acronym spatter.** *The Economist Style Guide:* "Do not use spatterings of abbreviations and acronyms simply in order to cram more words in"; "There is no need to give the initials of an organisation if it is not referred to again." *OECD 3rd ed.:* keep them "to an absolute minimum."

**61. Data dumping.** *UK Analysis Function* + *Cohen*: choose the numbers that carry the argument and delete the rest; a table is not an argument. *HKS (Brodsky):* "it is not recommended that you insert the [analysis] table in lieu of good writing."

**62. Foreign and Latin tags.** *OECD Style Guide (4th ed.), "Choosing the right words":* avoid *ceteris paribus, inter alia, ipso facto* — "alternatives exist in English." *Orwell's rule 5*, reproduced in the *Economist Style Guide*.

**63. Curse of knowledge.** *Pinker (2014):* "a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know"; the remedy is to have non-experts read the draft. *OECD 3rd ed. drafting checklist:* "reread your own text, and have a non-specialist read it as well."

**64. Being hectoring, boastful, chatty or didactic.** *The Economist Style Guide:* "Those who disagree with you are not necessarily stupid or insane. Nobody needs to be described as silly: let your analysis show that he is." Avoid opening too many sentences with *Compare, Consider, Note, Remember* — "readers will think they are reading a textbook."

## 6. STRUCTURE OF POLICY ARGUMENTS

**65. Order every section: shared context → problem (condition + cost) → claim.**
*Williams & Bizup, Lesson 7.* A problem is a *condition* plus a *cost*: "Readers have to see the condition and cost together before they recognize a problem."
✓ "Alcohol has long been part of college life [context]. But binge drinking is spreading [condition]… it has been cited in at least six deaths [cost]. We must control its worst costs by educating students [claim]."

**66. Find the cost by asking "So what?" of your own condition sentence — and answer it in the introduction.**
*Williams & Bizup.* "You get just one shot at answering that question: in your introduction."

**67. Quantify the problem before you write a word of it.**
*HKS Communications Program, "How to Write a Policy Memo" (Brodsky).* "Our school district must deal with overcrowding" is an issue; "elementary school classrooms in the district will be oversubscribed by 5 students per class next academic year" is a problem. "A quantifiable problem statement will provide clarity, focus and urgency for the rest of your memo."

**68. Split the space roughly half problem, half analysis-and-recommendation.**
*HKS (Brodsky).* "Spend about half of your space illustrating the problem, and the other half explaining your analysis and recommendations."

**69. Bottom line up front.**
*HKS (Brodsky):* state problem and solution in the first paragraph. *OECD 3rd ed.:* the inverted pyramid "puts the most important information first — generally the conclusion or recommendations." *EC, Hint 3:* "If your outline includes a summary, begin with that… Put it at the beginning because that is the first (and sometimes the only) part that people will read."

**70. Prefer problem–cause–solution over chronology.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* "Findings and recommendations are more compelling than the history of how they were reached."

**71. Derive the recommendation: state the criteria, apply them to options, show the trade-off, then decide.**
*Bardach & Patashnik, Eightfold Path* (define the problem; assemble evidence; construct alternatives; select criteria; project outcomes; confront trade-offs; decide; tell your story). *HKS (Brodsky):* "tell the story of your analysis: what criteria did you use to decide? Why is your recommendation the best pathway? Are there counterarguments to be considered?"
✗ A recommendations box whose contents appear nowhere in the analysis.

**72. Where the evidence does not select an option, present options with their benefits, harms and costs rather than a recommendation.**
*Lavis et al., SUPPORT Tools 13 (2009).* Briefs should "describe the problem, costs and consequences of options" and present "what is known (and not known)," without concluding with specific recommendations. Decide deliberately which stance you are taking — *honest broker* or *issue advocate* (*Bennett Institute/CSaP, 2020*).

**73. Make recommendations actionable and attached to a decision point.**
*ODI (2008).* "Recommendations must be actionable and clearly connected to specific decision-making junctures in the policy-making process" and must "provide the necessary information to differentiate between various policy options."

**74. Cover implementation: name the barriers and who removes them.**
*Lavis et al., SUPPORT Tools 13.* Identify barriers "at different levels, ranging from the consumer… through to healthcare providers, organisations, and broader systems," and describe strategies addressing them.

**75. Make headings informative claims, not topic labels.**
*European Commission, Hint 3.* "A heading such as 'Mergers need to be monitored more carefully' is more informative than 'Monitoring mergers'." *OECD 3rd ed.:* headings must "capture the central idea using keywords," "orient the reader," and "stand on their own."

**76. Use subheadings sparingly.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.).* "Avoid multiple headings, which can disorient readers: use subheadings sparingly to signpost key elements."

**77. Write the executive summary as a self-contained document: 850–1,000 words, main findings first, no figures, tables, notes or citations.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.), "Executive summaries".* "It should begin by stating the main take-away findings and messages," then issues covered, then labelled "Key findings" and "Key recommendations."

**78. Cap the whole document and defend the cap.**
*European Commission, Hint 4:* "1 document = 15 pages at the most." *Bennett Institute/CSaP:* "Think brevity and clarity: two pages is what you're aiming for" for a brief. The cost of ignoring this is measurable: of 1,611 World Bank policy reports (2008–12), **31% were never downloaded** and about 87% were never cited (*Doemeland & Trevino, 2014*).

**79. Run the revision checklist before release.**
*OECD Style Guide (3rd ed.), "Drafting checklist".* "Is every word meaningful and necessary? What can be deleted without loss of meaning or emphasis? Can I replace jargon with plain words? Could I use stronger action verbs? Am I using the active voice? Can I turn negative structures into positive ones? Can I shorten and vary sentence length? Are there too many lists or headings breaking up and complicating the text?"

**80. Final test — the one-paragraph test.**
*HKS (Brodsky).* Have a colleague read **only your first paragraph** and state the problem back to you. If they cannot, the problem statement is not yet specific or quantified. Then ask them to circle every sentence that was hard to read; those are the sentences to cut.

**Two research notes for the caller.** First, the OECD's prose-craft guidance is *not* in the current (4th, 2025) Style Guide: the 4th edition explicitly moved "guidance on good writing" to an internal OECD Communications Guidance Portal, keeping only rules and conventions. The citable OECD writing rules therefore come from the **3rd edition (2015)**, which remains publicly available and is what I have quoted throughout. Second, Chatham House, Brookings, RAND, IFS and CGD publish no substantive public prose-style guidance (Brookings defers to Chicago; RAND's public standards cover research quality, not writing), so I substituted equally authoritative and more specific sources — ODI's empirical study of what policymakers actually want, the UK Analysis Function statistics-writing standard, the IPCC calibrated-language note, and the SUPPORT Tools policy-brief protocol.
