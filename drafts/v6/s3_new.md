# 3. Policies to bring former teachers back

Governments have been legislating and campaigning for the return of former teachers for more than a quarter of a century. North Carolina (United States) suspended the pension penalty attached to re-employment in 1999 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024); the OECD recommended in 2005 that education systems maintain contact with teachers who had left, keep them informed of vacancies and provide targeted retraining (OECD, 2005); the Netherlands regulated a returner subsidy in 2017 (Ministerie van OCW, 2017); and the instruments adopted since 2022 run from the fixed-term contracts Portugal offers retired teachers to the collaborator status that Andalusia (Spain) created for them in February 2026 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024; Decreto 12/2026). Most of what is now in force was adopted or widened under post-pandemic staffing pressure, and very little of it has been evaluated. The record consists mainly of statutes, scheme regulations and administrative counts, produced by systems that differ in nearly everything except the population they address.

These instruments group into four families, ordered by the sequence in which a former teacher encounters them (Table 2). Permission governs whether a return is legally possible, through licence validity and refresh requirements, waiting periods, service thresholds and eligibility screens, and its levers belong to professional regulators and to pension legislation. Price governs what returning produces financially, through pension-salary concurrency, the salary step on which a returner is placed, and bonuses or subsidies paid to the teacher or to the school, and its levers belong to pension, civil-service and finance authorities. The job governs the shape of what is offered, from a full-time post to supply work or a defined role such as tutoring, and its levers belong to employers and schools. Reach governs how former teachers learn that any of this exists and how they are helped through it, and its levers belong to education ministries and their agencies. The correspondence with the exit motives set out in Section 2 is close but not complete. Permission answers the life-cycle and administrative barriers that accumulate during an absence, price answers the economic motives, the job answers the environmental motives that the evidence ranks highest, and reach answers no motive at all, since it is the channel through which an offer travels rather than the offer itself.

The evidence these families have produced is administrative rather than experimental, and one pattern runs across it. Among the many campaigns implemented to attract teachers back, both those providing incentives and those easing certification, there is suggestive evidence that they weigh less than changes to the legal rules of the game, which appear more successful in re-attracting teachers. The counts behind that reading measure different outcomes against different denominators, from a permanent post in a shortage subject to a casual placement or a subsidy grant, and none of them rests on a comparison with what would have happened otherwise. The pattern therefore orients a policy choice without settling it, and the sections below record for each instrument what its publisher counted as carefully as what the instrument did.

## 3.1 Permission

The first question a former teacher meets is whether returning is allowed. A licence expires during the absence, a pension statute imposes a wait, a service threshold excludes teachers who retired early, and a subject or geography screen limits where a return may take place. Instruments in this family remove or soften those conditions, and they cost the budget little at the point of adoption, which is part of why they are the most common re-entry measures in the record. The empirical warrant for the family is a study conducted for the education department of Michigan (United States), which linked certification and employment records for 141 810 certificate-holders and found that certificates had expired for 5.7% of those teaching in 2017/18, against 44.2% of those who had taught since 2013/14 but not in that year and 48.5% of those who had not taught since 2013/14 (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Roughly half of the visible reserve in that system would have to re-certify before returning.

One set of instruments acts on the licence, by waiving refresher requirements, by extending permission to teach without a current certificate, or by dropping the requirement that certification match the subject of the vacancy. A second acts on time and status, by shortening the break in service a pensioner must observe, by setting or keeping service thresholds, and by capping how much a returner may earn or work. The settings that result are documented for the systems examined here in Table 3. They are also, in most systems, written outside education law, in pension statutes and civil-service codes, which places the decisive parameters with authorities that do not answer for staffing.

**Georgia.** Georgia (United States) employs about 125 000 teachers and reported roughly 6 000 vacant or not-fully-certified positions when its legislature acted in 2022 (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). House Bill 385, codified at O.C.G.A. § 47-3-127.1, allows a certified teacher retired for at least a year, with at least 30 years of creditable service, to work full-time on a full pension, provided the post lies in one of the three highest-need content areas of the employer's regional education service agency as determined annually from five-year vacancy surveys; the employer pays both the employer and the employee pension contribution, and the returner accrues no further service. The statute set an expiry date and commissioned a performance audit before it. That audit counted 635 retired teachers working full-time under the law for at least one school year between fiscal years 2023 and 2025, in 133 of about 240 school systems, roughly 350 in any given year and under 1% of the workforce (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). Its method combined administrative pension and personnel records with a comparison of 12 other southern states and a census survey of school systems to which 163 of 236 responded, a response rate the auditors say should not be generalised to the population. The audit judged the effect on the shortage and on the pension fund alike to be minimal, and reported that 45% of responding systems had been stopped from making a hire by the statutory conditions, while 63% of non-users could not find candidates both willing to work full-time and meeting the criteria. It supports the finding that stacked eligibility screens hold take-up down; it cannot show what a looser design would have produced, since no counterfactual and no actuarial study of the alternative exists.

**Virginia.** Virginia (United States) has allowed retirees to draw salary and pension concurrently in designated critical-shortage posts since 2001, when the twelve-month break in service was set after analysis by the retirement system, the legislature's audit commission and their actuaries (Virginia Retirement System, 2026). The licence side was moved administratively rather than by statute. Executive Directive Three, signed on 1 September 2022, instructed the Superintendent of Public Instruction to use all discretion within law to issue teaching and renewal licences, including to teachers licensed in another state and retired teachers whose licences may have lapsed (Commonwealth of Virginia, 2022; SwailLandis, 2023). No count exists of licences issued under that instruction to returners with lapsed licences, and the statutory follow-through went to the adjacent margin, with universal licensure by reciprocity legislated in 2024 and 4 535 such licences granted to currently licensed teachers from other states (Virginia Department of Education, 2025). What Virginia does count is the wait. After the break in service for critical-shortage teacher posts was halved to six months in 2023, the retirement system recorded 107 retirees in such posts in 2022/23 and 154 by late October 2023, against 18 at the equivalent point of the previous year, and summarised the episode as a general doubling of returns (Virginia Retirement System, 2023; Virginia Retirement System, 2026). The case supports treating a lapsed licence as a discretionary administrative obstacle rather than a legal one, and it shows the wait to be the parameter whose movement is visible in the counts. It cannot support attributing the decline in the state's vacancy rate to either instrument, nor reading the reciprocity licences as returners, since they describe currently licensed teachers from elsewhere.

House Bill 1306 of 2026 would have cut the break in service for retired teachers from six months to one. The retirement system's fiscal impact statement observed that credible data on member behaviour under such programmes are lacking, and therefore assumed that every member reaching unreduced eligibility would retire at the earliest permitted date, describing the resulting figures as a lower bound. On that assumption the teacher plan's unfunded liability would rise from USD 11.18 billion to USD 13.91 billion, an increase of USD 2.73 billion, and the employer contribution rate from 12.20% to 14.63% of payroll, about USD 290.8 million a year.

The statement added the observation that matters most for re-entry policy, namely that the change "likely will not net additional active teachers but instead will reclassify a portion of the current active population", and that in a pooled plan the divisions using the route shift costs onto those that do not. House Appropriations struck the bill from the docket on 13 February 2026 by 22 votes to none.

**New Mexico.** The educational retirement system of New Mexico had 62 970 active members and 55 706 retirees and beneficiaries at 30 June 2024, and estimated when it legislated in 2022 that 12.9% of active members were already eligible to retire (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025b; New Mexico Legislative Finance Committee, 2022). House Bill 73 of 2022 and Senate Bill 133 of 2025 allow a retiree to work for any participating employer for up to 36 months, extended to 60 in 2025, with no limit on salary or hours and no suspension of the pension, subject to a 90-day layout, a prohibition on pre-arranged returns, board approval, and contributions that are not refunded and buy no further service credit (New Mexico Legislative Finance Committee, 2025). Participation on that route rose from 154 in fiscal 2023 to 361 in fiscal 2024 and 506 in fiscal 2025, reported by the pension board to a legislative oversight committee; across all four statutory routes, returners amount to under 6% of retirees and beneficiaries (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025b). No outcome evaluation exists. The legislature's finance committee has instead priced the design, warning in 2022 that the route incentivises earlier retirement, noting that the companion bill for other public employees removed that channel by restricting eligibility to those already retired on a fixed date, and costing the 2025 loosening at USD 2.22 million to USD 3.10 million a year in foregone contributions (New Mexico Legislative Finance Committee, 2022; 2025). The case shows what the most permissive design on record yields, and says nothing about posts filled, since the board counts participants rather than positions.

**Michigan.** Michigan (United States) inherited its nine-month waiting period from anti-double-dipping rules adopted in 2010, and district leaders pressed for its relaxation on staffing grounds, with the state teachers' union supporting the change while describing retiree return as a temporary and limited solution (Michigan Education Association, 2023). Public Act 184 of 2022 replaced a patchwork of critical-shortage exceptions with two bright-line conditions, a bona fide termination and nine consecutive months in retirement, after which a retiree may hold any public-school position with no earnings limit and no loss of pension or insurance subsidy; Public Act 147 of 2023 cut the wait to six months, permitted up to USD 15 100 of earnings during it, and carries an expiry in October 2028 that leaves the underlying framework permanent (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022; 2023). No official count of retirees working under either act has been published, since the state simplified the rule without building a reporting series around it. Michigan does hold the family's best measure of the problem. In the study of certificate-holders cited above, respondents who had previously taught were significantly more likely to select pension retention and salary as what would move them, while those who had never taught were the ones selecting a cheaper or easier route to certification and the availability of full-time posts (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). On this evidence, clearing the licence obstacle is necessary for people who have taught without being an incentive for them.

**New Zealand.** Registration in New Zealand is administered by the Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, whose standard-setting functions passed to the Secretary for Education in July 2026 (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026b). Provisions under Schedule 3 of the Education and Training Act 2020 operate together. A registered teacher without a current practising certificate may undertake relief teaching for up to 20 half-days a calendar year, the Council may issue an Extension to Teach beyond that limit where the certificate has expired and an offer of employment exists, and a returner renewing a certificate with a relief-teaching offer in hand is not required to complete the Teacher Education Refresh programme however long the absence has been, completing a future-ready teaching plan with the hiring principal instead (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026; New Zealand Legislation, 2020). Since July 2025 the government has also paid certificate fees and levies until 30 June 2028, a universal measure of NZD 53.3 million covering about 115 000 school and early-learning teachers rather than a subsidy aimed at returners (New Zealand Government, 2025b). No take-up has been published for any of these provisions, and the Council publishes no series of extensions granted or refresh exemptions. The design point the case supports is that a waiver keyed to an employer's offer converts the offer into the market test of whether a return is real, and that it can be written as a standing rule rather than an annual suspension.

**Ireland.** Ireland renews its permission measures one year at a time. The Department reported 2.1% of 74 611 allocated posts unfilled in November 2024, describing the level as low, and presented the measures extended in the same announcement as short-term (Department of Education and Youth, 2025b). Chapter 7 of Circular 0054/2019 limits the substitute cover a teacher on career break or job-sharing may provide, and that limit has been suspended for each school year since 2021/22, for 2025/26 by Information Note TC/IN 0005/2025, alongside a scheme allowing post-primary teachers on full contracts up to 35 additional hours of substitution a term (Department of Education, 2019; Department of Education and Youth, 2025). No count of career-break teachers substituting has been published. Two features of the case travel beyond it. The population activated consists of temporary leavers who have not resigned, the cheapest reserve any system can reach, and the annual form of the suspension means that neither schools nor teachers can plan around it, a limitation the responsible minister acknowledged in describing the measures as not long-term solutions (Department of Education and Youth, 2025b).

**France.** For a retired State civil servant, which a career public-school teacher in France is, the terms of re-employment are set by the pension code rather than by education law, and no exception for teaching has been enacted. Under the Code des pensions civiles et militaires de retraite, a retiree who does not meet the conditions for unlimited concurrent earnings may earn EUR 8 198.04 plus one third of the gross pension in a calendar year, above which the excess is deducted from the pension, and new rights accrue only where those conditions are met and a six-month carence is observed when the return is to the last employer, which for a public-school teacher means the education ministry itself (Service des Retraites de l'État, 2026). From 1 January 2027 an age-based test replaces the full-rate criterion for new pensioners. No official aggregate of retired teachers re-employed by regional education authorities exists, and a Senate finance committee inquiry into replacement capacity, which documented unreplaced absences rising by 49% in primary and 93.2% in secondary schools between 2018 and 2024, published none (Sénat, 2025). A general anti-abuse architecture can therefore operate as an unlegislated charge on re-entry, and repeated parliamentary initiative has not changed it, since a bill creating a reserve of retired teachers for replacement duty, tabled in May 2024 and endorsed by that committee, has never been examined (Sénat, 2024).

**Louisiana.** Louisiana (United States) screens shortage posts by a market test rather than by a subject list, and legislated its licence change inside a pension bill. Act 344 of 2026, in force from 1 July 2026, consolidates the return-to-work rules for all retirees of the teachers' retirement system and, on the permission margin, requires a retiree hired into a critical-shortage post to hold general teacher certification rather than certification in the shortage subject, adds further eligible areas including adult education and specialists for visually impaired students, and streamlines the advertising and certification steps through which an employer declares a shortage (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). The underlying test requires the post to be advertised and fewer than three certified applicants to have applied, and the 2024 act that preceded it added an explicit priority for a certified applicant who is not a retiree (Louisiana Legislature, 2024). No take-up has been published, and the act was weeks old when the research for this paper closed. The case supports separating certification matching, an adjustable screen, from certification itself, and it illustrates that a paper reading only licensure statutes would miss the licensing changes legislatures make. It supports no outcome claim, and the first evidence instrument written into the act is an actuarial report due before the 2034 session.

What this family teaches is bounded by what it does. Permission determines who may return and on what terms, and the counts show movement when a wait is shortened, as in Virginia, or when a permissive route is opened, as in New Mexico, while the systems that stack screens record take-up below 1% of their workforce and can name the screens their own employers found binding, as in Georgia. The Michigan evidence sets the limit of the family's ambition, since the people who want cheaper and easier certification are those who never taught, while former teachers rank pension retention and salary first. Permission is therefore best read as a precondition rather than as an inducement, and the same movements that open it carry a cost that pension actuaries, not education ministries, are the ones to price. That pricing is the subject of the next family.

## 3.2 Price

What a return pays is set by the interaction of two systems that were not designed together. A pension scheme decides whether a benefit continues, is abated or is suspended while its holder earns a salary again, and a pay scale decides which step a returner is placed on after an absence. The sum of those decisions, together with the waiting periods and licensing costs of the family above, is what this paper terms the price of a return. Instruments in this family move that price, either by removing a penalty already in the rules or by adding money on top, paid to the teacher as a supplement or allowance and to the school as a subsidy for making the appointment.

The family divides between rules and payments, and the two have produced very different records. Rule changes generate administrative data as a by-product, which is why the largest documented return flows in this paper belong to them, and they also generate actuarial objections, since a rule that makes returning attractive makes retiring earlier attractive as well. Payments generate budget lines and, in several systems, unspent budgets. No study anywhere prices re-entry incentives quasi-experimentally, so every figure below is a count of take-up against a stated denominator rather than an estimate of what the money bought.

**North Carolina.** The retirement system of North Carolina allows an unreduced benefit after 30 years of service at any age, which concentrates retirements sharply at that threshold, and the state legislated in 1999 to pre-empt the retirement wave then approaching. Outside the window, a retiree returning full-time forfeited the pension and retiree health coverage, and part-time work was permitted only under an earnings cap of half the previous full-time salary; the window suspended that constraint, allowing a full salary alongside a full annuity, restricted in its first year to low-performing schools and interim roles and opened to all schools from June 2000. It was extended repeatedly and expired in autumn 2009 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Roughly 3 500 teachers returned over the decade, 2.1% of a sample of about 169 000 teachers in 2 900 schools; more than half of schools hired at least one; returners averaged 16.5 more years of experience than other teachers and went disproportionately to schools with higher shares of economically disadvantaged and Black students without any rule directing them there; and 54% returned to the school they had retired from. The evaluation is descriptive, on administrative records with year fixed effects, and its authors state that they cannot say whether the policy induced any return, since the structure of the policy provides no natural experiment. The state's own actuaries have since priced the mechanism from the other side. Scoring a 2025 bill that would revive a narrower version, they assumed that the design would raise the probability of unreduced retirement at first eligibility by between 2.5 and 10 percentage points, at a cost of 0.01% to 0.02% of pay, or USD 4.10 million in 2025/26 rising to USD 4.66 million in 2029/30 (North Carolina General Assembly, 2025). The successor programme that ran from 2019 to 2021, confined to Title I schools and schools graded D or F and paying returners at the first step of the scale, published no participation count at all (North Carolina General Assembly, 2019).

**Portugal.** Portugal legislated for two margins at once in 2024, within a package of measures the government described as exceptional and temporary. Article 5 of Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024 authorises fixed-term contracts with retired teachers in shortage recruitment groups or under-staffed schools, subject to ministerial authorisation grounded in exceptional public interest and closed to teachers retired more than five years; the returner keeps the pension and receives compensation at the first step of the salary scale, pro-rated to the teaching hours assigned, with a full timetable defined as 20 weekly hours in pre-school and the first cycle and 14 hours at other levels, and the annual volume is fixed by a joint order of the finance, public administration and education ministries. Article 6 pays a supplement of EUR 750 a month to teachers who already meet the conditions for retirement and remain in effective teaching duties to the end of the school year. In 2024/25, 63 retired teachers were accepted under Article 5 against a government target of 200, of whom 55 were placed. The ministerial balance sheet for 2025/26 records 1 008 teachers returning after at least a year away, on a definition much broader than the Article 5 route, and 2 232 teachers postponing retirement under Article 6, with more than 2 000 drawing the supplement at a cost of about EUR 10 million for the school year (ECO, 2026; Executive Digest, 2026). The comparison the case supports is asymmetric and hedged. Under a single law and at broadly comparable money, the instrument that prevented an exit attracted an order of magnitude more takers than the instrument that reversed one, and the government subsequently narrowed the supplement to shortage areas for 2026/27 on the ground that its blanket form had in many cases not been justified (RTP, 2026). What the case cannot support is a verdict on the EUR 750, which was never offered to returners, or a reading of the 1 008 as retirees returning under Article 5.

**Austria.** Austria prices the return of its former civil-servant teachers by an explicit rule against double counting. Periods of service that already generate a pension are excluded from prior-service crediting throughout the federal service and for teachers employed by the Länder, so an established civil-servant teacher who returns draws the pension together with the entry salary of a beginning teacher, while a former contract-scheme teacher has earlier teaching service counted and broadly recovers pre-retirement pay; the ministry has confirmed the exclusion as deliberate policy rather than oversight (BMBWF, 2023). A second charge sits in general pension law, under which a person drawing an early old-age pension loses it for any month in which earnings exceed the marginal-earnings threshold of EUR 551.10 in 2026, while a person past the standard pension age faces no earnings limit at all (oesterreich.gv.at, 2026). Since January 2026 a partial-pension option allows working time to be reduced by a quarter, a half or three quarters with the corresponding share of the pension drawn (Pensionsversicherungsanstalt, 2026). The Court of Audit counted 1 062 teachers in retirement or retired status resuming teaching duties nationwide across the six school years to 2023/24, in a workforce of about 123 000, concentrated in 2021/22 and 2022/23 and not sustained thereafter (Rechnungshof, 2025). Those counts cannot be attributed to the crediting rule, since there is no design variation to exploit, and the qualitative work on Austrian returners finds the decision biographically rooted rather than pay-driven (Resch, 2025a; Resch, 2025b).

**China.** China attaches money to movement rather than to return. The Silver Age plans, established in 2018 and extended in 2023 to a corps of about 120 000 across five strands of the education system, recruit retired principals, teaching-research staff and senior teachers, generally aged 65 or under, to serve in schools in formerly poor, ethnic-minority and border counties (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). The 2025 implementation notice sets a recruitment target of 7 000 for compulsory education, after 6 000 in 2023 and 7 000 in 2024, and specifies the money as working funds of CNY 20 000 per person per year covering the work subsidy, travel and accident insurance premiums rather than as salary, with the pension unchanged and the receiving county providing accommodation and assessing performance; provinces with low completion rates face investigation and public criticism (MOE and MOF, 2025). One design rule closes the deadweight case directly, since re-employment of a retiree by their own former employer does not count under the plan. No figure for actual recruitment against any annual target has been published in Chinese or English, and the cumulative claim of more than 20 000 recruits by mid-2023 comes from the sponsoring ministry. A small research literature now exists without measuring effects. A qualitative case study of one rural primary school in Gansu, with ten interviewees and reported intercoder agreement of 0.92, describes retirees working through classroom teaching and school governance on one side and collective lesson planning and monthly training of local teachers on the other, and states that only successful practice was examined and that students were not assessed (Cheng, 2025). An implementation study using a policy-process framework identifies ambiguity in the policy text, uneven administrative capacity and constraints on the retirees themselves (Zhang, Jiang and Jiang, 2025), a formation study places the plans in the response to population ageing and records one province recruiting 378 lecturing teachers against a plan of 200 (Jiang, 2023), and a document analysis locates them within a wider redistribution strategy (Zhang, Fang and Jiang, 2025). A prominent education researcher has warned publicly against treating silver-age teachers as a main force rather than a supplement (CCTV, 2025).

**Netherlands.** The Netherlands attached its money to the school rather than to the teacher. From November 2017 to October 2020 a regulation paid a school board EUR 2 500 for each returner appointed for at least six months who had not taught for at least twelve consecutive months, announced as a way of activating a hidden reserve then estimated at 31 000 or more in primary education alone, with an initial ceiling of EUR 1.25 million and room for 500 grants (Ministerie van OCW, 2017). Just over 100 returners were subsidised in 2018 and nearly 200 cumulatively by autumn 2019, and the regulator responded to the under-subscription by writing it into law, lowering the annual ceilings for 2019 and 2020 to amounts covering at most 120 and 100 grants because the budget had not been exhausted (Ministerie van OCW, 2019a; Tweede Kamer, 2019). Final grant counts for those two years were never published, and the instrument was never evaluated. The activation goal it carried has become progressively less visible as it was absorbed into larger structures, appearing among the fundable but least-realised activities of the regional staffing partnerships evaluated in 2022 and absent from the second interim evaluation of their successor network in December 2025 (De Jong et al., 2022; Berenschot, 2025b). The case cannot support the conclusion that subsidies do not work, since this one was small, paid to boards and never tested. It supports the narrower finding that a returner budget can go unclaimed in the system with the best-measured reserve in the record.

**Hesse.** German re-entry policy is made by the Länder and is priced through pension law rather than through bonuses. Thirteen of the sixteen Länder actively approach pensioned teachers, and the recurring instrument is relief from the earnings limit that otherwise offsets teaching income against the pension, with non-pensionable supplements of about 10% as a second-order addition where the limit still binds; one Land abolished its mandatory retirement age, another proposed a supplement of 20% for teachers continuing three years past 65, and none imposes an age ceiling on employed retirees (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023; Deutsches Schulportal, 2023b). Just over 5 000 pensioners were teaching nationwide when the sixteen ministries were surveyed in 2023, under 1% of a workforce of about 800 000. Hesse, which employs about 67 000 teachers, publishes the only dated series, from answers by its minister to parliamentary questions, showing teachers aged 66 and over in its primary schools rising from 130 in 2018 to 282 at 1 October 2024, with parallel increases at other school types (news4teachers, 2025). No Land publishes an evaluation, definitions of a reactivated teacher are not harmonised across the survey, and Hesse attributes the roughly 550 posts filled by its short-term package to retiree hiring, service extensions and part-time increases together rather than to retirees alone (Hessisches Ministerium für Kultus, Bildung und Chancen, 2024).

**England.** England's pension charge on returning is narrow and shrinking. Only final-salary benefits are abated, so that a retiree drawing them may earn up to an index-linked salary of reference less the annual pension before the pension is suspended for the remainder of the scheme year, while the career-average benefits that cover all service since 2015 are not abated at all, and compliance runs through employers' monthly data returns or a certificate of re-employment (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.). No count of pensioners returning under these rules is published. The most informative episode is a decision not to act. Asked in January 2022 whether abatement would be suspended for retired teachers covering pandemic absence, as emergency legislation had done for the health service scheme, the government declined, reasoning that returning retirees would be engaged by supply agencies which tend not to participate in the scheme and that short or part-time appointments would fall below the threshold in any case (UK Parliament, 2022b). The barrier was left standing because the labour market routes around it, through agencies that sit outside the scheme and therefore outside pension accrual. The national return flow of 17 274 teachers to state-funded schools in 2024/25, the highest in a decade, owes nothing to the rule and is not a measure of it (Department for Education, 2026).

**Ontario.** Ontario (Canada) prices quantity rather than pay. Under the plan rules, a retired teacher may work 50 days in a school year for a participating employer without any effect on the pension, and forfeits the pension for subsequent months in which work continues, with tracking left to the member (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). The cap has moved in both directions. It stood at 20 days before 1990, was raised to 95 days for the first three post-retirement years during the shortage of that period, and was reduced to a flat 50 days from September 2012, a change announced in 2010 after journalism documented CAD 108.3 million spent on retired teachers by the ten largest boards in a single year, a daily cost for retirees well above that of new teachers, and newly certified graduates outnumbering available openings (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). During the pandemic it was temporarily restored to 95 days for three school years, with monthly reporting by boards to the ministry as a condition set by the teachers' federation, and returned to 50 days on 1 July 2023 (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; OTF, 2023). The parameter moves only by agreement between the plan's two co-sponsors, and in March 2024 the federation declined a fourth extension, its president stating that the pension plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers and is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges (Ontario Teachers' Federation, 2024; Alphonso, 2024). Against 142 000 retired members, about 60 had expressed interest in the pandemic arrangement by January 2022 (Global News, 2022), and the monthly returns collected as its price have never been published.

What this family teaches depends on whether a price is removed or added. Where a penalty on concurrent pension and salary is removed outright, as in North Carolina, returns are visible in the administrative record and stop when the removal stops. Where money is added on top, as in the Netherlands and Portugal, the sums have been small relative to a teaching salary and the budgets have gone unclaimed or have performed better at holding teachers in post than at bringing them back. Where the price is set inside a pension code, as in Austria, France and Ontario, the education authority is not the authority that can change it. Cost-effectiveness is beyond what the family can deliver, since no system has estimated what any of this money or forgone revenue bought, and the only quantified counterweights in the record are actuarial statements of what a loosening would cost the fund (Louisiana Legislative Auditor, 2024; Virginia Retirement System, 2026). Price also leaves untouched the motive that leaver surveys place first, which is the shape of the job.

## 3.3 The job

Instruments in this family change what a returner is asked to do. They range from the intensity of the post, part-time hours, job-share, supply and reserve work, to roles that are not classroom posts at all, such as tutoring a small group to a protocol or mentoring novice teachers. The lever belongs to employers and schools rather than to ministries or pension authorities, which makes this the family that education systems can move fastest and the one whose parameters are least often written down. It also addresses the motive the evidence ranks highest, since workload, working hours and control of time drive the resignations of working-age teachers more than pay does, and the returner segment with the highest documented return rate has been the one seeking part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Section 2).

The family carries the only randomised evidence in this paper, and that evidence tracks role specification closely. Where the role was scripted, dosed and trialled, an effect was measured; where the role was left to the judgement of the person filling it, the trial returned an indeterminate answer; and where a tutoring role was taken to system scale, the measured effects fell to a fraction of trial values. The corresponding weakness is compositional, since none of the trials or programmes below publishes what share of the people doing the work were former teachers.

**Japan.** Japan institutionalised the retiree reserve rather than campaigning for it. Boards of education conventionally re-employ teachers after mandatory retirement, and retirees dominate the substitute and adjunct pool; in the one prefecture studied in depth, 52.7% of part-time adjunct hires were in their sixties and 7.1% in their seventies, while only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools sought full-time re-employment (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024). The national shortage survey now records that reserve thinning. In the shortage survey published in March 2026, counting a shortfall of 3 827 teachers, or 0.45%, at 1 May 2025 against 2 065 and 0.25% in 2021, the ministry asked every board to rate candidate causes; of roughly 68 boards answering, 29 said that the number of mandatory retirees wishing to be re-employed had fallen short of expectations and 27 said the same of already re-employed staff wishing to continue (MEXT, 2026). The ministry's stated responses include creating further opportunities for experienced retired teachers, redistributing non-teaching duties in view of the physical demands, and piloting a supply-teacher system of the Japanese type. The case is the strongest official evidence in the record that a staffing model resting on retirees depletes on the retirees' schedule, and the raising of the civil-service retirement age from 60 to 65 by 2031 is converting the category back into ordinary employment. No current national count of re-employed teachers exists.

**New South Wales.** New South Wales (Australia) offered returners the kind of work its schools needed covered. From November 2023 its education department approached teachers who had resigned in the previous five years and placed them in casual and temporary roles, with permanent posts and small-group tutoring also open, and with accreditation reactivation and the working-with-children clearance handled inside the same contact (NSW Department of Education, 2026). Of the 145 teachers placed by May 2024 across more than 600 schools, about 60% were returning from retirement, and the placements were casual and temporary rather than permanent (NSW Government, 2024); the contact funnel that produced them is discussed with the reach family in Section 3.4. No programme-specific payment was attached, and no evaluation exists. The case supports the observation that the offer and the take-up matched, in that a system offering casual work drew mainly retirees willing to do casual work, and it cannot support any inference about the permanent workforce, nor about the fall in vacancies recorded in the same period, which followed a large pay settlement as well.

**Bahamas.** The Bahamas built a standing register rather than a campaign. Facing a shortfall of about 300 teachers across 162 public schools on 24 islands, the ministry created a teacher reservist programme on the platform of the existing supply-teacher scheme, in which retired educators, trained teachers who left for other work and recent university graduates enlist once and are called on by schools as needed, with a multi-agency task force fast-tracking vetting, credential assessment and public service commission processing (ZNS Bahamas, 2026; Cooper, 2026). No reservist-specific payment has been published. By August 2026 about 100 Bahamians had been enlisted for the September school year, a figure that belongs to the recruitment drive as a whole rather than to the reserve alone, with 42 applications then before the commission (The Tribune, 2026). No enrolment count for the register itself, no retiree share and no cost have been published. The design point is institutional, since a register that a former teacher joins once removes the need for a campaign to find the same people again each year.

**Victoria.** The largest deployment of an out-of-service teacher pool into a defined role is the tutor learning initiative of Victoria (Australia), announced in October 2020 as a learning-recovery measure with an initial package of AUD 250 million and extended to AUD 1.2 billion over 2021 to 2025. It called explicitly on pre-service teachers, teachers on leave, retired teachers and casual relief teachers, and employed them as qualified tutors delivering small-group instruction of two to five students during school hours (Premier of Victoria, 2020). More than 4 100 tutors worked in government schools in 2021 and more than 5 150 in 2022, and over 127 000 government-school students were tutored in 2023; the share of tutors who were retired teachers has never been published, which is the case's central evidentiary gap. The Victorian Auditor-General's Office, comparing the learning gains of tutored students with those of non-tutored students at similar baseline scores, found no significant improvement, with the median year 3 tutored student gaining 7.1 in reading against 7.4 for a comparable non-tutored student, and reported that fewer than a third of schools had reached fully effective practice on targeting and fit by 2023 although 96% delivered tutoring on time (Victorian Auditor-General's Office, 2024). The audit locates the failure in role specification rather than in tutoring as such, which is the variable this family turns on.

**United States (Number Rockets).** The clearest test of whether former teachers can deliver a specified instructional role at scale was conducted in four urban districts of the southern United States. A scripted second-tier mathematics protocol for at-risk first-grade students was delivered in groups of two or three, three times a week over 17 weeks, by tutors recruited through a temporary services agency and trained in a single day with short follow-ups; 77.3% of them reported being retired teachers or working as substitutes, 44.0% had five years of teaching experience or less, and 27 replacement tutors were hired mid-year against 84 trained originally (Rolfhus et al., 2012). The cluster-randomised trial covered 994 students in 76 schools and measured an effect of 0.34 standard deviations, with no spillover to other students, at an estimated cost of about USD 10 000 per school and USD 700 per student (Rolfhus et al., 2012; Gersten et al., 2015). Two qualifications travel with the result. The tutor statistic combines retirees with substitutes and no split was published, so the trial cannot isolate former teachers as a class. And the effect must be quoted at its at-scale value, since an expanded meta-analysis of 265 randomised trials finds effects declining almost linearly with programme size, from 0.55 standard deviations below 100 students to 0.14 above 1 000, with preferred estimates for programmes at scale of 0.16 to 0.21 standard deviations, between a third and a half of the pooled figure; the same analysis puts the penalty for tutors who are community members or volunteers at 0.16 standard deviations relative to a teacher (Kraft, Schueler and Falken, 2024). Those two findings pull in opposite directions and are the family's most useful pair, since they deflate the promise of tutoring while strengthening the case for staffing it with people who have taught.

**United States (Aurora).** The one rigorous causal test of retirees mentoring novices was run in a district in Colorado. Recently retired master educators of the district, screened for excellence and paid USD 41 an hour, mentored probationary teachers in Title I elementary schools over two years through weekly one-to-one sessions and monthly cohort meetings, with caseloads of three to seven and no evaluative role, layered on top of the district's ordinary induction (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). Seventy-seven probationary teachers in 11 schools were randomised within school. The trial was rated by the What Works Clearinghouse as meeting standards without reservations, and all three reviewed domains were characterised as indeterminate, with no statistically significant findings in reading or mathematics on student samples above 960 in each year, and none on teacher retention on samples of 74 to 77 (What Works Clearinghouse, 2019). The student side was adequately powered and the retention side was not. The result is a statement about evidential power rather than a verdict for or against retiree mentoring, and no replication has been located.

**Andalusia.** The defined-role logic has produced a standing legal figure in Andalusia (Spain). Decreto 12/2026 of 4 February 2026 regulates the status of collaborating emeritus teacher in publicly funded non-university schools and creates a network through which retired teachers, under 75 and with at least 15 years of service in the region's schools, may promote reading and libraries, give lectures and workshops, mentor new teachers, support university student interns, mediate in conflicts and advise on guidance (Junta de Andalucía, 2026; Decreto 12/2026). The status confers no right to occupy a post, no active-teacher functions and no remuneration, and offers in return insurance cover, annual certification and access to cultural institutions on the terms serving teachers enjoy; appointments are annual and renewable within a maximum of three years, applications open for one month each June, and the decree caps participation at 2 500 collaborators a year. That figure is a ceiling and not a count, since the first application window opened in June 2026 and no enrolment has been published. The case is the purest available test of whether role definition alone mobilises former teachers, and its fencing of the role away from the establishment is both what makes it politically survivable and what limits what it can deliver. The unpaid design has been contested in national coverage.

Comparable defined roles operate at national scale without isolating former teachers within them, including England's national tutoring programme, whose third-year evaluation measured 0.06 standard deviations in mathematics and 0.03 in English at the end of primary school and null effects at the end of secondary school, and the district and state tutoring corps funded from federal pandemic relief in the United States, which recruited retired teachers among several sources and reported no share for them (Kraft, Schueler and Falken, 2024).

The job is the family with the strongest theoretical claim on the evidence about why teachers leave and the weakest reporting of who fills its roles. Two lessons survive that asymmetry. Offers of partial intensity match the documented preferences of the returner segments that move, and the systems whose take-up is dominated by casual and adjunct work, in Japan and New South Wales, are the systems that offered casual and adjunct work. And the value of a defined role appears to depend on how tightly it is specified, since the scripted protocol reproduced its effect with a workforce drawn largely from retired and substitute teachers, the audited billion-dollar deployment of a similar pool did not improve learning and was faulted on targeting and fit, and the discretionary mentoring role returned an indeterminate result. None of this reaches the returner unless the returner hears of it, which is the concern of the last family.

## 3.4 Reach

Reach covers the instruments through which education authorities find former teachers and accompany them back, from letters written to names held in employer or pension records, to advisers, refresher courses and help with applications. It is the family education ministries own outright, the cheapest to launch and the only one that changes nothing about the terms of a return. That last property is what makes it interesting analytically, since a contact campaign holds permission, price and the job constant and therefore measures how much of the gap between a large reserve and a small return flow is an information problem.

Enough systems have now published conversion figures for the question to be answered provisionally. Letters convert at low single-digit rates, advisory services at rates an order of magnitude higher on looser outcome definitions, and employer-records outreach that bundles re-licensing with placement somewhere between the two. Interest itself is not the constraint. The English pilot below exceeded its registration target by a wide margin and missed its placement target by a wider one, and Dutch regional partnerships reported receiving more applications from returners and side entrants than schools could place (De Jong et al., 2022).

**England.** England has the only published end-to-end returner funnel with costs attached. The national college established a return-to-teaching pilot in 2015 for secondary shortage subjects, grant-funding partnerships led by lead schools, with in-school support delivered between January and October 2016 to teachers not then teaching in the English public sector and no payments to returners; the targets were about 3 000 registrations of interest and about 650 returners in permanent posts in target subjects by autumn 2016 (Buchanan et al., 2018). Registrations reached 5 729, 412 began training, 354 were supported, 62 were in any state-funded teaching post at the November 2016 census and 27 were in a permanent post in a target subject in a state-funded secondary school. Non-supported registrants returned at about 4%, a figure the evaluator states is not a valid estimate of additionality because supported returners were already more certain of returning. Grant payments totalled GBP 586 900, and the cost per additional returner ran from GBP 17 800 to GBP 142 500 depending on the additionality assumed, against GBP 33 300 to GBP 46 900 for training a new teacher still teaching after a year. The verdict was that the pilot was unlikely to represent good value for money, with the shortfall attributed to the timing of school recruitment, the suitability of some participants and the scarcity of part-time work (Buchanan et al., 2018). The same evaluation supplies the base rate any such scheme must beat, since the annual rate at which qualified out-of-service secondary teachers return unaided falls from 14% to 15% at ages 20 to 29 to 3% at ages 50 to 59. The successor programme, restricted to two regions and three subjects, paid schools up to GBP 5 000 and returners up to GBP 600 for a course with GBP 150 a week towards childcare, ran two intakes in 2017 and was withdrawn in March 2018 without evaluation (NCTL, 2017). What England kept is the cheapest instrument. Two advisers supported 541 eligible returners in 2015/16 and 63 of them had secured teaching posts in state-funded schools by the end of September 2016, a conversion of 11.6% against a strand target of 50, in a process evaluation whose participants praised prompt contact while struggling to obtain classroom experience from schools (Straw et al., 2017). The service later cost about GBP 340 000 a year and recorded 128 returns in its three eligible subjects across 2019/20 and 2020/21, a count the responsible minister noted excludes returns to other subjects and returns not reported back (UK Parliament, 2022a). It now runs without subject or phase restriction and is listed among the measures of the government's 2026 teacher-supply plan (Department for Education, 2026).

**Austria.** Austria mailed at scale and published what came back. From autumn 2022, under a federal recruitment strategy, the regional education directorates wrote to retired teachers, with the directorate in Vienna contacting about 300 already-retired teachers in December 2022, Salzburg writing to all retirees up to age 67 with transport tickets and higher premiums attached, and Vorarlberg sending re-entry information out with the retirement paperwork (APA/ORF, 2023). More than 3 000 letters went out nationally and about 45 teachers accepted, a conversion of roughly 1.5%; in Vienna the 300 letters produced three returns (Resch, 2025a; ORF Wien, 2023). The directorate in Vienna then changed instrument, asking principals to speak individually to staff approaching retirement about staying on, and recorded 46 deferrals in the preceding year and 20 more by late March 2023. Neither the letters nor the conversations can be credited with the audited stock of returns discussed in Section 3.2, since that series begins four years before the mailing and peaks in the years of maximum staffing emergency. What the case establishes is that mass personalised contact with a named, addressable population converts at low single digits, and that the instrument which visibly moved people in the same city and the same months was a conversation about not leaving.

**Hesse.** Hesse has contacted its retired teachers since 2020 and wrote to about 7 000 of them in 2022/23, within a national pattern in which 13 of 16 Länder approach pensioned teachers (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). The ministry's own account puts the annual yield of the exercise at up to 200 posts, a conversion of roughly 3%, and about 200 retired teachers returned to school service at the start of 2024/25 alongside service extensions and other short-term measures (fuldainfo, 2024). No cost has been published for the mailing, no Land publishes an evaluation, and the ministry attributes its short-term package as a whole rather than the letters in particular to the posts filled. The German case adds a warning about denominators as much as a conversion rate, since the country holds no register of qualified teachers outside its schools and the recruitment debate therefore defaults to the one population that can be counted.

**New South Wales.** The outreach that produced the placements described in Section 3.3 worked from the department's own staffing records rather than from advertising. More than 1 500 former teachers who had resigned within the previous five years were contacted from November 2023, more than 260 expressed interest and 145 were placed, a contact-to-placement conversion of about 10% and an interest-to-placement conversion of about 56% (NSW Government, 2024). The design feature the case supports is the bundling of accreditation reactivation, background clearance and placement into a single contact, rather than anything about the message. Nothing has been published since May 2024, so the programme's continuation can be inferred but not established, and no cost or additionality estimate exists.

**Ireland.** Ireland removed a price barrier before advertising. Retired public servants in Ireland have their pensions abated where pension and new salary together exceed pre-retirement pay, a direct bar to substitute work, and since 2021 the first 50 days worked in a calendar year have been exempted from abatement, each working day counting as one day whatever the hours, with the exemption extended by circular for 2024 and 2025 and again for 2026 and 2027 (Retired Teachers' Association of Ireland, 2024; Association of Secondary Teachers, Ireland, n.d.). A communications campaign encouraging retired teachers to return followed in early 2024 (Houses of the Oireachtas, 2024). The outcome the department attaches to this pairing is measured in days rather than heads, namely a 49% increase in teaching days worked by retired teachers between 2021/22 and 2022/23, a pre-and-post comparison on a base depressed by the pandemic which in any case measures the exemption rather than the later campaign. Departmental figures relayed by teachers' representative bodies put the number of retired teachers who had returned to work in the primary sector at 1 388 by the end of 2023, of whom 2.88% remained subject to abatement (Retired Teachers' Association of Ireland, 2024). The campaign's reach, budget and cost have not been published, and the day cap is designed so that returners supplement rather than displace the standing workforce.

**Scotland.** Scotland (United Kingdom) offers the contrast that shows reach to be a choice. Teachers who left the register or lapsed through non-payment return to it by sending scanned identity and clearance documents to the regulator under a stated subject line, supported by a booklet on returning to teaching, with no outreach campaign and no accompaniment attached (General Teaching Council for Scotland, n.d.). No take-up has been published. A system with the same measurement infrastructure as England has chosen registration mechanics over contact, which is worth recording because it establishes that the reach family is optional rather than inevitable.

Reach is the family whose yield is measured most often and found smallest, and the measurements are consistent enough across systems to be treated as a finding rather than as a set of local failures. Contact is cheap wherever employer or pension records hold names, and conversion is the binding constraint, running at roughly 1% to 3% for letters, at about 10% where re-licensing and placement are bundled into the contact, and at 11.6% for an advisory service measured against any teaching post. Ranking the four families by the scale of return their records document, the rules of permission and price come first, since only rule changes have moved more than 1% of a stated denominator; the job comes next, in the systems where retirees fill a standing supply and adjunct function; and reach comes last, which is what the classification predicts, since it alone changes nothing about what a return costs or offers. The reading is qualified throughout by the absence of any additionality estimate anywhere in the record, and by the tendency of publication to stop with the launch announcement rather than to follow the instrument.
