# PART A — TAXONOMIES OF WHY TEACHERS LEAVE

## A.0 Preliminary: there is a genealogy, and it is not an education genealogy

The single most useful orienting text for this section is Nguyen and Springer (2021), because it is the only paper that reconstructs the *descent* of teacher-attrition taxonomies rather than simply proposing another one. Their Figure 1 traces three phases:

1. **Foundation building (1958–1986), in organisational behaviour, not education.** March and Simon (1958) catalogue the determinants of participation and turnover; Porter and Steers (1973) break "job satisfaction" into five categories (job satisfaction; organisational factors; immediate work environment; job content; personal factors); Mobley et al. (1979) revise them, finding age, tenure, satisfaction and job content robust and pay, promotion, education and peer interaction less conclusive than believed; Bluedorn (1982) adds a path model in which satisfaction feeds organisational commitment, which feeds job search, which feeds exit; **Cotton and Tuttle (1986)** produce the three-way split — *personal correlates / work-related correlates / external correlates* — that every later education taxonomy inherits.
2. **Sector-specific application (2006–2008).** Guarino, Santibañez and Daley (2006) import the economic supply-and-demand frame; Borman and Dowling (2008) produce the five-category factor list (teacher characteristics; teacher qualifications; school organisational characteristics; school resources; student body characteristics).
3. **Research, practice and policy (2010– ).** Longitudinal administrative data permit four new categories — relational demography, accountability, school improvement, workforce — folded into Nguyen et al.'s nine-subcategory, three-group scheme.

**The implication for our paper is important and should be stated in the text: the dominant taxonomy of teacher attrition is a borrowed private-sector employee-turnover taxonomy with schooling variables poured into it.** Nguyen and Springer say so themselves: Borman and Dowling "did not leverage the broader employee turnover literature", and lacked "a conceptual framework explaining turnover in the broader workplace and policy contexts". That is a criticism of insufficient borrowing; one can equally read the history as showing that education never developed a taxonomy of its own.

## A.1 Push and pull factors

**What it classifies.** Reasons, sorted by their *location relative to the person's current position*: push factors are aversive properties of the job or school being left; pull factors are attractive properties of an alternative destination (another school, another occupation, non-employment, family life).

**Origin — and this is older and more specific than the education literature usually admits.**
- Ravenstein's (1885, 1889) "Laws of migration" first contrasts repellent and attractive forces.
- **Everett S. Lee (1966), "A theory of migration", *Demography* 3/1, pp. 47–57, is the canonical systematisation.** Lee's scheme has *four* elements, not two: (i) factors associated with the area of origin, (ii) factors associated with the area of destination, (iii) **intervening obstacles**, and (iv) **personal factors**. Each factor may be a plus, a minus or a zero, and — crucially for us — the *same* factor can be a plus for one person and a minus for another. **[conceptual]**
- In labour economics the same logic is the job-search / job-matching tradition: exit occurs when the utility of the best alternative, net of mobility costs, exceeds the utility of the incumbent job. Mobility costs are Lee's "intervening obstacles".

**Use in the teacher literature.** Extremely widespread, usually in the degraded two-term version. Representative uses: Viano et al. on turnaround schools (push = school-level conditions, pull = destination-school characteristics); the Ghanaian rural-to-urban transfer literature; teacher-emigration studies (e.g. Philippine DepEd teachers moving abroad). It is also the organising frame of a strand of "former teacher" interview studies, e.g. the *Teaching and Teacher Education* paper titled "Being pushed out of the career: former teachers' reasons for leaving the profession" (2021) **[secondary source — I could not retrieve the full text; confirm authorship and country before citing]**.

**Strengths.** (i) Cheap and intuitive; readable by ministers. (ii) It is the only common frame that treats the *destination* as part of the explanation, which matters enormously for teacher labour markets in tight economies. (iii) It maps directly onto policy instruments: reduce pushes (working conditions) versus block pulls (bonded service, salary competitiveness). (iv) Lee's full version already contains the two things the simplified version loses — obstacles and heterogeneity.

**Criticisms.**
- *Oversimplification / tautology.* Anything that correlates with leaving can be relabelled a push, anything that correlates with the destination a pull; the scheme has no independent content and generates no prohibitions.
- *Neglect of context and structure.* The migration critique — that push–pull is ahistorical and ignores political economy — transfers wholly.
- *Neglect of agency and of the decision process.* It is a static ledger of forces, not a model of how a person decides over time.
- *The push/pull boundary is not stable.* "Low pay" is a push in a school with poor conditions and a pull when a private-sector offer arrives; the same fact is coded differently depending on the respondent's narrative.
- *Mason and Matas (2015) name it explicitly* as one of the frames that treats attrition factors as isolated variables and cannot capture interaction.
- *The Scottish evidence undercuts its behavioural assumption.* Hulme et al. (2026) find most exits accumulate over months to years without a discrete push event.

**Who uses it.** Practitioner and union literature almost universally; a large minority of peer-reviewed qualitative studies; comparatively rare in the economics of education, which prefers the utility formulation.

## A.2 Personal / school / external correlates — the Nguyen–Pham–Crouch–Springer scheme

**Full framework (verified from the working-paper text and Figure 1).** Three groups, nine subcategories:

| Group | Subcategories | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal correlates** | Teacher characteristics; Teacher qualifications | Age, gender, race/ethnicity, full/part-time status, career satisfaction; academic ability, degree level, certification route, subject specialism, experience |
| **School correlates** | School organisational characteristics; School resources; Student body characteristics; Relational demography | Size, urbanicity, school level, sector, discipline problems, working conditions, administrative support, induction/mentoring, PD, leadership, collaboration; class size, aides, materials; achievement, %minority, %FRPL, %IEP; teacher–principal and teacher–student race/gender congruence |
| **External correlates** | Accountability; School improvement; Workforce | Teacher evaluation, merit pay, teacher effectiveness, principal effectiveness; turnaround/reform policy; unemployment rate, late hiring, teacher salary, retention bonuses, non-teacher salary, unions, tenure, pensions |

**Evidence base.** 120 studies, 1980–2018, systematic search, odds-ratio meta-analysis with hazard-ratio robustness checks and robust variance estimation. Published as Nguyen et al. (2020) in *Educational Research Review* 31, 100355. **[associational — the authors are explicit that the pooled estimates are correlational; they welcome the growth of difference-in-differences designs but do not claim causal identification]**

**Magnitudes worth quoting in our paper (odds ratios; OR<1 = less likely to leave):**
- Teachers older than 28: OR ≈ 0.70 (30% lower odds of leaving) than those 28 or younger.
- Gender: **null** across 37 studies — a direct reversal of Borman and Dowling (2008).
- Hispanic vs white teachers: odds of leaving reduced by 53% (11 studies).
- Standard certification: OR ≈ 0.53 (47% lower odds), 16 studies.
- New vs veteran teachers: +54% odds of attrition.
- Better working conditions (facilities, fewer discipline/violence problems): odds reduced by "nearly a factor of two", 5 studies.
- Stronger administrative support: OR ≈ 0.80, 13 studies.
- Salary: statistically significant but point estimate close to 1 — an extra US$1 000 barely moves attrition; 18 studies.
- Merit pay: −1.6 percentage points on average (linear probability), rising to ≈ −15 pp in hard-to-staff schools in one subgroup analysis (Hough, 2012).
- Union membership: OR ≈ 0.75, marginally significant, 3 studies.
- Class size, teacher aides, %FRPL, %Black, %Hispanic, %IEP: **null**.
- Charter vs traditional public: higher odds of leaving.
- Teacher evaluation and higher measured effectiveness: associated with *lower*, not higher, attrition.

**Strengths.** Empirically the best-grounded taxonomy available; comprehensive; explicitly policy-legible (the "external" box is where ministries act); transparently derived and updateable.

**Criticisms.**
- *It is a taxonomy of variables, not of reasons.* It classifies what researchers measured, which is what administrative data records. Nothing in the scheme can hold "I no longer recognise the job I trained for."
- *Overwhelmingly US.* Despite the "international literature" in the 2021 title, the evidence base and the policy categories (NCLB, FRPL, IEP, charter schools, district-set salaries) are American.
- *The categories are not mutually exclusive.* Salary was moved from "school resources" to "workforce" purely because US salary is district-set — an admission that the boundary is institutional, not conceptual, and will not travel to systems with national pay scales.
- *Mechanism-blind.* Nguyen and Springer concede that "the precise mechanism through which the policies affect turnover is largely unexplored", and that they know of no rigorous mediation analysis of merit pay or evaluation.
- *It cannot separate voluntary from compelled exit*, nor movers from leavers, at the level of the reason.

**Who uses it.** It has become the default organising scheme for reviews, ministry evidence summaries and doctoral literature chapters since 2020. Gundlach, Slemp and Hattie (2024) extend the same logic to ~70 antecedents and 3 430 correlations, separating attrition / migration / retention and intention / behaviour — reporting for example affective commitment–turnover intention at r ≈ −0.67, and education level at 0.11 for staying in a school but 0.03 for staying in the profession **[associational; figures via secondary sources — verify against the article]**.

## A.3 Individual / organisational / systemic levels (the multi-level or ecological frame)

**What it classifies.** The same reasons, but by the *level of the system at which the cause sits and at which a remedy would have to be applied* — micro (the person), meso (the school as organisation), macro (policy, labour market, society).

**Origins and principal instances.**
- **Billingsley (1993, 2004)** is the earliest fully worked education version. Her model of teachers' career decisions has three major factor blocks: **external** (societal, economic, institutional), **employment** (professional qualifications, work conditions and rewards, commitment, employability) and **personal** (demographics, family, cognitive/affective). Developed for special-education attrition; widely reused as a general framework. Billingsley and Bettini (2019) update it in *Review of Educational Research*.
- **Tiplic, Brandmo and Elstad (2015)**, *Cambridge Journal of Education* 45/4, pp. 451–474, explicitly frame beginning-teacher turnover intention as individual × organisational × contextual. Norway; 227 beginning teachers in 133 schools; survey, SEM. Four significant antecedents: collective teacher efficacy, teacher–principal trust, role conflict, affective commitment. Headline conclusion: **organisational and contextual factors, not individual competence perceptions, drive early turnover intention.** **[associational]**
- **Bronfenbrenner-derived ecological versions.** A 2026 *Teachers and Teaching* systematic review of 29 Australian studies (2014–2024, PRISMA) proposes "an adjusted ecological systems framework" with the teacher and their history at the centre and school leadership as a moderator of the surrounding layers. **[secondary source — the search result attributed authorship in a way I could not verify; confirm authors before citing]**
- Räsänen et al.'s (2020) Finnish categories are also read by their authors as "system-wide, school- and personal-level" (see B.3).

**Strengths.** (i) It is the taxonomy that maps most cleanly onto governance: who owns the lever. (ii) It survives translation across systems better than the US-specific variable lists. (iii) It naturally accommodates cross-level interaction (a systemic reform experienced through a school's leadership).

**Criticisms.** (i) The three levels are a container, not a theory — nothing in them says which level dominates or how levels interact. (ii) Attribution is contested: is "workload" personal (poor boundary-setting), organisational (timetabling) or systemic (curriculum reform and accountability)? Different literatures place it differently, and the placement is itself a political act. (iii) Ecological versions proliferate layers without adding predictive content.

## A.4 Job Demands–Resources theory applied to teaching

**What it classifies.** Not reasons for leaving as such, but the *working conditions* that produce the psychological states that precede leaving — split into **demands** (effort-requiring, deplete) and **resources** (goal-facilitating, motivate), running through two parallel processes: a **health-impairment path** (demands → exhaustion/burnout → exit) and a **motivational path** (resources → engagement/commitment → stay).

**Origin.** Demerouti, Bakker, Nachreiner and Schaufeli (2001), "The job demands–resources model of burnout", *Journal of Applied Psychology* 86/3, pp. 499–512; extended in Bakker and Demerouti (2007, 2017). Not an education theory; imported.

**Key applications in teaching.**
- **Madigan and Kim (2021)**, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 105, 103425. Random-effects meta-analysis; 24 studies, 38 effect sizes (10 on burnout–intention, 14 on satisfaction–intention), multi-country, all **cross-sectional**. Exhaustion–intention to quit **r+ = .41**; depersonalisation **r+ = .32**; reduced accomplishment **r+ = .21**; job satisfaction–intention **r+ = −.40**; exhaustion–satisfaction **r+ = −.42**. Together burnout and satisfaction explain **27% of variance** in intention to quit, most of it from burnout. Publication year was the **only** significant moderator: the burnout–intention link has strengthened over time. Age, gender, education stage and country did **not** moderate. **[associational; authors flag cross-sectional design as the principal limitation]**
- **Collie (2023)**, *British Journal of Educational Psychology*, doi 10.1111/bjep.12587. Australia; 426 teachers; SEM with personality controls. Three resources (autonomy-supportive leadership, relatedness with colleagues, relatedness with students) and three demands (autonomy-thwarting leadership, time pressure, disruptive student behaviour). On turnover intentions: **only relatedness with colleagues** among the resources was directly protective; **autonomy-thwarting leadership** and **time pressure** raised intentions; subjective vitality lowered them; **disruptive student behaviour was not associated with turnover intention** once the other factors and personality were controlled. **[associational; cross-sectional]**
- **Skaalvik and Skaalvik (2011)**, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 27/6, pp. 1029–1038. Norway; 2 569 elementary and middle-school teachers. Six school-context variables (value consonance, supervisory support, relations with colleagues, relations with parents, time pressure, discipline problems) all related to satisfaction and to motivation to leave, but **the relations were mainly indirect**, mediated by belonging and emotional exhaustion. **[associational]**
- **Li and Yao (2022)**, *Educational Research Review* 100477. Meta-analysis: 94 studies, 99 independent samples, 269 effect sizes, n = 39 508. Burnout is the single strongest predictor of turnover intention; workload and stress positively related; trust, professional identification and organisational commitment the three strongest protectors. Demographic effect sizes small; relations largely stable across age, gender, cultural context and grade level, except that burnout and intrinsic motivation bind more tightly to intention among younger teachers. **[associational]**

**Strengths.** (i) It supplies a *mechanism*, which the correlate taxonomies lack. (ii) It is measurable, with validated instruments, and therefore comparable across countries. (iii) The demand/resource distinction is genuinely generative: it predicts buffering interactions, not just main effects. (iv) It handles the "same factor, different valence" problem better than push/pull, because a demand can be either hindering or challenging.

**Criticisms.**
- *Nearly all the evidence is cross-sectional and on intention, not behaviour.* Madigan and Kim's 27% of variance is 27% of variance in a self-reported intention.
- *Classification of a given condition as demand or resource is often post hoc.* Collie's finding that time pressure raised *behavioural engagement* while lowering vitality is a live example.
- *It psychologises structural problems.* Everything arrives at the teacher as an individual-level appraisal, which makes it easy for policy to respond with resilience training rather than staffing. The German NRW study (B.1) is an explicit rebuttal from within this tradition.
- *It cannot represent moral or vocational reasons for leaving* — a teacher who leaves because the job no longer permits what they think teaching is for has neither a demand nor a resource problem in JD-R terms.

## A.5 The teacher-labour-market frame: supply, demand and compensating differentials

**What it classifies.** Exit as a market outcome. Attrition is *supply* withdrawal, driven by the relative utility of teaching versus the outside option, where utility includes both the wage and the non-pecuniary characteristics of the job. Reasons are therefore sorted into: relative wage; alternative-occupation opportunity wage; non-pecuniary job amenities and disamenities; and mobility/entry costs.

**Origin.** Rosen's (1974, 1986) hedonic wage theory and the theory of equalising differences, itself traceable to Adam Smith. Applied to teaching by **Murnane and Olsen (1989, 1990)** — duration analyses on North Carolina and Michigan data showing salaries and opportunity costs affect length of stay — and by **Hanushek, Kain and Rivkin (2004)**, "Why public schools lose teachers", *Journal of Human Resources* 39/2, showing that teachers' school-to-school moves respond strongly to student characteristics and weakly to salary, implying large implicit compensating differentials for teaching disadvantaged students. **Guarino, Santibañez and Daley (2006)**, *Review of Educational Research* 76/2, pp. 173–208, formalise it as the organising frame of a review of 46 US studies; their best-known descriptive result is the **U-shaped attrition–experience curve** (high in the first five years, low mid-career, rising again near retirement). Loeb and Myung's "Economic approaches to teacher recruitment and retention" is the standard textbook statement.

**Strengths.** (i) It is the only frame that prices non-pecuniary conditions, and therefore the only one that can say how much a working-condition improvement is worth in salary terms. (ii) It naturally integrates entry, exit and re-entry into one model. (iii) It explains cross-sectional patterns other frames treat as anomalies (why STEM and special-education teachers leave more; why attrition is counter-cyclical). (iv) Best-identified evidence base — administrative panels, policy discontinuities, bonus experiments.

**Criticisms.**
- *Its central prediction under-performs.* Nguyen et al.'s pooled salary effect is barely distinguishable from one; and the Danish register evidence (B.3) shows leavers taking a **pay cut**, which a pure wage model cannot generate without invoking unobserved amenities — at which point the theory is unfalsifiable.
- *"Compensating differential" describes an equilibrium, not a decision.* It tells us nothing about how a particular teacher came to leave in April rather than the previous April.
- *Preferences are treated as fixed.* The vocational-erosion story — that the job changed and with it what the teacher wanted — is outside the model.
- *Data-driven category boundaries.* Nguyen and Springer note that data limitations, especially reliance on administrative records, have constrained the very conceptualisation of the variables.

**Who uses it.** Economics of education; finance ministries; OECD's own *Effective Teacher Policies* line of work; teacher-pay reform advocacy on both sides.

## A.6 Ingersoll's organisational-conditions frame

**What it classifies.** Not primarily reasons but the *level of explanation*: Ingersoll (2001), "Teacher turnover and teacher shortages: an organizational analysis", *American Educational Research Journal* 38/3, pp. 499–534, reframes an apparent *supply* problem (not enough teachers being trained) as a *demand-side, organisational* problem (schools losing the teachers they have). Data: US Schools and Staffing Survey and its Teacher Followup Survey (NCES).

**Core claims.** (i) School staffing problems are driven by a **"revolving door"** — large numbers of qualified teachers leaving for reasons other than retirement; retirement is a minor share of total turnover. (ii) Turnover is concentrated in urban, high-poverty schools. (iii) The dissatisfaction that drives exit is organisationally specific: low salaries, lack of administrative support, student discipline problems, lack of student motivation, and **lack of teacher influence over decision-making**. Administrator leadership and support is among the most reliable predictors. **[associational, on nationally representative survey data]**

**Strengths.** (i) It moved the policy question from recruitment to retention, and from the teacher to the school — arguably the single most consequential reframing in the field. (ii) It is the source of the now-standard emphasis on *teacher voice/influence* as a distinct condition, separable from workload and pay. (iii) It gave the field its empirical anchor rate (~15% annual turnover in US public schools) and the disaggregation of that rate by school poverty.

**Criticisms.** (i) It is an *analysis*, not a taxonomy: its categories are the SASS questionnaire's reason codes, which are fixed, non-exhaustive and self-reported at a single moment. (ii) US institutional specificity — the school-as-organisation is a far weaker unit in centralised systems (France, Japan, Italy) where staffing, pay and posting are national. (iii) It underweights the macro: accountability regimes, national reform cycles and labour-market conditions sit outside the frame. (iv) By locating the problem in "the organization of schools", it can license blaming principals for conditions set by ministries.

**Who uses it.** Nearly universal as the framing citation in US retention research; the CPRE / Learning Policy Institute line of work; and it is the frame most often imported wholesale into non-US contexts where it fits worst.

## A.7 Kelchtermans's career-story / narrative-biographical frame

**What it classifies.** Not factors but *meanings*. The unit is the teacher's career story, and the analytic object is the **personal interpretative framework** — "a set of cognitions, of mental representations that operates as a lens through which teachers look at their job, give meaning to it and act in it" (Kelchtermans, 2009, p. 260). It has two domains:

1. **Professional self-understanding**, with five components: **self-image** (descriptive), **self-esteem** (evaluative), **task perception** (normative — what one believes one *must* do to be a proper teacher), **job motivation**, and **future perspective**.
2. **Subjective educational theory** — the personal system of knowledge and beliefs about how to act.

Three further concepts do the explanatory work for exit:
- **Vulnerability** as a *structural* characteristic of the job, not a personal weakness: teachers do not control essential working conditions, cannot prove their effectiveness yet are judged on proxies for it, and lack an unquestionable basis for professional judgement. Kelchtermans argues this structural vulnerability is "key to understanding a number of complex issues, such as teacher attrition, resistance to change, teacher burnout, and intensification".
- **Micropolitics and professional interests**: beginning teachers hold views of the conditions necessary to do a proper job; when these are threatened they act strategically. **Micropolitical literacy** — reading situations in terms of professional interests and having the tactical and emotional repertoire to defend them — is what distinguishes those who survive induction.
- **Task-perception conflict**: exit as the resolution of an intolerable gap between what one believes teaching requires and what the job permits.

**Origin and use.** Kelchtermans (1993) in *Teaching and Teacher Education*; Kelchtermans and Ballet (2002) on micropolitics; Kelchtermans (2009) in *Teachers and Teaching* 15/2, pp. 257–272; the retrospective statement is Kelchtermans (2017), *Teacher Education Quarterly* 44/4. The frame dominates European qualitative teacher-career research (Leuven; the Nordic and Dutch traditions) and — importantly for Part B — supplies the analytic apparatus of the leading Latin American review (González-Escobar et al., 2020, on which Kelchtermans is a co-author).

**Strengths.** (i) It is the only framework here that can represent *moral* reasons for leaving. (ii) It explains why identical conditions produce exit in one teacher and not another without recourse to "resilience". (iii) It is longitudinal by construction: the story has a before and an after. (iv) It generates the concept of the *compelled* exit of a still-committed teacher, which the Scottish and Danish quantitative evidence has now independently confirmed.

**Criticisms.** (i) Small, self-selected samples; no magnitudes; no capacity to rank causes. (ii) Not commensurable with the variable-based literature — its categories cannot be counted. (iii) Retrospective narrative is reconstructive: leavers build coherent accounts after the fact. (iv) Almost impossible to convert into a policy lever without translation into organisational terms, at which point it collapses back into the conditions taxonomies.

## A.8 Self-determination theory applied to teacher motivation

**What it classifies.** Reasons by which of the three **basic psychological needs** they satisfy or frustrate: **autonomy** (behaviour is self-endorsed rather than compelled), **competence** (effectance, mastery), **relatedness** (mutual care, belonging). Exit follows sustained need frustration, which converts autonomous motivation into controlled motivation or amotivation.

**Origin.** Deci and Ryan (1985, 2000); Ryan and Deci (2017, 2020). Explicitly a theory of *need frustration* as well as satisfaction (Chen et al., 2015; Bartholomew et al.) — the asymmetry matters: frustration is not merely low satisfaction, it actively produces alienation and disengagement.

**Application to teaching.**
- Collie (2023) (see A.4) is the cleanest teacher-attrition application: the resources and demands are chosen precisely because they "tap into the basic psychological needs", and autonomy-thwarting leadership emerges as a direct predictor of turnover intention while autonomy-*supportive* leadership works indirectly through well-being.
- Nie et al. (2015) on autonomy support and teacher job satisfaction; Fernet et al. on teacher motivation and burnout.
- The teacher-motivation meta-analysis of autonomous versus controlled motivation (Slemp et al., 2020, *Journal of Vocational Behavior*) supplies the general effect structure. **[associational]**
- Li and Yao's (2022) finding that intrinsic motivation binds more tightly to turnover intention among younger teachers is consistent with an SDT reading.

**Strengths.** (i) A parsimonious three-category scheme with a very large validated instrument base and demonstrated cross-cultural applicability. (ii) It gives *autonomy* the theoretical status that Ingersoll gave it empirically, and connects it to accountability and curriculum-prescription policy. (iii) It explains why pay rises do not retain: money is a controlled-motivation lever operating on a need it does not touch. (iv) Relatedness gives a principled account of the collegial findings in Germany, Denmark and Australia.

**Criticisms.** (i) Three needs are too few to organise a policy paper: workload, pay, discipline, bureaucracy and status all have to be re-described as need frustrations, losing policy specificity. (ii) Universality of the three needs is contested cross-culturally, though empirical support is broad. (iii) It is a theory of motivation, not of decision: need frustration explains wanting to leave, not leaving. (iv) Overlaps heavily with JD-R in practice, and the two are usually run together, making their distinct contributions hard to identify.

## A.9 FIT-Choice: entry motives, and what they imply for exit

**What it classifies.** Motivations for *entering* teaching. Watt and Richardson's Factors Influencing Teaching Choice scale (2007), *Journal of Experimental Education* 75/3, pp. 167–202: 58 items, 18 factors, grounded in expectancy–value theory. Motivation factors include perceived teaching **ability**; **intrinsic career value**; **fallback career**; **job security**; **time for family**; **job transferability**; **shape the future of children/adolescents**; **enhance social equity**; **make a social contribution**; **work with children/adolescents**; plus perceptions of the profession (social status, expertise, difficulty, salary, social dissuasion) and career-choice satisfaction. Validated internationally (Australia, USA, Germany, Norway; Watt et al., 2012, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 28/6) with strong factorial invariance.

**What it implies for exit — and this is the part our paper should press.**
- The dominant entry motives are *altruistic-social* and *intrinsic*: shaping the future, social contribution, working with young people. Exit is therefore best predicted not by the absence of extrinsic rewards but by the **thwarting of the motive that brought the person in** — the inability to make the difference they entered to make.
- **Fallback career** is the diagnostic exception: those who entered teaching as a fallback show weaker persistence and lower professional engagement, so entry motive is itself a retention-relevant covariate.
- Longitudinal FIT-Choice work follows entrants to "exit levels" of engagement and planned persistence at the end of teacher education, providing the bridge from entry motive to early attrition.
- The Danish (leavers accept lower pay) and French (vocation intact, conditions intolerable) findings are exactly what a FIT-Choice reading predicts.

**Strengths.** (i) It is the only well-validated instrument on the *entry* side, and therefore the only way to test the "unmet expectations" account rigorously. (ii) Internationally invariant, which is rare here. (iii) It offers a selection-versus-treatment decomposition: some attrition is mis-selection at entry, some is degradation of the job.

**Criticisms.** (i) It measures motives, not outcomes; the exit link is inferential and mostly untested against actual behaviour. (ii) Socially desirable responding inflates altruistic motives. (iii) Its categories are entry categories and do not map onto the reasons leavers actually give. (iv) It risks the policy conclusion "select better candidates", which shifts responsibility away from working conditions.

## A.10 Typologies of *leavers*, as opposed to typologies of *reasons*

This is a distinct and under-exploited family: schemes that classify the exit event or the exiting person, not the cause.

### (a) Stayers / movers / leavers — the NCES scheme
From the US Schools and Staffing Survey and its **Teacher Follow-up Survey** (first published as Bobbitt, Faupel and Burns, 1991; the canonical statement is *Characteristics of Stayers, Movers, and Leavers*, NCES). Definitions: **stayers** teach in the same school in consecutive years; **movers** teach in a different school; **leavers** are no longer teaching K–12. Ingersoll's work rests on it; Nguyen et al. use it to separate "leaving the school" from "leaving the profession"; Gundlach et al. (2024) separate attrition, migration and retention as distinct outcomes and find the *same* antecedent has different effect sizes for each.

*Strength:* it forces the analytically vital distinction between school turnover (a distributional problem) and occupational attrition (a supply problem). *Weakness:* it is a one-year snapshot with no exit-motive content; it treats a move to a better school and a move away from a failing one identically; and it cannot see re-entry.

### (b) Voluntary / involuntary
Voluntary = employee-initiated; involuntary = employer- or circumstance-initiated (dismissal, non-renewal of fixed-term contracts, redundancy, ill-health retirement, visa or family relocation). Standard in the general turnover literature (Cotton and Tuttle, 1986; Griffeth, Hom and Gaertner, 2000) and formally used in teacher research (e.g. Adams, 1996, Cox regression on *voluntary* teacher turnover). Gundlach et al. (2024) attribute part of the divergence between antecedents of intention and antecedents of behaviour to involuntary turnover contaminating the behavioural measures.

*Criticism, and it is now empirically grounded:* **Hulme et al. (2026)**, "Teacher turnover in Scotland: reluctant leavers, downscaling, and the limits of voluntary exit", *Oxford Review of Education*, qualitative, 63 teachers in Scotland, semi-structured interviews analysed with Lee and Mitchell's unfolding model and proximal-withdrawal-states theory. Findings: most departures unfolded through **accumulated dissatisfaction over months to years** rather than discrete triggering events; **over two-thirds of those who exited did so despite sustained vocational commitment**, because conditions had become untenable; and a further **27 teachers had "downscaled"** — cut hours or given up promoted posts — a category invisible in both the voluntary/involuntary and the stayer/mover/leaver schemes. The authors conclude that many departures categorised as voluntary "are better understood as compelled exits". **[associational/interpretive; single-country]**

### (c) Retirement / pre-retirement exit
Ingersoll's foundational move was to show that **retirement is a minor share** of US turnover. But the retirement/pre-retirement split behaves very differently across systems, and this is a key Part B result:
- **US (TFS, 2011–13):** ~18% of turnover retirement; ~30% voluntary pre-retirement leaving; ~37% voluntary moving; ~67% of all turnover voluntary and pre-retirement. **[descriptive; secondary source — verify against the NCES first-look tables]**
- **Germany (FiBS/Dohmen, 2025):** the ratio of age-related to other departures has moved from about 1:1.1–1.3 to **1:2.6**, projected to reach 1:3; early departures now ≈ **5.4% of the teaching workforce annually**, ranging from 3.0% (Hamburg, Hesse) to 9.4% (Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, 2023/24).
- **Japan (MEXT, 2022 School Teachers Survey):** retirement is **still the majority** of public-elementary exits (8 006 of 15 030 leavers, ~53%) — but falling fast (12 005 in FY2012), while career change rose from 1 318 to 2 098.
So "retirement is a small part of the problem" is an *Anglophone-specific* empirical claim, not a general one, and our paper must not repeat it as a universal.

### (d) Returners / re-entrants
Rarely theorised. Chile: Ortiz-Bugueño et al. study "deserción y (re)ingreso" 2021–22 as a single system. Sweden: SCB reports **about half** of teacher-trained leavers would consider returning (one framing gives six in ten). Netherlands: ResearchNed finds **80% of leavers say they could have been retained** and 60% still view their teaching career positively. This "reversibility" dimension is arguably the most policy-relevant and the least classified.

### (e) Proximal withdrawal states — a genuine two-dimensional typology
**Hom, Mitchell, Lee and Griffeth (2012)**, *Psychological Bulletin* 138/5, cross *desire to leave* with *perceived control over leaving*, yielding four types: **enthusiastic stayers**, **reluctant stayers** (want to leave, cannot), **enthusiastic leavers** (want to leave, can), **reluctant leavers** (want to stay, must go). Empirically, reluctant stayers resemble enthusiastic leavers on commitment, satisfaction and embeddedness; reluctant leavers resemble enthusiastic stayers. Satisfaction and embeddedness predict intention and search far better in the "enthusiastic" cells. **[associational]** This is the frame Hulme et al. import into teaching, and it is the best available answer to the voluntary/involuntary problem.

### (f) The unfolding model — a typology of *paths*, not of causes
**Lee and Mitchell (1994)**; extended by Lee, Mitchell, Wise and Fireman (1996) in *AMJ*. Four decision paths distinguished by whether a **shock** occurs, whether a pre-existing **script** is invoked, whether an **image violation** occurs, and whether job search precedes the decision. Path 1: shock triggers a pre-existing script (leave on having a child). Path 2: shock produces image violation, exit without alternative. Path 3: shock produces image violation plus search and comparison. Path 4: no shock — gradual accumulated dissatisfaction, with or without search. Hulme et al.'s finding that most Scottish teacher exits are Path 4 is precisely the finding that push/pull cannot express.

*Strength of the whole family:* these typologies classify *the exit*, so they can be applied to administrative data and to interviews alike, and they discipline the reason-taxonomies by telling us which reasons belong to which kind of exit. *Weakness:* they say nothing about content — one still needs a reason taxonomy to fill them.

## A.11 The four-capital model — an explicit critique and an explicit alternative

**Mason, S. and C. P. Matas (2015)**, "Teacher attrition and retention research in Australia: towards a new theoretical framework", *Australian Journal of Teacher Education* 40/11. Thematic content analysis of **20 Australian studies** across 17 journals.

**Their critique of the field** (useful to quote directly in our paper):
- The Australian field is "still in its infancy", dominated by small-scale qualitative exploratory studies.
- **Almost no theory is used.** Only three theoretical frames appeared across 20 studies (Situated Theory, Social Critical Theory, Quality of Worklife), and in at least one case the theory was named in the abstract without definition or any demonstrated role in design or analysis.
- There is a "lack of consistency amongst studies" and no shared model, so "current studies remain isolated and disconnected from a broader understanding of the complexities of teacher attrition".
- Factor lists and push/pull treat determinants as isolated variables and cannot represent interaction.

**Their proposal.** Thirteen empirical themes are mapped onto four established capitals:
- **Human capital** — the teacher's own skills and knowledge; quality of pre-service preparation is the key lever.
- **Social capital** — relationships, networks, collegiality, mentoring; five of the thirteen themes.
- **Structural capital** — "everything left at the office when the employees go home": infrastructure, systems, workload allocation, compensation and promotion structures, curriculum frameworks.
- **Positive psychological capital (PsyCap)** — hope, efficacy, resilience, optimism.

**Strengths.** (i) It is a *theory-anchored* taxonomy: each category comes with an existing literature and measurement tradition. (ii) It is explicitly interactional — attrition is "a product of the interaction of elements" across the four capitals. (iii) The human/social/structural split cuts differently from personal/school/external: pre-service preparation and collegiality end up in different boxes from "school characteristics", which is analytically clarifying. (iv) Its "capital" language is legible to economists and to human-resource policy.

**Criticisms.** (i) It rests on 20 Australian studies of variable quality. (ii) Capital metaphors are contested (the human-capital critique the authors themselves acknowledge), and PsyCap in particular risks re-individualising structural problems — the very failing they diagnose. (iii) The boundary between human and psychological capital is soft. (iv) It has not been operationalised in a large-scale empirical test, so it remains a proposal rather than an established framework.

## A.12 Explicit critiques of the taxonomies, gathered

For the paper it is worth having these in one place.

1. **The taxonomies are inherited, not designed for education.** (Nguyen and Springer, 2021: the genealogy runs March and Simon → Porter and Steers → Mobley → Bluedorn → Cotton and Tuttle → Guarino et al. → Borman and Dowling.)
2. **They classify measured variables, not stated reasons.** Administrative data determine the categories: "data limitations, such as relying exclusively on administrative data, have constrained the conceptualization and therefore the analysis of variables influencing teacher labor market decisions."
3. **They are mechanism-blind.** Nguyen and Springer: "little is known about the how"; no rigorous mediation tests of merit pay or evaluation exist.
4. **They are static where the phenomenon is processual.** Hulme et al. (2026): exits accumulate over months to years; Räsänen et al. (2020): 62% of Finnish teachers with turnover intentions still had them five years later, but the *reasons* they gave had changed significantly over that period (κ = 0.24, indicating non-persistence of reasons alongside persistence of intention).
5. **They cannot separate wanting to leave from being able to leave.** Hom et al. (2012); Hulme et al. (2026) on "reluctant leavers" and "compelled exits".
6. **They atheorise and disconnect.** Mason and Matas (2015).
7. **They are Anglophone-parametrised.** The categories carry US and UK institutional structure (charter schools, FRPL, district salary-setting, school-level hiring) that does not exist in most OECD systems; and the empirical anchors ("retirement is a minor share of turnover") are country-specific claims presented as general ones.
8. **They psychologise the structural.** The German NRW study (B.1) is an explicit corrective: team structures and organisational conditions, not individual resilience.
9. **They ignore partial exit.** Downscaling — cutting hours, refusing or relinquishing promotion, disengaging while remaining — is invisible in every scheme except Hulme et al.'s and Schaarschmidt's AVEM pattern S ("protective posture"/*Schonhaltung*).
10. **They ignore reversibility.** Returners, re-entrants and conditional leavers are almost never in the taxonomy, yet 80% of Dutch leavers say they were retainable and around half of Swedish leavers would consider returning.

**Proposals for better taxonomies already on the table:** Mason and Matas's four capitals (A.11); Nguyen and Springer's nine-category integration with the employee-turnover literature (A.2); the Bronfenbrenner-adjusted ecological framework in the 2026 Australian review (A.3); González-Escobar et al.'s (2020) micropolitical-literacy reading for Latin America (B.6); and Hulme et al.'s (2026) importation of proximal withdrawal states into education (A.10e).

# PART B — EVIDENCE OUTSIDE THE ANGLOSPHERE

## B.0 The cross-national baseline: TALIS 2024

**OECD (2025), *Results from TALIS 2024*, ch. "Sustaining the teaching profession", OECD Publishing, Paris.**

- **17% of teachers who are not about to retire intend to leave the profession within five years**, on OECD average.
- **Country range: below 6% in Korea, Portugal and Viet Nam; above 30% in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania.**
- **Teachers under 30: 20% on OECD average**, up 3 percentage points since 2018; ranging from **3% in Viet Nam to 53% in Latvia**; approximately half of young teachers in Latvia, Lithuania and Estonia. US 29%; Alberta (Canada) 21%.
- The share of teachers aged 30–49 intending to leave *fell* by 5 pp or more since 2018 in Bulgaria, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Sweden and the UAE — attrition intention is not moving in one direction everywhere.
- Correlates: **satisfied teachers are about five times less likely to intend to leave** than dissatisfied colleagues; nearly nine in ten report job satisfaction overall; **52% cite administrative/paperwork as a source of stress**, **45% discipline**, **18% verbal abuse from students (47% in Brazil)**; **~40% satisfied with salary**, under 20% in Iceland, Malta, Portugal and Serbia; **14% on fixed-term contracts of a year or less**; 10% say teaching affects their mental health "a lot". Schools reporting instruction hindered by a lack of qualified teachers rose from **26% (2018) to 47% (2024)**.
- **[associational and self-reported; the OECD itself warns that TALIS "relies solely on self-reports, which reflect perceptions but may be shaped by social and cultural context", so cross-country comparisons should be interpreted with caution]**

*Note for our paper.* An Italian secondary summary reports a **European figure of 23.5% intending to leave within five years, rising to 42.7% among the over-50s**. **[secondary source — the age gradient is worth confirming directly in the TALIS tables, because it reverses the Anglophone "young leavers" emphasis.]**

**Cross-national meta-analytic baseline.** Li and Yao (2022) — 94 studies, 39 508 teachers — report that the relations between most antecedents and turnover intention are **stable regardless of cultural context**. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that the Anglophone *structure* of explanation travels, even where the *levels* do not.

## B.1 Germany

**Scale and composition of exit.**
- **FiBS / Dohmen (2025)**, analysis of official statistics on departures from school service across all 16 *Länder*, 2018/19–2022/23. The ratio of age-related departures to all other departures moved from roughly **1:1.1–1.3 to 1:2.6**, projected to reach 1:3. Early departures ≈ **5.4% of the teaching workforce per year**, from **3.0%** (Hamburg, Hesse) to **9.4%** (Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, 2023/24). Reasons attributed: workload well beyond a nominal 40-hour week, rising psychosocial and behavioural difficulty among students, growing heterogeneity, declining attractiveness. **[descriptive, administrative data; the attribution of reasons is the author's interpretation, not measured]**
- Projections of a shortfall exceeding **100 000 teachers by 2035** circulate widely. **[secondary source]**

**Why they leave — the current German study.**
- **University of Cologne (Zentrum für LehrerInnenbildung) and Katholische Hochschule NRW, presented at didacta 2026.** Survey of **1 178 teachers in North Rhine-Westphalia**, including both serving teachers and those who had already left the school service. Headline: **team structures and organisational framework conditions matter more than individual resilience.** Serving teachers scored *team resilience* **4.06** versus **3.45** for leavers, and *organisational resilience* **3.58** versus **3.02**. The largest single frustration factor identified is *Einzelkämpfertum* — the solo-operator culture of German schools. Approximately one in ten German teachers leaves for another occupation; ~8 000 teaching posts were unfilled in NRW at the time of the study. **[associational, cross-sectional, comparing current and former teachers — a design with obvious recall and selection issues; verify the published version before citing the resilience scores]**

**The German psychological tradition.**
- **Schaarschmidt's Potsdamer Lehrerstudie** (2004/2005), AVEM instrument (*Arbeitsbezogenes Verhaltens- und Erlebensmuster*), now over **7 600 educators**. Four work-related behaviour and experience patterns: **G** (healthy — strong but not excessive engagement, resilience, satisfaction); **S** (*Schonung*, protective posture — reduced engagement with preserved resilience and relative satisfaction); **Risk pattern A** (excessive effort, low resilience, low satisfaction); **Risk pattern B** (burnout proximity, resignation, chronic exhaustion). Teachers show a markedly worse distribution than comparison occupations. **[associational, cross-sectional]** *This matters for our taxonomy work: pattern S is internal withdrawal — the German analogue of Scottish "downscaling", and evidence that partial exit is a recognised category in at least one national tradition.*
- Qualitative German work on exit is emerging: Bauer, Fuhrmann, Herzmann, Hövels, Matthes and Proske (eds., 2026), *Ausstiege und (Dis-)Kontinuitäten im Lehrer:innenberuf*, an edited volume covering Germany and Switzerland that frames exits through *Kontinuität*/*Diskontinuität* and crisis management rather than a stay/leave binary. Its recurring empirical claim is that exits are the endpoint of long deliberation, not abrupt events — converging with Hulme et al. **[conceptual/qualitative]**

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** Partly. Workload, leadership and student behaviour recur. But two divergences: (i) the German literature places far more weight on the **absence of team structure** as a distinct cause, which the Anglophone "administrative support" variable does not capture; (ii) the retirement/pre-retirement ratio is a live and shifting quantity, not the settled "retirement is minor" of Ingersoll.

## B.2 The Netherlands

- **Van Casteren, W. et al. (2023), *Vertrekredenen leraren en docenten in het po, vo en mbo*, ResearchNed, commissioned by the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science (OCW), December 2023.** Design: **184 interviews** with teachers who had recently changed jobs or left education, plus **129 written responses** (over 300 respondents in total), across primary (po), secondary (vo) and vocational (mbo) education. Ranked reasons, with the percentages of respondents naming each:
  1. **Workload, stress and burden — 47%**
  2. **Lack of personal challenge and professional development — 41%**
  3. **Dissatisfaction with school board and school leadership — 37%**
  4. **Poor team support and collaboration — 27%**
  5. **Disagreement with the educational vision / lack of autonomy — 25%**
  6. **Pay and conditions of employment — 22%**
  7. **Frustration at one's own impact as an educator — 22%**
  8. **Work–life balance and inflexibility — 14%**
  9. **Autonomous factors (relocation, health) — 12%**
  10. **Contract ending or dismissal — 9%**
  Two findings of first-order importance for our paper: **80% of leavers said they could have been retained "if certain things had been different"**, and **60% still viewed their teaching career positively**. **[descriptive, retrospective self-report, purposive sample — not representative; the percentages are of a self-selected respondent pool]**
- Context: roughly **a third of teachers under 30 stop teaching within five years**; 13–16% of starting secondary teachers leave within five years. **[secondary source]**
- Amsterdam-specific work by Onderzoek en Statistiek finds workload the leading reason for leaving among primary teachers in the city. **[descriptive]**

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** Strong agreement on workload and leadership; **notable divergence in the position of pay (6th, 22%) and in the prominence of "lack of personal challenge" (2nd, 41%)** — a growth/stagnation motive that scarcely appears in US taxonomies, where the equivalent category ("professional development") is treated as a retention *intervention* rather than as a *reason for leaving*. The 80% retainability figure is the strongest single argument in the whole non-Anglophone corpus that attrition is a management problem rather than a supply problem.

## B.3 The Nordic countries

### Finland
**Räsänen, K., J. Pietarinen, K. Pyhältö, T. Soini and P. Väisänen (2020), "Why leave the teaching profession? A longitudinal approach to the prevalence and persistence of teacher turnover intentions", *Social Psychology of Education* 23/4, pp. 837–859.**
- Design: two-wave longitudinal survey of Finnish comprehensive-school teachers, **T1 2010 n = 2 310** (response rate 39%), **T2 2016 n = 1 450** (follow-up response rate 65%); 1 427 responded at both. Open-ended "why" responses content-analysed into five categories. **[associational/descriptive; longitudinal but not causal]**
- **Prevalence: 49.5% had turnover intentions** — 25.5% momentarily, **24.1% persistently**. **62% of those with intentions at T1 still had them five years later.**
- Five inductive categories, with prevalences at T1 and T2:
  - **Lack of commitment** (alienation from the professional goals and values that guided the career choice): **24.9% → 27.5%** — the largest category at both waves.
  - **The school system** (matters the teacher cannot influence: resources, contracts, salary, job description; top-down reform): **21.7% → 24.6%**.
  - **Workload** (affective and physical responses to load): **23.3% → 20.6%**.
  - **Multiple factors**: **14.1% → 16.9%**.
  - **Challenges of interaction** (with staff, students, parents, stakeholders): **16.3% → 10.5%**.
- **The reasons were not persistent even where the intention was** (κ = 0.24, p < 0.001) — the same teacher gives a different reason five years on. This is a serious finding for any taxonomy that treats reasons as stable properties.
- Gender differences at T1: men cited lack of commitment far more (39.3% vs 21.9%); women cited interaction (18.4% vs 7.1%) and workload (26% vs 9%) far more. Still significant but attenuated at T2.
- The authors note their 35–39% prevalence is far above earlier Finnish estimates (OECD 2015: 23–25%) and comparable to Ladd's US 40%.

*Why this matters for us:* Finland is the system Anglophone policy holds up as the counter-example, and half its teachers have considered leaving. And the largest category is **not** workload or pay but **loss of commitment** — i.e. a Kelchtermans-type task-perception failure, which no Anglophone variable taxonomy has a box for.

### Norway
- **Skaalvik, E. M. and S. Skaalvik (2011)**, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 27/6, pp. 1029–1038. 2 569 elementary and middle-school teachers; six school-context variables (value consonance, supervisory support, relations with colleagues, relations with parents, time pressure, discipline problems); all related to job satisfaction and motivation to leave, but **mainly indirectly, through feeling of belonging and emotional exhaustion**. **[associational]** Follow-up longitudinal work (Skaalvik and Skaalvik, 2020/2021, *Teachers and Teaching* 26/7-8) confirms the burnout–satisfaction–motivation chain.
- **Tiplic, D., C. Brandmo and E. Elstad (2015)**, *Cambridge Journal of Education* 45/4, pp. 451–474. 227 beginning teachers in 133 schools. Significant antecedents of turnover intention: **collective teacher efficacy, teacher–principal trust, role conflict, affective commitment**. Explicit conclusion: **organisational and contextual factors, not individual competence perceptions.** **[associational]** A parallel Swedish study by Tiplic and colleagues appears in the *International Journal of Learning, Teaching and Educational Research*.
- **Norwegian FIT-Choice validation** (Nesje et al., 2018) confirms entry motives are comparable to Australian and German ones.

### Sweden
- **Statistics Sweden (SCB), *Lärare utanför yrket* (teachers outside the profession), survey of just over **4 700 former teachers**.** Leading reasons for leaving: **poor work environment, stress and workload.** More than half report that overtime was the rule rather than the exception; six in ten worked overtime at least weekly in their last teaching job. **Roughly half — one framing gives six in ten — would consider returning**, and the main stated condition for returning is **more time for actual teaching**. **[descriptive, retrospective self-report on a register-drawn sample — methodologically stronger than most leaver surveys because the sampling frame is a register, not a volunteer pool]**
- **SCB register statistics (*Lärares sysselsättning*)**: the number of trained teachers rose ~25% in ten years to ~160 000 in 2024, but the number teaching rose less, because the share leaving grew; **2024 saw the largest share ever leave the profession**. **[descriptive]**
- **Sveriges Lärare (2023), *Sökes: fler lärarkollegor***, using SCB's occupational and education registers (2021 cohort): **~35 000 trained teachers, 15% of all teacher-qualified individuals, work outside the profession.** Highest rates in Norrbotten, Västerbotten and Stockholm; lowest in Jönköping, Blekinge and Halland. Vocational and practical-aesthetic subject teachers have the highest exit rates; special-education teachers the lowest. **[descriptive; union report using official registers]**
- Arbetsmiljöverket, *Arbetsmiljön 2024*: **57% of teachers say workload is "always or mostly" too high**, ten percentage points above the all-industry average and the highest of any occupational group. **[descriptive]**

### Denmark
Denmark supplies the single most important non-Anglophone finding in this whole review.
- **ROCKWOOL Foundation (Jacob Nielsen Arendt et al.), register-based study of teachers leaving the *folkeskole*.** Teachers who leave move to jobs paying **about DKK 2 000 less per month**, and their probability of being on sick leave is **12 percentage points lower**. Arendt's interpretation: teachers "are willing to take a pay cut in exchange for working conditions that suit them better". **[quasi-causal at best — a matched register comparison of before/after outcomes for leavers; the direction is robust, the causal interpretation is the authors']** *This is a direct empirical refutation of the naive compensating-differentials story in which exit is wage-driven, and a direct confirmation of the compensating-differentials logic in its sophisticated form: the differential being equalised is a disamenity, not a wage.*
- **Böwadt, P. R., N. K. Vaaben and R. Pedersen (2016/2017), *Hvorfor stopper lærerne i folkeskolen?*, Københavns Professionshøjskole (then UCC).** Qualitative; **400 responses in five days** to an open call on folkeskolen.dk from teachers who left during 2013–15. Four reasons: (1) **forjagede arbejdsmåder** — rushed, fragmented working methods leaving no time for individual students, with documentation crowding out relationship; (2) **udefra og ovenfra kommende styring** — externally and top-down imposed governance narrowing professional scope; (3) **tidspres og klokkeslæt** — time pressure and clock-driven scheduling (a direct consequence of the 2013 reform of teachers' working time); (4) **dårlig ledelse** — leaders prioritising municipal targets over professional support. **[qualitative; self-selected sample, so magnitudes are not interpretable, but the categories are]**
- Danish work also foregrounds **moral stress** (*moralsk stress*): knowing what would be right for pupils and being unable to do it, producing a sustained sense of insufficiency. This is Kelchtermans's task-perception conflict under another name, and it is a mainstream category in Danish policy discourse.
- Scale: **about half of Danish *folkeskole* teachers and pre-school-class leaders have considered leaving**; **one in five newly qualified teachers leaves within three years**. Of those who considered leaving in the past year, **just over half** cite the mismatch between tasks and resources/time; the psychological work environment is the second most-cited. **[descriptive, union member surveys]**

**Nordic verdict.** The Nordic evidence agrees with the Anglophone literature on workload and leadership, but diverges in three specific ways: **(i) pay is demonstrably not the operative margin (Denmark);** **(ii) loss of professional commitment / meaning is the single largest stated category (Finland);** and **(iii) governance and reform — top-down steering, time regulation, curriculum change — appear as a first-order named cause (Denmark, Finland) in a way they do not in the US taxonomies, where "accountability" is an external correlate that turns out to be weakly or even negatively associated with exit.**

## B.4 France

- **Périer, P. (2025), *Choix, non-choix et démission du métier d'enseignant: enquête qualitative sur l'attractivité de l'enseignement*, Document de travail n° 2025-02, Haut-commissariat à la Stratégie et au Plan, Paris, June 2025.** Design: focus groups plus interviews with **~70 people** spanning licence students, MEEF master's students, newly tenured teachers in the public and private sectors, resigners (*démissionnaires*) and recent career-changers (*reconvertis*) — including four interviews with teachers who resigned after several years. **[qualitative; explicitly interpretive, not causal]**
  - Core finding: entry into teaching is **"un choix déterminé, porté par la valeur et le sens des missions"** — a determined choice driven by the value and meaning of the work — but daily practice collides with classroom management, task proliferation and parental relations. The resulting physical and mental wear coexists with **declining status and loss of recognition**, evidenced by salary level and lack of institutional support.
  - Specific French mechanisms: the **cost of entry** (a competitive *concours*, training judged inadequate, a geographically constrained first posting) is judged too high relative to private-sector alternatives; the **non-choice of first posting** is no longer temporary but a multi-year condition; and the **institution's reluctance to permit transfers or resignations**, particularly in under-staffed *académies*, produces a retention-by-obstruction that respondents resent. Resigning also forfeits the *CAPES*, so exit is irreversible — which, Périer argues, both blocks legitimate reconversion and traps disengaged teachers in post.
  - Historical framing: the current crisis is structural, not cyclical. Cnesco reported that in *éducation prioritaire*, requests to leave ran at **90% in 2015 and 70% in 2016**.
- **Scale (DEPP administrative data).** Resignations rose from **0.05% of teachers in 2008/09 to over 0.3% in 2020/21**; **2 836 tenured teachers resigned in 2021/22**. In 2015/16, 4.1% of tenured teachers were on secondment or leave of absence. **[descriptive; via secondary reporting of DEPP — verify against the DEPP *Note d'information*]**
- French sociology of the exit decision links it to **institutional transformation and new public management**: dissatisfaction is rooted in the implementation of NPM and new management practices rather than in the classroom as such. The three-word summary circulating in the French debate — **"salaire, moyens, respect"** (pay, resources, respect) — is a fair compression.

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** The content is familiar (workload, pay, recognition, leadership) but the *structure* is not. Two French-specific categories have no Anglophone equivalent: **the entry cost of the concours**, and **posting/mobility constraint as a push factor operating through the impossibility of internal mobility**. In a nationally administered corps, "leave the school" is not an available move, so school-level turnover — the entire object of Ingersoll's analysis — largely does not exist as a category. Exit is all-or-nothing, and is therefore rarer and more consequential. **Our paper should treat "the availability of internal mobility" as an institutional moderator of the leave decision.**

## B.5 Spain

- **Tahull Fort, J. and F. Molina-Luque (2025), "El abandono de la docencia y la escasez de profesores: datos y análisis sobre sus causas", *Antropología Experimental* 25, pp. 55–68.** Mixed methods: Catalan union vacancy data by specialism, international comparative data (OECD, UNESCO), and semi-structured interviews with three male former teachers aged 38–52 with 9–18 years of experience. **[descriptive/exploratory; N=3 for the qualitative component — cite for the argument, not for magnitudes]** The authors' key structural observation is that **Spain publishes no official data on teacher attrition**, and education competences are devolved to the autonomous communities, so the phenomenon cannot be measured nationally.
- Reported trend: the attrition rate roughly **doubled from 4.6% in 2015 to over 9% in 2022**. **[secondary source — I could not trace the underlying series; treat as indicative and verify before publication]**
- **IX Informe Young Business Talents: la visión del docente** (fieldwork September–November 2025; **634 teachers** in secondary, *bachillerato* and vocational training across Spain): **68.5% have considered leaving the profession**; two in three are dissatisfied with their work situation; eight in ten feel their work is insufficiently recognised. Reasons emphasised: high class ratios, staff shortages, bureaucratic overload, and progressive loss of social recognition — explicitly *not* loss of vocation. **[descriptive; non-probability sample; a private-sector survey, so treat with care]**
- Longitudinal attitudinal deterioration: 38% now report experiencing their work with indifference versus 2% in 2007; 15 years ago 78% firmly rejected the idea of leaving, now 47% are indifferent to it. **[secondary source]**

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** Broadly yes on bureaucracy and recognition. The distinctive Spanish features are (i) **the measurement vacuum itself**, which our paper should name as a policy failure, and (ii) the prominence of **social recognition/status** relative to pay — a theme Spain shares with France, Italy and Portugal and which is markedly weaker in US and English work.

## B.6 Latin America

- **González-Escobar, M., I. Silva-Peña, A. Precht Gandarillas and G. Kelchtermans (2020), "Abandono docente na América Latina: revisão da literatura", *Cadernos de Pesquisa* 50/176, pp. 592–604.** Systematic review of Spanish- and Portuguese-language literature (WoS, EBSCO, SciELO, Dialnet, Redalyc; search terms *abandono docente*, *deserción docente*), focused on teachers leaving classroom work **before completing five years**. **Principal conclusion: the encounter with "school life" (*la vida escolar*) is the main reason for leaving** — that is, the concrete daily reality of the school as a social and political setting, rather than pay or system-level policy. The authors propose **micropolitical literacy** (Kelchtermans and Ballet, 2002) as the appropriate conceptual approach. **[review; conceptual]** *This is the most important single non-Anglophone theoretical contribution in the corpus: a region-wide review that rejects the factor-list approach in favour of a career-story/micropolitical frame.*
- **Chile.** CIAE, Universidad de Chile, register-based analysis 2015–2023: **annual attrition ≈ 4%**, implying an average of **25 years in the labour market**; among young public-sector teachers attrition fell from **5.2% (2015) to 3.2% (2021)**, implying 31 years of expected service. In private schools not covered by the *Ley de Carrera Docente*, attrition is **69% higher** than in public schools, reaching **7.6% in 2021**, which the authors attribute to more unstable contracts producing "pushed" exits and outright dismissals. The improvement in the public sector is attributed to the Teacher Career Law. **[descriptive with a policy-evaluation interpretation — the pre/post comparison across sectors is suggestive of a causal effect of the Ley de Carrera Docente but is not a clean identification]**
- **Ávalos, B. and J. P. Valenzuela (2016)**, on Chilean teacher desertion: key correlates are dissatisfaction with professional development opportunities, salary, complementary benefits, school leadership and management teams, and working conditions. **[associational]**
- **Brazil.** Work on *exoneração* in the São Paulo state network (Pro-Posições) and on intention to abandon among Brazilian teachers (*Psicologia em Pesquisa*) indicates that early-career resignation from state networks is substantial; TALIS 2024 records **47% of Brazilian teachers citing verbal abuse from students as a source of stress**, against an 18% OECD average.

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** The *causal content* is similar (leadership, conditions, pay, development). The **rates are lower** than the Anglophone alarm level in Chile at least, and the **theoretical framing is different**: the leading regional review is explicitly Kelchtermansian, not factor-analytic. Latin America also contributes a category the Anglophone literature lacks a name for: **contract-precarity-driven "pushed" exit**, where the voluntary/involuntary boundary is systematically blurred by fixed-term employment.

## B.7 Italy

- Italy's distinguishing features are **precarity (*precariato*)** and an ageing workforce, alongside very low occupational status.
- TALIS-derived: **social recognition of the profession in Italy is at the OECD minimum, around 12%**. **[secondary source]**
- Reported burnout prevalence: **over 67% of Italian teachers report burnout symptoms (2024 research)**; 73% report work-related stress; sources of stress ranked: students 26%, parents 20%, colleagues 20%, head teacher 2%. **[secondary source, union/press reporting — these figures come from advocacy contexts and should not be cited without tracing the underlying study]**
- Italian secondary reporting of TALIS 2024 gives **23.5% of European teachers intending to leave within five years, 42.7% among the over-50s**, and reports that among Italian under-30s intending to leave, the most frequent motives are **better professional opportunities outside schooling, personal or family needs, and the wish to pursue more specialised study** — i.e. **pull** motives, not push. **[secondary source — worth verifying, because a pull-dominant profile among young leavers would be a genuinely distinctive finding]**
- Barbieri, G., C. Rossetti and P. Sestito (2011), "The determinants of teacher mobility: evidence using Italian teachers' transfer applications", *Economics of Education Review* 30/6, pp. 1430–1444, is the standard quantitative Italian reference — but note that it studies **transfer applications within a centrally administered corps**, i.e. mobility, not attrition. **[associational]**

## B.8 Portugal

Portugal is the most instructive OECD case of a system where **the attrition problem is almost entirely a demographic and entry problem, not an exit problem.**
- **TALIS 2024: fewer than 6% of Portuguese teachers intend to leave within five years — among the lowest in the OECD.** Simultaneously, **under 20% are satisfied with their salary** — also among the lowest. High dissatisfaction with very low exit intention is exactly the *reluctant stayer* cell of Hom et al.'s typology, and Portugal is the clearest natural demonstration that stated reasons and exit behaviour come apart when the outside option is poor.
- Ageing: Portugal has the **oldest teaching workforce in the EU**, average age around 50; **53% of teachers are over 50** (59% in the second cycle of basic education); over 30 000 are over 60. Teachers aged 50+ went from **28% in TALIS 2013 to 47% in TALIS 2018**, against an OECD average of 34%. **[descriptive]**
- **Around 40% of teachers in public schools in 2018/19 will retire by 2030/31; roughly 34 500 teachers must be recruited by 2030.** **[projection]**
- **87% of Portuguese teachers report a high level of stress at work — the highest in the OECD, against an average of 49%.** **[descriptive; TALIS 2018 — verify whether this is the 2018 or 2024 figure before citing]**
- The Conselho Nacional de Educação attributes the shortage to workforce ageing, the collapse of entry into initial teacher education because of low attractiveness, and instability of employment and career progression.

## B.9 Poland, central Europe and the Baltics

**Poland.**
- **University of Warsaw research team for the Związek Nauczycielstwa Polskiego (ZNP), fieldwork February–April 2026: 1 748 teachers from 200 institutions across Poland.** **[descriptive survey; commissioned by a union, which should be flagged]**
  - **One in four teachers is already taking steps to leave education**; **72% cannot say whether they will remain in the profession**.
  - **50%** report high or very high cumulative stress; only 18% describe their load as low. **67.5%** report exhaustion; over half regularly experience negative emotions at work; **60%** report significant physical symptoms (insomnia, chronic pain, exhaustion); **82%** report feeling tired *before* arriving at work.
  - **Of the 20 most burdensome factors, 13 are systemic**: low pay, frequent changes in regulations, having to absorb successive reforms without support, bureaucratic documentation, absence of legal protection in conflicts, inadequate resources for implementing inclusive education. Student behaviour is *not* the leading stressor.
  - **79% of those who left cited pay as the reason.** **[secondary source for this specific figure]**
  - Separately, ~70% of teachers report thinking about leaving because of stress and low pay; Poland is short roughly **20 000 teachers**, gaps filled with overtime hours. **[secondary source]**

**The Baltics — the most extreme intention-to-leave figures in the OECD.** TALIS 2024: **over 30% of all non-retiring teachers in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania intend to leave within five years; approximately half of those under 30; 53% in Latvia.** Given that Estonia is a top PISA performer, this is the sharpest available counter-example to any claim that teacher retention and system performance move together. **These three countries deserve a dedicated paragraph in our paper; I did not find country-language primary studies of *reasons* for them in this pass, and that is a gap worth closing.**

**Central Europe generally.** Bulgaria is one of the systems where intention to leave among 30–49-year-olds has *fallen* by 5 pp or more since 2018 (TALIS 2024) — heterogeneity within the region is large.

## B.10 Japan

Japan is the clearest case in which the Anglophone framing is category-mismatched, because the dominant exit-adjacent phenomenon is not resignation but **health-related leave and long-hours-driven incapacity**.

- **MEXT, *学校教員統計調査* (School Teachers Survey), FY2022 confirmed results (published 27 March 2024).** Public elementary schools, FY2021: **15 030 leavers in total**, down 1 589 on the previous survey. Breakdown by reason:
  - **定年 (retirement, incl. early-retirement encouragement): 8 006** (~53%) — down from **12 005 in FY2012**, 11 642 in FY2015, 10 235 in FY2018.
  - **転職 (change of occupation): 2 098** — up from **1 318 in FY2012** (a ~59% increase).
  - **家庭の事情 (family circumstances): 1 879**
  - **病気 (illness): 753, of which 精神疾患 (mental illness) 569** — up from 589/350 in FY2012.
  - **死亡 (death): 139**; **職務上の問題 (job-related problems): 111**; **大学等入学: 44**; **その他: 2 000**.
  - MEXT's own summary: apart from retirement, the most common reasons are **career change, family circumstances and illness**.
  **[descriptive; official triennial census of teachers]**
- **Mental-health crisis indicators.** FY2023: **7 119 public-school staff on sick leave for mental illness** — the first time above 7 000 and a record; nearly half in elementary schools; most common among teachers in their 30s; **2.02% of teachers in their twenties** took a month or more of mental-health leave (one in fifty). FY2024: **1 319 teachers resigned due to mental illness**, a record and the first time above 1 000, up 369 on the FY2021 figure. When MEXT asked about causes of sick leave for the first time, **"matters related to guidance of pupils and students" was the most common factor**. MEXT's own analysis: teacher shortage concentrates work on "capable teachers" while thinning the mid-career ranks who could advise them — a compounding cycle. **[descriptive, administrative]**

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** No, structurally. (i) **Retirement remains the majority of exits**, contradicting Ingersoll's headline. (ii) The rising component is **health-driven incapacity**, which Anglophone taxonomies handle only as an "involuntary/personal" residual. (iii) Long hours are the acknowledged systemic cause, but the outcome variable is 休職 (leave) rather than 離職 (exit) — a *partial and reversible* withdrawal that no Anglophone taxonomy contains. Japan is the strongest evidence for adding a "degree of withdrawal" dimension to any classification we design.

## B.11 Korea

- **Scale.** Early retirements rose from **869 in 2005 to 6 594 in 2021** (Korean Educational Development Institute, 2023 report); resignations from **5 937 in 2019 to 7 404 in 2023**; honorary retirements (available after 20 years' service) from **5 242 in 2021 to 6 480 in 2023**. **[descriptive; via press reporting of KEDI figures]**
- **Attitudes.** KEDI survey: **30.2% of Korean elementary teachers held a negative attitude toward teaching in 2023, nearly double the 17% of the previous year.** In a survey of the causes of the exodus, **40.9% attributed it to the infringement of teachers' rights (교권 침해)** — the erosion of teachers' authority in the face of parental complaints and legal exposure, which became a national issue after the Seoi Elementary School case of 2023. Student management, undermined teaching authority and low compensation are the three headline causes. **[descriptive survey; secondary source]**
- **Turnover intention by age.** Among Korean middle-school teachers, roughly **25%** were likely to leave within five years — but with an extreme age gradient: **~3% among those in their twenties and ~60% among those in their fifties** (Korean-language study: "누가 교직을 떠나려 하는가?"). **[associational]** *Note the direct contradiction with TALIS 2024, which puts Korea below 6% overall and is widely quoted as showing Korea has no retention problem. The two are reconcilable — TALIS excludes those about to retire and the Korean study's 50-something figure is largely retirement-adjacent — but the discrepancy is a warning about how the retirement/pre-retirement boundary is drawn.*
- Korean burnout research is developing rapidly (e.g. a 2026 *Teaching and Teacher Education* study of elementary teachers' burnout trajectories and the "dual aspects" of teacher collaboration — collaboration as both resource and demand). **[associational, longitudinal]**

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** Partially. Workload and pay recur. But **"infringement of teachers' rights" — legal and reputational exposure to parental complaint — is the leading named cause and has no counterpart in any Anglophone taxonomy.** The nearest Anglophone category, "student discipline problems", misses the point entirely: the threat is from parents and the legal system, not from children. This is a strong argument for a taxonomy category covering **professional authority, legal exposure and status defence**.

## B.12 China

Chinese research is voluminous, overwhelmingly on **turnover intention** rather than behaviour, overwhelmingly on **rural teachers**, and heavily psychological in framing.
- **Yunnan Province: survey of 5 342 rural teachers** on turnover intention. **[associational]**
- **Western China: 973 rural teachers**, SEM using Herzberg's two-factor framing: both hygiene and motivation factors significantly reduce turnover intention, with **motivation factors having the larger effect**. **[associational]** *Note that Chinese work often uses Herzberg rather than JD-R or push/pull — a fifth taxonomy tradition our review should acknowledge.*
- **Shaanxi (Yulin City): 588 rural teachers.** Overall retention intention "not very strong"; significant differences by age, marital status, education level, years of teaching and living conditions. Professional identity and occupational well-being predict retention intention. **[associational]**
- Regional sorting: teachers in the western and central regions prefer to move east, producing high rural turnover; rural teachers' *professional honour* (职业荣誉感) is lowest at ages 31–40, coinciding with peak turnover. **[associational]**
- A cluster of studies on Chinese rural kindergarten teachers examines destructive leadership, negative workplace gossip and ego depletion as predictors of turnover intention, moderated by **编制 (bianzhi)** — establishment/tenured civil-service status. **[associational]** *Bianzhi is the Chinese analogue of the French statut and the Italian ruolo: an institutional variable determining whether exit is even available, and completely absent from Anglophone taxonomies.*
- University-sector: multiple job demands and Chinese university teachers' turnover intentions (*Asia-Pacific Education Researcher*, 2023).

**Agreement with the Anglophone pattern?** The correlates are familiar; the **framing and the moderators are not**. The distinctively Chinese contribution is the **status/establishment variable (bianzhi)** and the **geographic sorting gradient**, plus the near-total focus on intention rather than behaviour, which limits what can be concluded.

## B.13 What the non-Anglophone evidence changes

1. **Salary is not the operative margin, and one register study says so cleanly.** Danish leavers take a **DKK ~2 000/month pay cut** and a 12-percentage-point reduction in sick-leave probability. Nguyen et al.'s pooled salary odds ratio in the US is barely below 1. Yet pay ranks 6th (22%) in the Dutch leaver survey and 1st (79%) in the Polish one — the *rank* of pay is a function of the national wage level, not of the causal structure.
2. **Retirement's share of exits is a national parameter, not a constant.** Japan ~53%; Germany's non-retirement:retirement ratio 2.6:1 and rising; the US ~18%. Any taxonomy that treats retirement as background noise is an Anglophone artefact.
3. **The availability of *internal* mobility restructures the whole decision.** France, Italy, Japan and China administer teachers as a national or provincial corps. "Leave the school" is not on the menu, so the mover/leaver distinction collapses and the exit decision becomes all-or-nothing and irreversible (in France, resigning forfeits the *CAPES*). Conversely, where mobility is easy, much of what is measured as "turnover" is redistribution.
4. **Loss of professional meaning is the single largest stated category where it is measured qualitatively.** Finland: "lack of commitment" 24.9% → 27.5%, the largest of five categories. Latin America: "the encounter with school life". Denmark: "moral stress". France: vocation intact, conditions intolerable. This category is systematically absent from the variable taxonomies.
5. **Status, recognition and professional authority are first-order named causes outside the Anglosphere.** Korea's 교권 침해 (40.9%); Italy's OECD-minimum 12% social recognition; Spain's eight-in-ten "insufficiently recognised"; France's "respect".
6. **Partial and reversible withdrawal is real and large.** Japan's 休職 (7 119 on mental-health leave, dwarfing the 1 319 who resigned for that reason); Scotland's 27 downscalers; Germany's AVEM pattern S; the Netherlands' 80% retainable; Sweden's ~half who would return.
7. **The Baltic anomaly.** Over 30% intention to leave in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania — including a high-performing system — against under 6% in Korea and Portugal. No factor taxonomy currently explains a 5-to-10-fold cross-national range in intention.
8. **But the underlying association structure travels.** Li and Yao (2022), across 94 studies and 39 508 teachers, find the antecedent–intention relations "stable regardless of ... cultural context". So the non-Anglophone evidence disturbs the *levels*, the *ranks* and the *categories*, not the *directions*.

# PART C — THREE CANDIDATE CLASSIFICATIONS FOR THIS PAPER

Each is stated as dimensions, then what it groups, then what it separates, then its main weakness.

---

## Candidate 1 — **The Lever Taxonomy**: classify reasons by *who can act on them*

**Dimensions (one axis, four ordered levels):**
1. **Person and life course** — health, family, geography, ageing, entry motive and mis-selection, competing careers.
2. **Workplace as organisation** — leadership and support, collegial structure and teamwork, timetabling and time allocation, induction and mentoring, discipline support, voice in decisions.
3. **Occupation and its regulation** — pay scale and career structure, contract type and precarity, workload norms and working-time rules, accountability and inspection, curriculum prescription, entry route and qualification portability, mobility and posting rules.
4. **Society and labour market** — status and public regard, legal exposure and parental relations, outside-option wages, unemployment, demographic pressure.

**What it groups.** All of Nguyen et al.'s "external correlates" plus most of what the Nordic and French literatures call "the school system" collapse into level 3, where they belong: a ministry can change working-time rules, contract law and accountability regimes, and a principal cannot. Danish "top-down governance", Finnish "the school system", Polish "systemic factors" (13 of the top 20 burdens) and French "new public management" all land in the same box, which is correct and currently they do not.

**What it separates.** It separates **teamwork and collegiality (level 2) from pay and contract (level 3)** — the German NRW result and the Danish leadership result would be visibly different objects from the Polish pay result. It separates **status and legal exposure (level 4) from workplace discipline problems (level 2)**, which is exactly the distinction the Korean 교권 침해 finding requires and which "student behaviour" currently obscures.

**Main weakness.** Attribution is contestable and politically loaded. Workload sits at level 2 (timetabling), level 3 (working-time rules and curriculum) and level 4 (societal expectations of schools) simultaneously, and placing it determines who is blamed. It also has no theory: the levels are a governance map, not a causal model, so it cannot say which level dominates.

---

## Candidate 2 — **The Exit Architecture**: classify by the *structure of the exit decision*, not by its content

**Dimensions (three axes, applied jointly):**
- **Axis A — Desire × constraint** (from Hom et al., 2012): *enthusiastic stayer / reluctant stayer / reluctant leaver / enthusiastic leaver*.
- **Axis B — Degree of withdrawal**: *full exit from the profession / move within the profession / partial withdrawal (reduced hours, relinquished responsibility, extended sick leave, internal disengagement) / intention only*.
- **Axis C — Temporal form** (from Lee and Mitchell, 1994): *shock-and-script / shock-and-image-violation / shock-search-compare / gradual accumulation without shock*.

**What it groups.** It groups together phenomena that current taxonomies scatter: Japanese 休職, German AVEM pattern S, Scottish downscaling and Portuguese reluctant staying are all recognised as *the same species of outcome* — withdrawal without exit — and can be counted as such. It groups the Dutch 80%-retainable, the Swedish would-return half and the Chilean re-entrants under a single "reversibility" heading.

**What it separates.** It separates the **decision to want out** from the **ability to get out**, which is the single most consequential distinction the field currently lacks: Portugal (dissatisfied, 6% intending to leave) and Latvia (53% of the young intending to leave) are not measuring the same thing, and this scheme says why. It also separates **shock-driven from accumulated exit**, which matters directly for intervention timing — Hulme et al. found accumulation over months to years, i.e. extended windows in which retention was still possible.

**Main weakness.** It classifies exits, not reasons, so it cannot on its own answer the paper's title question; it must be crossed with a content taxonomy (Candidate 1 or 3). It is also demanding of data: axes A and C require interview or panel data, and almost no national statistical system collects them. Applied to existing administrative data it would be mostly empty cells.

---

## Candidate 3 — **The Bargain Taxonomy**: classify by *which part of the implicit bargain of teaching has broken*

**Dimensions (five clauses of the bargain a teacher enters into):**
1. **The purpose clause** — I will be able to do the good I came to do. *Broken by:* meaninglessness, moral stress, inability to help the pupils one judges need help, curriculum and assessment crowding out teaching. (Finland's "lack of commitment", Denmark's *moralsk stress*, Latin America's "encounter with school life", the Dutch "frustration at my own impact", 22%.)
2. **The load clause** — the work will be finishable. *Broken by:* hours, intensification, administrative accretion, staffing gaps absorbed as overtime. (Sweden's 57% "always or mostly too high", TALIS's 52% naming administrative work, Poland, Japan.)
3. **The standing clause** — I will be treated as a professional, by employer, parents and public. *Broken by:* loss of autonomy and voice, top-down steering, legal and reputational exposure, low social recognition. (Korea's 교권 침해, Ingersoll's "influence in decision-making", Collie's autonomy-thwarting leadership, Italy's 12%, France's "respect".)
4. **The company clause** — I will not be alone. *Broken by:* absence of team structure, weak collegiality, isolation of the classroom, poor induction. (Germany's *Einzelkämpfertum* and the 4.06 vs 3.45 team-resilience gap; Collie's finding that relatedness with colleagues was the *only* directly protective resource; Tiplic et al.'s collective efficacy and principal trust; Denmark's leadership.)
5. **The livelihood clause** — the job will pay and last. *Broken by:* pay level relative to alternatives and to living costs, contract precarity, career-progression blockage, posting and mobility constraint. (Poland's 79%, Italy's *precariato*, Chile's 69% higher attrition in non-covered private schools, TALIS's 14% on contracts of a year or less, France's *concours* entry cost.)

**What it groups.** It puts each empirical finding with the finding it is actually about rather than with the finding that shares its administrative label: "workload" in the sense of hours goes to clause 2, but "workload" in the sense of paperwork displacing teaching goes to clause 1 or 3 depending on whether the complaint is about purpose or about being distrusted. It gives the Kelchtermans/Finnish/Latin American "loss of meaning" evidence a clause of its own — clause 1 — which no existing taxonomy provides.

**What it separates.** It separates **pay from precarity** (both in clause 5 but distinguishable), and, more importantly, separates **isolation (clause 4) from lack of support from leadership (clause 3)** — currently merged in the Anglophone "administrative support" variable, and the German and Danish evidence says they are different problems with different remedies. It also separates **autonomy (clause 3) from load (clause 2)**, which JD-R merges as "demands".

**Main weakness.** The clauses are not empirically derived and are not orthogonal — clause 1 and clause 3 breaches co-occur almost always, and a single respondent statement ("I spend all my time on paperwork") can be coded to three clauses. It is also a *normative* frame: it presupposes what teaching ought to offer, which is defensible in a policy paper but is not a neutral measurement instrument, and it will not map onto any existing dataset without recoding.

---

### A note on combining them
The three are not rivals at the same level. **Candidate 1 is the policy spine** (who acts), **Candidate 3 is the content spine** (what broke), and **Candidate 2 is the cross-cut** (what kind of exit, and whether it was even available). A defensible design for the paper is to use **Candidate 3 as the primary section structure**, tag every finding with a **Candidate 1 lever level** so the recommendations chapter writes itself, and use **Candidate 2 as a standing analytic caution** applied at the start and end — because it is the only one of the three that can explain why a system can have miserable teachers and almost no attrition, or contented teachers and a lot.
