# 3. Policies to bring former teachers back

Governments have implemented policies to re-attract former teachers for decades, with two purposes that recur across otherwise dissimilar systems. The first is cover, in which returners fill posts that would otherwise stand vacant or pass to unqualified substitutes. The second is the recovery of experienced capacity, since a returner arrives with classroom experience that initial teacher education cannot produce at short notice and does not consume a training place. The OECD identified the policy field as early as 2005, recommending that education systems maintain contact with former teachers, keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities, and provide targeted retraining (OECD, 2005). The international record now contains statutory return-to-work windows, waiver regimes, employer subsidies, letter campaigns and redesigned roles across North America, Europe, Asia and Oceania, most of them adopted or expanded under post-pandemic staffing pressure.

These instruments group into five families according to what they change (Table 2). Pension and employment rules alter the statutory terms on which a pensioner or leaver may be re-employed; certification and licensing routes back remove the regulatory steps between a lapsed licence and a classroom; a third family attaches money to the act of returning; a fourth has education authorities locate former teachers and accompany them back; a fifth defines a role, such as structured tutoring or mentoring, other than the classroom post the leaver gave up. The first two families act on what this paper terms the price of a return, the sum of salary-step placement, pension-salary concurrency, waiting periods and licensing costs that a qualified former teacher faces when weighing re-entry; the remaining families add money, information or a different job on top of whatever price the rules have set. The families correspond to the exit motives documented in Section 2, and the final column of Table 1 indicates which family addresses which motive.

The record these families have produced consists mainly of administrative take-up counts rather than evaluations, and the counts measure different outcomes against different denominators, so any comparative reading must stay modest. One pattern is nonetheless visible. Among the many campaigns implemented to attract teachers back, both those providing incentives and those easing certification, there is suggestive evidence that they weigh less than changes to the legal rules of the game, which appear more successful in re-attracting teachers. The largest documented return flows follow changes to pension and employment rules, while marketing-led and subsidy-led schemes have converted registrations into placements at rates their own sponsors describe as disappointing. That reading rests on uncontrolled episodes rather than on designed comparisons, and it is qualified wherever the underlying counts are missing or unpublished.

## 3.1 Pension and employment rules that set the price of a return

For a retired teacher, the price of a return is set by the pension system before any school makes an offer. Governments in this family spend little new money; they move the statutory settings, the earnings caps, waiting periods, service thresholds and suspension provisions, that determine what a pensioner forfeits by drawing a salary again. The route the family enables is the re-employment of pension-drawing retirees, occasionally of all leavers, on terms the pension system will tolerate. Its evidence base is the strongest of the five families, because rule changes generate administrative take-up data as a by-product, and because in North Carolina and Georgia (United States) and in Austria the resulting record has been examined at arm's length rather than by the sponsoring authority.

Across the systems whose rules could be documented, the price of a return is set on four regulatory dials (Table 3). A pay dial governs salary-step placement and pension-salary concurrency; an exclusion dial sets waiting periods, service thresholds and eligibility screens; an intensity dial caps how much a returner may work; a licence dial determines what re-certification a return requires. Systems position the dials very differently. Austria's crediting rules place formerly civil-servant returners at the starting salary, because service that already generates a pension is excluded from crediting, while former contract-scheme teachers broadly recover their pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023). Ontario prices intensity rather than pay, through a cap on days worked per school year. Georgia stacks the exclusion dial, combining a long service threshold with a waiting period and a subject screen. The dials matter jointly, since a return that is permitted on one dial can remain unaffordable on another, and most systems document their settings without publishing what happened under them.

The family's benchmark case is the return-to-work window that North Carolina (United States) operated between 1999 and 2009, the only pension-rule re-entry episode anywhere to have received a large-scale peer-reviewed evaluation (Box 1). Suspending the rule under which a full-time return cost a retiree the pension, without any bonus payment, drew roughly 3 500 retirees back into full-time posts over the decade, 2.1% of the sampled workforce, and returns ended when the window expired (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). The episode supports two claims found nowhere else at this scale. Removing the concurrency penalty can re-activate retirees in numbers that campaigns do not approach, and the returners flowed disproportionately to schools serving disadvantaged students without any mandate directing them there. What the evaluation cannot support is equally well defined, since the data contain no vacancy or achievement counterfactual, and some hires may have displaced other candidates.

**Box 1. The return-to-work window in North Carolina (United States), 1999-2009**

The Teachers' and State Employees' Retirement System allowed retirement at any age after 30 years of service, but a retiree returning to full-time teaching forfeited pension and retiree health benefits, and part-time work was permitted only under an earnings cap of half the previous full-time salary. In 1999 the General Assembly suspended that rule, initially for low-performing schools only and from June 2000 for all schools. Under the window, a retiree could teach full-time on a full salary while drawing a full pension, for a combined income of up to about 50% more than that of a regular full-time teacher. The window was repeatedly extended and expired in autumn 2009, when the earlier earnings cap returned.

The evaluation, on administrative records covering about 169 000 teachers in 2 900 schools, counted roughly 3 500 returners over the decade. More than half of schools hired at least one, 54% of returners went back to their pre-retirement school, and returners were disproportionately Black, more likely to teach mathematics, and more likely to work in schools with higher shares of economically disadvantaged students. By 2009, just over 35% of teachers who had been retirement-eligible in the previous year were returning to full-time work; after the window closed, returns fell to zero. The authors note that the marginal cost of a returner is the salary difference relative to a novice, since the annuity is paid regardless. The data permit no conclusions about effects on vacancies or student achievement, and cannot rule out that some hires displaced other candidates.

In Georgia (United States), a notable example of statutory self-evaluation, House Bill 385 allowed selected retirees to teach full-time on a full pension and wrote a performance audit into the statute itself (Box 2). The design stacked the exclusion dial, combining a long service threshold, a waiting period and a subject-and-region screen, the most restrictive combination among the eight southern states with comparable laws. The mandated audit counted 635 full-time returners over three fiscal years, under 1% of the workforce in any given year against roughly 6 000 vacant or not-fully-certified posts; it reported that nearly half of surveyed school systems said the statutory conditions had blocked a hire they would otherwise have made, and it judged the effect on the shortage and on the pension fund alike to be minimal (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025). On the authority of an official audit rather than of advocacy, the case supports the claim that stacked eligibility screens depress take-up. It cannot show that a looser design would have filled the vacancies.

New Mexico marks the permissive end of the same design spectrum. Legislation passed near-unanimously lets educational retirees return to any local administrative unit for an extended period with no limits on salary or hours and no pension suspension, subject to a layout of about three months, board approval and a prohibition on pre-arranged returns (New Mexico Legislature, 2022; New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2025). The pension board's tiered reporting counted 2 792 working retirees across all four statutory routes in the first fiscal year, of whom 154 used the new no-limits route, against roughly 48 000 potentially eligible retirees (New Mexico Educational Retirement Board, 2023).[^1] No outcome evaluation exists. The first-year figure carries a caution for the family as a whole, since even a maximally permissive rule converted only a small fraction of the retiree stock.

Several other American systems have moved the same dials without publishing what followed. Recent Michigan legislation replaced a patchwork of critical-shortage exceptions with two conditions, a bona fide termination and a waiting period, after which a retiree may work in any public-school position with no earnings limit; a later amendment cut the wait from nine months to six (Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2022; Michigan Office of Retirement Services, 2023). A Virginia executive directive repackaged a statutory mechanism dating from 2001, under which retirees may draw salary and pension concurrently in designated critical-shortage positions, and added the licensure fast-tracking treated in the next subsection (Will, 2022; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023; Virginia Retirement System, n.d.). Louisiana has liberalised progressively, first extending its no-suspension critical-shortage route to all retirees and then consolidating the rules from 2026. The consolidated law adds a route with no pension impact for retirees aged 65 and over and a market test under which a critical-shortage hire requires the employer to have received fewer than three certified applications for the advertised post; it also schedules an actuarial review before the 2034 legislative session (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2024; Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). None of the three systems has published take-up counts, so their record consists of rule design alone.

A recurring design choice across these American cases is the sunset clause. North Carolina's window was allowed to expire, Georgia's statute carried an automatic expiry, Michigan's shortened waiting period reverts to the longer one unless renewed, and Virginia's amended provisions expire in the same period. Tying a loosened rule to the shortage episode that justified it keeps the pension concession reviewable, and in Georgia the expiry date is what forced the take-up evidence into existence, since the audit was due before the renewal decision. The pattern suggests that legislatures treat concurrency concessions as exceptional, which is consistent with their fiscal logic, in that every loosening also pays pensioners who might have returned anyway.

The Canadian province of Ontario shows that the intensity dial turns in both directions. Under the Teachers' Pension Act, a retired member may work 50 days per school year without affecting the pension; the ceiling was raised from 20 to 95 days during an earlier severe shortage and cut back after a newspaper investigation found the ten largest school boards spending over CAD 100 million a year on retired teachers while newly certified graduates outnumbered openings by roughly two to one (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026; Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). The higher ceiling returned temporarily during the pandemic years by agreement between the co-sponsors (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2022; OTF, 2023). Because changes require the agreement of those co-sponsors, the provincial government and the Ontario Teachers' Federation, organised labour holds a veto, and the federation refused a further extension once the temporary ceiling had lapsed, on the stated ground that the pension plan is not a tool for addressing labour-market challenges (OCEOTA, 2024; Alphonso, 2024). Ontario is the only documented case in which a re-employment ceiling was lowered for equity reasons, to protect the hiring prospects of new graduates. The pandemic episode also gauges appetite, since only around sixty of the plan's roughly 142 000 retired members had expressed interest in returning by the second pandemic winter (Global News, 2022).

France illustrates a price that education authorities do not control. Under the "cumul emploi-retraite" framework, a six-month waiting period applies to resuming work with one's last employer, a rule designed to stop employers re-hiring their own just-retired staff at lower pay; because the education ministry is a single employer, the rule delays every retired public-school teacher who wishes to return, including as a substitute (Assemblée nationale, 2020). Teachers who left before 2015 with early pension rights also face income ceilings that teachers with complete careers do not, a differential the government has defended on contributory grounds while pointing to harmonising legislation (Assemblée nationale, 2026). While a recent pension reform liberalised the framework and lets resumed work accrue new pension rights, no aggregate count of retired teachers returning under it has been published, and a parliamentary question proposing to lift the obstacles for substitute work lapsed unanswered (Assemblée nationale, 2023; Assemblée nationale, 2024). The French case is thus a barrier documented in parliamentary exchange rather than a policy documented in take-up.

In England, by contrast, the equivalent barrier is dissolving without any reform decision. Abatement, the suspension of pension above an earnings ceiling, attaches only to legacy final-salary benefits, and all service since 2015 accrues in a career-average scheme whose benefits are never abated, so the constraint applies to a shrinking cohort of entitlements (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.). A pension barrier can therefore disappear passively through scheme succession, which places England at the zero-reform end of a spectrum whose opposite end, in Georgia, legislates each screen explicitly.

The family's record supports a conditional learning rather than a universal one. Where the concurrency penalty was removed cleanly, in North Carolina, retirees returned at a scale that no outreach or subsidy scheme in the record approaches. Where screens were stacked, in Georgia, an official audit found take-up too small to matter, and most systems in between have not counted at all. No system has varied one dial while holding the others constant, so the inference that the pay and exclusion dials govern take-up remains suggestive. What the family establishes beyond that is procedural, since the arms-length examinations it contains are the only ones in the whole re-entry record, and each exists because a rule change left administrative traces that a body was obliged to inspect.

## 3.2 Certification and licensing routes back

A former teacher who has decided to return must still pass the regulator. Lapsed practising certificates, refresher requirements, re-accreditation paperwork and vetting stand between a qualified person and a classroom, and each step carries fees, delays and uncertainty that fall entirely on the returner. Governments in this family remove those steps, seeking the conversion of an already-qualified stock at low fiscal cost, and the routes they enable run from statutory relief-teaching allowances and refresher waivers to licensing relief folded into pension legislation.

The empirical warrant for the family comes from Michigan, the one system to have measured how far certification decays once teachers leave. Between 44% and 49% of the 61 252 certified Michigan teachers who were not teaching in a public school in the most recent measured year held expired certificates, against 6% of active teachers (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). An expired certificate can be restored, but roughly half of the visible reserve would face re-certification before any return, which makes relicensing friction a first-order policy margin rather than an administrative detail. The same analysis cautions against treating the certified stock as available supply, since it also contains out-of-state residents and subject mismatches.

The most complete removal of re-certification friction in the record is New Zealand's, assembled from separate instruments rather than announced as a programme. A standing statutory rule allows a registered teacher whose practising certificate has expired to relief teach for up to twenty half-days per calendar year without a certificate, with extensions available where an employment offer exists (New Zealand Legislation, 2020; New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026). A returner with a relief-teaching job offer is exempted from the refresher course normally required after five or more years out of teaching, agreeing instead a light-touch "Kia Maia | Future-Ready Teaching Plan" with the principal, and the exemption has effectively been mainstreamed, since the Teaching Council's 2026 registration policy makes the refresher contingent mainly on not having a job offer (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026). The government also pays practising-certificate fees and levies until mid-2028 (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2026). The design principle is consistent across the package, in that friction is removed as a standing rule keyed to a job offer, treating the offer itself as the market test of whether a return is real. While the package is the clearest example of regulatory barrier removal for re-entry in the record, no take-up figures or evaluation have been published for any element of it.

Licensing relief also travels inside instruments that belong primarily to other families. Virginia's directive ordered the schools superintendent to expedite renewal licences for retired teachers whose licences had lapsed and to classify all vacant teaching posts so that retirees could fill them, a fast, legislature-free lever on the licensing margin whose effects were never counted (Will, 2022; Commonwealth of Virginia, 2023). Louisiana's consolidated return-to-work law provides that, from mid-2026, a critical-shortage hire requires only general teacher certification rather than a subject-specific match (Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana, 2026). While neither measure has published counts, together they indicate that certification-matching requirements are themselves an adjustable screen, and that legislatures tend to bundle licensing relief inside pension bills rather than treat it as a separate policy.

Ireland applies the family's logic to teachers who have not fully left. Registered teachers may take unpaid career breaks of up to five years at a time with a right of return, and the scheme ordinarily bars them from publicly funded teaching posts and caps their substitute work (Department of Education, 2019). Since at least 2021/22 the education department has suspended those restrictions annually, most recently for 2025/26, so that career-break teachers may substitute without restriction and are paid as regular substitutes, with substitute days counting toward salary progression under the general rules (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). The population addressed is the least costly reserve to activate, because it never fully left and its registration has not lapsed. At the same time, the instrument illustrates a design weakness the rest of the family avoids, an annually renewed waiver that a prospective returner cannot plan a school year around, and no counts of the substitution flexibility's use have been published.

This family is therefore documented as design and undocumented as outcomes. In no system in the family has a take-up count or an evaluation been published, a gap that is itself informative given how inexpensive the counting would be, since every waiver granted and every certificate restored passes through a regulator's records. The learning the family supports is accordingly a design principle, that friction removal appears most credible as a standing rule tied to a job offer rather than as an annually renewed concession, and the principle awaits its first published test.

## 3.3 Money attached to the return

A third family attaches cash to the act of returning, through per-head subsidies to employers, monthly supplements, annual allowances and relocation or bonding grants. The temptation to read this family through the causal literature on teacher pay should be resisted. Quasi-experimental studies show that targeted payments retain serving teachers (Section 2), but no such study anywhere directly prices re-entry, and elasticities estimated on people still in post cannot be carried over to people who have already revealed a preference against current conditions (Fleck et al., 2025). What the family offers instead is take-up evidence under real budgets, from three governments that published at least part of what happened.

A notable example is the case of the Netherlands, the system with the best-measured teacher reserve, where a hidden reserve of roughly 62 000 qualified people stood against a measured shortage of 13 600 full-time-equivalent posts (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b). The dedicated activation instrument, the "Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs", paid primary school boards, not teachers, a one-off EUR 2 500 per returner appointed for at least six months after at least twelve months out of teaching, to finance the board's induction and guidance of the returner, with an initial budget sized for five hundred returners (Ministerie van OCW, 2017). Just over a hundred returners were subsidised in the first full year and nearly two hundred cumulatively by the second; the ministry then lowered its own annual ceilings explicitly because the budget was not being exhausted, and the regulation lapsed without a published evaluation (Ministerie van OCW, 2019a; Ministerie van OCW, 2019b; Tweede Kamer, 2019). Under-subscription is thus documented in the sponsor's own regulatory text. The episode cannot show that subsidies fail in general, since the instrument was small and paid to boards rather than to returners, but it does indicate that a well-measured reserve did not respond to a marginal payment.

Portugal ran the family's clearest natural comparison, although not by design. Decreto-Lei 51/2024, part of the "+Aulas +Sucesso" plan, created two instruments in one law. Under one route, teachers retired for five years or less may return on fixed-term contracts in shortage subjects or understaffed schools, keeping their full pension plus compensation indexed to the first step of the career salary scale, pro-rated by teaching hours; under the other, teachers who meet retirement conditions but stay in the classroom to the end of the school year receive a monthly supplement of EUR 750 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024). Against a government quota of 200 returner contracts for the first school year, 63 retirees were accepted, most of them near the end of their sixties (Diário de Notícias, 2024; Renascença, 2024). Roughly 2 200 teachers extended their careers under the stay-on supplement in its second year, after which the government confined it to shortage areas, conceding that the blanket version was in many cases not justified (RTP, 2026). The two routes differ in design as well as in money, so the comparison is not a controlled one; what it suggests is that, at broadly comparable cost per teacher, preventing a departure is far easier than reversing one. Teacher unions read the returner figures as revealed preference, with one federation describing the idea that the retired would return as an illusion (SÁBADO, 2024).

In China, money is attached to a defined destination rather than to return as such. The National Silver Age Teachers Action Plan, issued by the education ministry together with nine other agencies, expands an earlier rural lecturing scheme into a target corps of roughly 120 000 silver-age teachers across five strands of the education system, including part-time and volunteer service (MOE et al., 2023; MOE and MOF, 2018). In the school strand, retired principals, teaching-research staff, special-grade and senior teachers, generally aged sixty-five or under, keep their full pension and receive working funds of CNY 20 000 per person per year for service in poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority and border counties, with housing, travel and insurance provided by hosts and the central budget carrying the full cost in western provinces (MOE et al., 2023; State Council, 2023). The predecessor schemes had cumulatively recruited over 20 000 retired schoolteachers by the time the plan was issued (State Council, 2023; MOE, 2025). All published figures are government-reported recruitment counts, no independent evaluation exists, and secondary coverage warns that some localities may use silver-age recruits to delay hiring new full-time teachers (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025). As policy design, however, the plan is the family's cleanest example of targeting, since the allowance compensates one identifiable cost of returning, service in remote counties, rather than subsidising returns that might have happened anyway.

New Zealand has recently opened place-based money to returners. From late 2025, a bonding scheme pays any teacher appointed to a designated vacancy at a priority staffing school rising annual amounts of up to NZD 40 000 before tax over five years, and a relocation grant of up to NZD 10 000 supports New Zealand-trained teachers returning from abroad (New Zealand Government, 2025). Both instruments price location rather than return as such, and both are too recent for any take-up to have been published.

Across the family, the take-up records point in one direction, although none of them is causal. Modest money went largely unclaimed where working conditions had caused the exits, in the Netherlands and in Portugal's returner route, while money aimed at a specific, identifiable friction, remoteness in the Chinese case, recruited at scale on the government's own count. The evidence therefore suggests that a payment attached to the act of returning does not re-price a job the leaver has already judged, and that the margin money moves most readily is the one it moved in Portugal, the decision of a teacher still in post to stay another year.

## 3.4 Active recruitment and accompaniment

The fourth family treats the reserve as a recruitment problem. Education authorities locate their own former teachers, through departmental staffing or pension records or through open marketing, contact them directly and accompany the return with a named adviser, bundled re-licensure, refresher training and placement support. The approach operationalises a recommendation the OECD made in 2005, to maintain contact with former teachers, keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities and provide targeted retraining schemes (OECD, 2005). It is also the only family that has ever published a complete recruitment funnel, from contacts through expressions of interest to placements, which makes it the family in which conversion can actually be observed.

England has run the family's instruments for longest and evaluated them most rigorously. The Return to Teaching pilot of 2015-16 offered returners to priority secondary subjects non-financial support, subject-knowledge refreshers, classroom experience and mentoring, through grant-funded school partnerships behind a national campaign. Registrations ran at nearly double the target, 354 returners were supported, and 27 of them were in a permanent post in a target subject in a state-funded secondary school by the workforce census of the following November (Table 4; Buchanan et al., 2018). The independent evaluation costed the outcome at GBP 17 800 per supported returner employed, or roughly twice that per additional returner once plausible deadweight is assumed, judged the programme unlikely to represent good value for money, and found that returners retain worse than new entrants, with odds of leaving after the first year back around 60% above average (Buchanan et al., 2018). The successor programme corrected the mechanisms the evaluation identified, tying funded places to actual vacancies and synchronising support with the hiring cycle (NCTL, 2017).

The standing English instrument, a free one-to-one adviser service for prospective returners, has never been independently evaluated. While the service was restricted to mathematics, physics and modern foreign languages, the department recorded around 128 returns over two years, implying a cost near GBP 5 400 per recorded returner with additionality unknown (UK Parliament, 2022; Walker, 2022).[^2] The service has since been extended to all subjects and phases (Department for Education, 2023). Two features of the English record frame the whole family. Interest proved inexpensive to generate while conversion was the constraint, since expressions of interest far outstripped what the pilot could convert into posts; and by mid-2026 the government was reporting more returners than at any point in ten years without any returner-specific scheme in operation, an indication that the background return flow of roughly 17 000 teachers a year dwarfs anything the programmes themselves recorded (UK Parliament, 2026; Department for Education, 2023).

In New South Wales (Australia), the education department turned its own staffing records into the family's only complete published funnel. "Teachers Re-Engage", launched in late 2023, contacted teachers who had resigned from the public system in the previous five years and packaged the re-employment friction into one contact, covering accreditation reactivation, approval to teach and the working-with-children check, with no programme-specific bonus (NSW Government, 2024). Within six months, more than 1 500 former teachers had been contacted, more than 260 had expressed interest and 145 had been placed in casual or temporary roles, about 60% of them returning from retirement (Table 4; NSW Government, 2024; NSW Department of Education, 2024). The funnel suggests, without proving, that employer-records outreach with one-stop re-licensure converts contacts at a far higher rate than England's marketing-led pilot converted registrants, although the outcomes differ in kind, casual placements against permanent posts. No cost figures or evaluation were published, the programme is absent from the following annual report, and no figures have appeared since the launch-year media release, although the returner webpage remains live (NSW Department of Education, 2025; NSW Department of Education, 2026).

Austria ran the same outreach without the funnel. Facing one of the highest vacancy rates in the OECD's most recent data collection, with unfilled positions equal to 107% of the previous year's teaching graduates, the nine regional education directorates acted as the retirement wave crested, under the federal "Klasse Job" umbrella; Vienna wrote directly to already-retired teachers, Salzburg contacted all recent retirees with transport tickets and premiums, Styria encouraged principals to approach teachers of retirement age, and Vorarlberg created a standing service point, "Arbeitsplatz Schule", that sends re-entry information out with the retirement paperwork (OECD, 2025a; APA/ORF, 2023). The Court of Audit subsequently counted 1 062 retired teachers resuming service nationwide across the six school years to 2023/24, against a workforce of roughly 123 000, with the peak in the pandemic-adjacent years and decline thereafter (Rechnungshof, 2025). The outreach cannot be credited with the returns, since the audited series begins four years before the letters. What qualitative evidence exists points away from marketing altogether, as episodic interviews with returning and non-returning retirees find the decision biographically rooted in individual, family, school and system factors, with continuing professional contact with the former school the main facilitator (Resch, 2025a; Resch, 2025b). The price the returner faces is meanwhile set by the crediting rule recorded in Table 3, under which formerly civil-servant teachers return at the starting salary.

Germany shows the pattern at federation scale. Thirteen of the sixteen Länder actively recruit pensioned teachers, typically pairing direct letters with lifted earnings limits, salary supplements where pension caps bind, and flexible hours; Hesse alone wrote to about 7 000 retired teachers in a single school year (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023; Hessisches Ministerium für Kultus, Bildung und Chancen, 2024). The visible result is just over 5 000 pensioners teaching nationwide, under 1% of the workforce, with counts collected by journalistic survey under varying definitions.[^3] No Land publishes a letters-to-returns conversion rate, and Hesse attributes its roughly 550 filled posts to a combined short-term package of retiree hiring, service extensions and part-time top-ups rather than to retirees alone (Hessisches Ministerium für Kultus, Bildung und Chancen, 2024). Germany also lacks any register of qualified people outside its schools, so the recruitment debate defaults to the one pool that is countable, a retiree stock more than half the size of the active workforce (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).[^4]

The Netherlands embedded accompaniment in regional infrastructure rather than in a single scheme. From 2019 the regional staffing programmes required each funded partnership of school boards and teacher-training institutions to run an information desk for prospective entrants and returners, and listed activation of the hidden reserve with tailored guidance among fundable goals (De Jong et al., 2022). The programme evaluation, process-based and self-reported, found tailored support to returners among the least-realised activities, and several regions reported receiving more applications from side entrants and returners than schools could place (De Jong et al., 2022). The second finding matters beyond the Netherlands, since it identifies school absorption capacity, rather than applicant interest, as a binding constraint that outreach alone cannot relieve.

Setting this family's funnels beside the take-up counts of the other families shows how thin the published evidence layer is (Table 4). Every count in the table measures a different outcome, from a permanent shortage-subject post to a casual placement to a subsidy grant, against a different denominator, so the rows do not rank the instruments. Read each against its own denominator, however, and the counts cluster well below 1% of the relevant workforce or eligible stock, with the North Carolina window the only instrument to have moved a full percentage point of its denominator. The clustering is consistent with the suggestive evidence that changes to the legal rules of the game weigh more than campaigns and incentives, although the outcome definitions differ enough that the comparison offers orientation rather than measurement.

The learning this family supports has two parts. Interest is inexpensive to generate and conversion is the constraint, a result visible in every funnel stage that has been published. No scheme anywhere has measured additionality, so every cost-per-returner figure in the family divides spending by returns that may have happened anyway; and where a funnel was published, publication stopped within a year, in New South Wales after a single media cycle, which suggests that reporting practice follows the announcement rather than the instrument.

## 3.5 Return as a defined role

The fifth family stops trying to refill the post the leaver gave up. Instead, the system defines a different job, a tutor working to a protocol and a dose, a mentor of novice teachers, a member of a standing reserve, a re-employed retiree on adjusted terms, and recruits former teachers into that role. The family rests on the destination evidence of Section 2, which describes leavers as mostly employed, mostly close to education and often still using their teaching skills, and on the working-conditions motives that make the original post unattractive at any price; a defined role at reduced intensity answers the workload motive in a way a full-time classroom return cannot. It is also the only family with randomised evidence, and the pattern of that evidence tracks role specification closely.

The strongest result concerns structured tutoring. Small-group tutoring is among the best-evidenced interventions in education, with a pooled effect of 0.288 standard deviations across randomised trials and the largest effects for teacher and paraprofessional tutors, but the original efficacy trials used graduate students as tutors, leaving open whether a realistic workforce could deliver the effect (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024; Fuchs et al., 2005). The "Number Rockets" replication in the United States made the workforce itself the question. Eighty-six tutors recruited from local pools of retired and substitute teachers delivered a scripted first-grade mathematics protocol, in small groups several times a week across a semester, to 994 at-risk students in four urban districts, and reproduced an effect of 0.34 standard deviations first obtained with graduate students (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). The trial supports the hedged claim that a tutor workforce drawn from retired and substitute teachers can deliver a specified role at scale. It does not isolate retirees as a tutor class, since the pool mixed them with substitutes and no split was published, and the dose conditions attached to the wider tutoring literature travel with the result.

The mentoring record consists of one rigorous trial and a qualified null. In Aurora (Colorado, United States), the "Retired Mentors for New Teachers" programme paid recently retired master educators with long careers in the district's high-need schools USD 41 per hour to mentor probationary teachers in disadvantaged elementary schools for two years, through weekly one-to-one sessions layered on top of the district's ordinary induction (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The randomised evaluation of 77 teachers within a single district met the What Works Clearinghouse's standards without reservations, and the Clearinghouse characterised every reviewed domain, reading, mathematics and retention, as indeterminate, with no statistically significant positive findings; the programme cost about USD 171 per student per year (What Works Clearinghouse, 2019; DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The verdict is a statement about evidential power rather than a negative result, from one small trial in one district that specified no dose. Mentoring roles for retirees are therefore best justified on instructional support and on the engagement of the retirees themselves, while any effect on whether mentees stay remains unestablished, consistent with the null result of the largest induction trial (Glazerman et al., 2010).

A parallel record concerns older volunteers more broadly. The "Experience Corps" programme places trained volunteers aged fifty and over, of whom retired educators are an undocumented subset, as structured reading tutors for early-grade students reading behind level, typically in two half-hour sessions a week after substantial initial training (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; AARP, 2025). A randomised evaluation of nearly nine hundred students found gains of 0.13 standard deviations in passage comprehension and 0.16 in teacher-rated reading skills, larger at higher session counts, and randomised studies on the volunteer side document increased physical activity and gains in cortical and hippocampal volumes against age-typical decline (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; What Works Clearinghouse, 2010; Varma et al., 2016; Carlson et al., 2015; Fried et al., 2004). The programme shows nothing specific about former teachers. What it adds to the family is a dual-benefit rationale, in which a designed role produces measured value on both sides of the exchange, and with it the possibility of engaging a second budget, since the volunteer-side findings belong to healthy-ageing policy rather than to education.

Japan is the system in which the defined role is not a programme but the labour market's standing answer. Boards of education conventionally re-employ teachers after mandatory retirement until pension age, and retirees dominate the substitute and adjunct pool; in the one prefecture studied in depth, over half of part-time adjunct hires were in their sixties and retirees made up roughly 60% of adjuncts (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024). Institutionalised re-employment absorbs retirees at a scale no campaign in the record approaches, which places Japan beside North Carolina as the only cases outside the fraction-of-one-per-cent band, in both instances because an institution changed rather than because individuals were contacted. The same study documents the model's fragility, since only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools now seek full-time re-employment and ageing adjuncts increasingly decline the most demanding replacement work, so a staffing model resting on retirees depletes on the retirees' schedule rather than the system's (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024; Nippon.com, 2026).

The newest design in the family formalises the reserve itself. In the Bahamas, the "Teacher Reservist Program" announced in mid-2026 creates a standing register of qualified nationals, retired educators, trained teachers who left for other professions and recent graduates, whom schools can call on, with a multi-agency task force fast-tracking vetting and credential assessment, against a national shortfall of about 300 teachers across the archipelago's public schools (Cooper, 2026; ZNS Bahamas, 2026). The programme, still at announcement stage as of that date, has no published reservist counts. Its interest is the design contrast, a register that former teachers join once and remain reachable through, rather than a campaign that must find them each time a shortage recurs, and it formalises an existing reliance, since retired educators were already working full-time in supply roles (ZNS Bahamas, 2026).

Defined roles also anchor the largest scheme in the record, since China's silver-age plan assigns its recruits teaching, demonstration lessons, vice-principalships and the mentoring of young local teachers (MOE et al., 2023). At the other extreme of scale, a Spanish university project, "10 mirrors", paired ten retired teachers of recognised standing with 57 teacher-education students at the University of Zaragoza for three guidance sessions on the profession, evaluated by satisfaction questionnaires only (Lizalde Gil and Cortés Pascual, 2019). Neither adds outcome evidence. Together they mark how far the defined-role logic stretches, from national redeployment to the accompaniment of students who have not yet entered the profession.

The family's learning is the most precise in the record because it is the only one produced by trials. Where the role was specified and dosed, as in structured tutoring, it was measured at scale and the effect was positive; where the role was left to the veteran educator's discretion, as in mentoring, it was measured once, in a small trial, and the answer was indeterminate. The asymmetry reflects measurement effort rather than any verdict on returning teachers' craft, and it carries an implication that reaches beyond the family, since a role that is specified can be tested while a role that is merely permitted cannot. Across the five families, the instruments that changed rules or defined roles are also the instruments that produced a record worth learning from, and it is that record, rather than the announcements surrounding it, on which the concluding section draws.
