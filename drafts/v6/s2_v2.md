# 2. Why teachers leave, where they go and what they can bring back

Policies to bring former teachers back rest on prior questions about who leaves the profession, for which motives, into which destinations and with what remaining capacity to teach. The evidence on each of these questions has strengthened over the past two decades, as administrative registers and linked employer records have begun to supplement survey self-reports, and it describes a population made up of unlike groups.

## 2.1 Routes out of the classroom

Teachers stop teaching for reasons that differ in kind, and an aggregate leaving rate registers all of them alike. A retirement at the end of a full career and a resignation after three years enter that rate as the same event, although they concern different people and answer to different instruments. Three main routes out of the classroom can be distinguished, namely job-to-job mobility, withdrawal from the labour market and retirement. The weight of each route varies across the systems for which comparable data exist. Because teaching workforces have aged, retirement carries more than half of the outflow in five of the nineteen systems, namely France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel, while on average across the nineteen the two margins are close to evenly balanced, at 51% of leavers resigning rather than retiring (OECD, 2025a). Greece is an example of the retirement-dominated pattern, because ageing among its secondary teachers is the most advanced in the European Union (European Commission, 2023). The opposite case is illustrated by Denmark, England (United Kingdom) and Estonia, where resignations account for more than 80% of departures and the outflow is therefore composed of teachers of working age (OECD, 2025a).

The distinction matters because the two margins answer to different levers. Systems in which most leavers resign face a working-age population whose departure reflects, at least in part, a judgement about the job itself, whereas systems in which most leavers retire face an actuarial margin governed by pension rules more than by workplace conditions. The resignation margin is also disproportionately young. Among the seven OECD countries that report the seniority of resigning teachers, one in three resigners on average had less than five years of experience.[^2] England shows what that margin costs a system at scale, since it recorded 40 800 full-time-equivalent leavers from state schools in 2023/24, 9.0% of its qualified workforce (Department for Education, 2025a).

Within the resignation margin, a further distinction separates teachers who take other jobs from teachers who leave paid work altogether, and the two behave differently as a source of future supply. School district records linked to tax and census data in the United States indicate that about one fifth of leavers exit the labour force entirely (Brummet et al., 2024). Panel data from Australia show a similar order of magnitude, with 28.0% of leavers not in the labour force in the first year after exit (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The composition of this margin has shifted over time, and the older evidence should not be read forward. A cohort of certified American women followed between 1973 and 1986 marks the distance travelled, since around two thirds of those who left teaching withdrew from the labour force rather than switching occupation, with the arrival of a newborn child the dominant factor (Stinebrickner, 2002). Most leavers in the contemporary data continue to work.

Departures on the resignation margin are also mostly voluntary, and they fall on identifiable parts of the workforce. Voluntary departures unrelated to retirement accounted for roughly 74% of the United States total in 2021/22, and leaving rates run markedly higher among uncertified teachers, in special education and in schools serving the largest concentrations of low-income students (Tan et al., 2026). The typology carries one limitation throughout, in that no source yet provides a single cross-country decomposition of departures into the three routes, so the shares reported here combine the system-level resignation and retirement split with national studies of labour-market status after exit, and comparisons of the labour-force-withdrawal share across countries are only indicative.

## 2.2 The motives behind departure

Among the main motives for which teachers leave, four categories stand out. Intrinsic motives concern stress and declining satisfaction with the work itself; environmental motives are rooted in workload, working time and school leadership; economic motives concern salary and pension design; and life-cycle motives are linked to family formation, early-career exploration and retirement timing. This categorisation adapts the grouping established in the meta-analytic literature, in which the correlates of turnover cluster into personal characteristics, school factors and external policy factors (Nguyen et al., 2020). Each category weighs differently across the three routes out of the classroom and across countries, and each points towards a different family of re-entry policy (Table 1).

Intrinsic motives are the best measured internationally, though mostly as intentions rather than as exits. Across OECD countries, 19% of teachers report experiencing a lot of stress at work, and stress arising from student intimidation or from maintaining classroom discipline is more closely associated with the intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures (OECD, 2025b).[^3] Intentions overstate exits by a wide margin, with around a quarter of teachers stating an intention to leave within five years against realised leaving rates between 5% and 11%, so intention data are informative about the patterning of dissatisfaction rather than about future attrition (OECD, 2020; Nguyen et al., 2025). Revealed trajectories point the same way. Job satisfaction in the Australian panel declines in the years before exit and rises afterwards, by 0.13 standard deviations at one year and 0.19 at three, while overall life satisfaction does not change significantly (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025), and the pre-exit decline suggests that dissatisfaction is measurable in advance of departure.

Environmental motives carry the strongest revealed-preference support. Fixed-effects estimates from two decades of Australian panel data identify workload, stress and work-life balance, and not salary, as the drivers of exit (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). English leavers accept an average wage cut of around 10% relative to comparable stayers in exchange for shorter hours and higher job satisfaction, which places a price on the conditions they are leaving (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). Stated reasons align with these estimates, since school leadership and workload lead the accounts of the 256 Australian leavers surveyed between 2016 and 2022, at 17.7% and 16.6% respectively (Brandenburg et al., 2024), and supportive school leadership is the key non-pay correlate in the quasi-experimental retention literature (Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026). A notable example is the case of the Netherlands, where the qualified teachers who make up the country's reserve pool were asked directly what would bring them back, and lower work pressure, more control over working hours and greater autonomy ranked ahead of salary; those inactive teachers rated the working conditions of their current jobs more favourably than serving teachers rated theirs (Somers et al., 2024a).

The economic evidence is extensive but geographically skewed. The salary gradient is measured most precisely in the United States, where each additional USD 1 000 of cost-of-living-adjusted salary is associated with a turnover reduction of 0.34 percentage points (Tan et al., 2026). Pension design pulls in the same direction at the other end of the career, since pension wealth in six United States state systems accrues in spikes in the mid-to-late 50s and then declines (Costrell and Podgursky, 2009). Causal estimates confirm that targeted payments retain teachers. The cleanest of them comes from England, where a payment of GBP 2 000 a year after tax made eligible early-career mathematics and physics teachers 23% less likely to leave in eligible years (Sims and Benhenda, 2022), and bonus and loan-forgiveness schemes in Florida and North Carolina (United States) cut attrition in shortage subjects by roughly 9% to 17% (Feng and Sass, 2018; Clotfelter et al., 2008). These estimates price retention rather than return, and no quasi-experimental study anywhere directly prices a re-entry incentive, which concerns people who have already revealed a preference against current conditions. The weight of the evidence is also American, since 72 of the roughly 86 studies in the causal base are from the United States (Fleck et al., 2025), which cautions against reading the salary margin as universal.

Life-cycle motives operate at both ends of the career. Early-career resignation partly reflects ordinary labour-market behaviour, since young teachers change jobs at rates comparable to young professionals in similar occupations (Harris and Adams, 2007), and one in five teachers under 30 intends to leave within five years (OECD, 2025b). What distinguishes teacher turnover from that of comparable professions is early, pension-driven retirement at the other end, and pension enhancements that reward delayed retirement have been found to cut retirements by 4 to 10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). Between the two ends lies the family margin, which is also the margin on which return is most likely. The segment of registrants for an English returner programme labelled career breakers returned at about double the rate of the other segments and sought part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018).

Motives for entering teaching, by contrast, appear broadly similar across countries, and what differs is what the profession offers once entered. Social utility and intrinsic value rank highest everywhere in comparative applications of the FIT-Choice scale, while perceptions of salary, status and demand carry the country differences (Watt et al., 2012). A parallel reading fits the exit evidence. Where the pay gap with alternative graduate employment is wide, as in the United States, the salary motive weighs most; where part-time work inside teaching is scarce, as in the Netherlands and in England, working time weighs most; and where retirement dominates departures, as in France, Greece and Ireland, pension rules matter more than any workplace motive. Policies to re-attract former teachers therefore require a diagnosis of the local exit mix before an instrument is chosen.

The four categories do not point to the same remedies. Life-cycle motives, together with the administrative barriers that accumulate during an absence, are answered by permission, the licence, waiting-period and service-threshold rules that decide whether a return is possible at all. Price answers the economic motives, through the pension, pay-step and subsidy settings that determine what a return produces financially, while the job answers the environmental motives, through the intensity and definition of the post a returner is offered. Reach, the fourth family, answers no motive, since it is the channel through which an offer travels rather than the offer itself. Section 3 examines the four families in that order, and the correspondence is imperfect enough to be worth stating, since no family addresses school leadership, the motive that leaver surveys most often place first.

## 2.3 Where leavers go and what they can bring back

Not only does the leaving rate vary across countries; so does the composition of destinations. District records linked to tax and census data in the United States show that more than half of leavers remain in education broadly defined, about one fifth exit the labour force entirely and fewer than one quarter take jobs outside education, with mean earnings remaining slightly below pre-exit levels even eight years later (Brummet et al., 2024). Panel data from Australia draw a comparable picture, with 22.1% of leavers in non-teaching roles within school education in the first year after exit and a further 21.8% elsewhere in education, health care and social assistance (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); in a national survey of Australian leavers, 62% reported using their teaching skills a great deal in their new work (Brandenburg et al., 2024). The same pattern appears in England, where more than half of non-retiring leavers take jobs in the education sector, at the wage cut noted above (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). The former teacher of the contemporary record is therefore typically employed and close to education.

The stock that these flows accumulate into is large, mostly of working age and formally still qualified, though not uniformly deployable. The case of Michigan (United States) sets out the qualification, since 61 252 of its 141 810 certificate-holders were not teaching in a public school in 2017/18 (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Most of that reserve remains young enough to teach, at 93% under the age of 60. Re-certification would be required of roughly half of it before a return, since certificates had expired for between 44% and 49% of those out of service against 6% of active teachers, though a lapsed certificate can be restored. A comparable stock is recorded in England, at around 260 000 qualified teachers of working age out of service in 2018 (Worth, 2023). Stocks of this kind already generate return flows of consequence, since re-entrants have made up between 37% and 49% of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), even though only a minority of leavers, between one fifth and one half depending on the cohort, ever return (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022).

What returners could contribute rests, in the first instance, on experience. The best causal footing for the claim that exposure to experienced educators transfers skill comes from peer effects among serving teachers, where a one standard deviation increase in the quality of a teacher's peers raises her students' test-score growth by 0.02 standard deviations in reading and 0.04 in mathematics, with the strongest spillovers accruing to less experienced colleagues (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009). Experience is also what returns when rules change. North Carolina (United States) is the largest documented instance, where the roughly 3 500 teachers who came back over a decade after the pension penalty was suspended averaged 31 years of experience and went disproportionately to high-need schools (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

A second line of evidence concerns structured tutoring, the designed role in which former and older educators have been tested most rigorously. Across randomised trials of tutoring from pre-school to secondary school, the pooled effect is 0.288 standard deviations, with the largest effects for teacher and paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades and at doses of three or more days per week (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024). The Number Rockets replication carried that finding to scale, since 86 tutors drawn from retired and substitute teacher pools delivered a first-grade mathematics protocol to 994 students across 76 schools and reproduced an effect of 0.34 standard deviations first obtained with graduate students (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). Dose recurs as a condition of effect across this literature, and recruitment at token hours should not be expected to reproduce these results.

The mentoring record is thinner, and the asymmetry between the two roles is one of measurement effort rather than a verdict on returning teachers' craft. The one randomised trial of retired educators as mentors to new teachers found indeterminate effects in all three reviewed domains of reading, mathematics and retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; What Works Clearinghouse, 2019), and the largest trial of comprehensive induction found no effect on the retention of the teachers mentored (Glazerman et al., 2010). The tutoring role was specified, dosed and tested at scale; the mentoring role was tested once, without a specified dose. More generally, no study in the international record links an individual returner to the outcomes of the students or colleagues they work with, and the authors of the largest re-entry episode note that their data did not permit the attempt (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). What the record does establish is narrower, namely that a former teacher holds a valid or restorable licence, can be deployed without consuming a place in initial teacher education, and brings institutional familiarity while standing outside the school's evaluation hierarchy (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017).

The destination and contribution evidence therefore describes a population that is mostly employed, mostly close to education and formally qualified, with tested usefulness in at least one specified role. Whether any part of it returns depends on conditions that governments set, namely the rules that permit a return, the pension and pay settings that price it, the shape of the post offered and the channels through which the offer reaches a former teacher. Across OECD countries these instruments have been deployed in varying combinations over the past two decades, with results that include audited take-up at scale and independently evaluated failure.
