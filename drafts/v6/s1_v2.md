# 1. Introduction

Replacing the teachers who leave is a permanent task of school systems rather than an accident of one labour market or one cohort. Across the OECD education systems with comparable data, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23, and national rates run from under 3% to above 10%.[^1] Measured against other large graduate occupations, a rate of that order is ordinary. The case of the United States illustrates this, where the share of teachers leaving the occupation each year has been broadly stable for four decades and sits close to the rates recorded for nurses and for accountants (Harris and Adams, 2007; Aldeman and Yi, 2025). Attrition is therefore best treated as a permanent parameter of workforce planning.

An aggregate leaving rate, however, records departures of very different kinds as though they were the same event. Teachers who stop teaching do not all leave for the same reasons, and they do not all go to the same places. Three main routes out of the classroom can be distinguished, namely job-to-job mobility, withdrawal from the labour market and retirement. The weight of each route varies across the systems examined here. Where the teaching workforce has aged, retirement absorbs the larger part of the outflow, while across the nineteen systems with comparable data the two margins are close to evenly balanced, with 51% of leavers resigning rather than retiring (OECD, 2025a). Greece is an example of the first pattern, because ageing among its secondary teachers is the most advanced in the European Union and retirements there exceed half of all departures (European Commission, 2023; OECD, 2025a). The opposite case is illustrated by Denmark, England (United Kingdom) and Estonia, where more than four departures in five are resignations and the population leaving is consequently of working age (OECD, 2025a).

Which route dominates would matter less if recruitment kept pace with departures. Teacher shortages have become a pressing policy concern in most OECD countries (OECD, 2025a), and more than half of the additional primary and secondary teachers the world will need by 2030 are required to replace teachers who leave rather than to enlarge the workforce (UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024). The workforce that would do the replacing is itself ageing, which raises replacement demand and enlarges the stock of recent retirees at the same time. Ageing is most advanced in Greece and in Portugal among European Union systems, and it worsens from primary to upper secondary education (European Commission, 2023). The case of Germany gives the order of magnitude of what an ageing workforce leaves behind, where 457 000 retired or pensioned teachers were counted in 2021 against a serving workforce of roughly 800 000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Replacement demand of that kind directs attention to any source of qualified supply that does not have to be trained from the beginning.

One such source is produced by attrition itself. Every departure short of a final retirement adds a person who holds a teaching qualification and is not using it, and such people accumulate into what the literature calls a reserve pool. The pool is mostly of working age and formally still qualified. The case of Michigan (United States) illustrates both its size and its condition, because certification records in that jurisdiction can be linked to employment records, and 43% of the 141 810 teachers holding certificates were not teaching in a public school in 2017/18 (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Movement out of pools of this kind is already a supply channel of consequence. The clearest count is the English one, where roughly 17 000 teachers return to state schools each year, about one third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023). Movement on that scale is consistent with teaching functioning as a profession that people leave and rejoin over a working life, and most education systems already maintain pathways for teachers to return if they wish (OECD, 2024; OECD, 2025a).

Governments have been acting on this reserve for more than a quarter of a century, and the advice is older than most of the instruments. As early as 2005, the OECD recommended that education systems maintain contact with teachers who had left, keep them informed of vacancies and educational developments, and provide them with targeted retraining (OECD, 2005). The statutory record begins earlier still and continues to the present. A return-to-work window was opened to retired teachers in North Carolina (United States) in 1999 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024); a subsidy for schools recruiting returners was regulated in the Netherlands in 2017 (Ministerie van OCW, 2017); and fixed-term contracts for teachers who had already left were created in Portugal in 2024 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024). Most of the instruments now in force were adopted or widened under post-pandemic staffing pressure, and few of them have been evaluated.

The volume of that activity has outrun the analysis of it. This paper compiles the international record of policies to re-attract former teachers and analyses it alongside the evidence on why teachers leave, where they go and what they can bring back. The instruments are grouped into four families that follow the sequence in which a former teacher meets them. Permission covers the licence, waiting-period and eligibility rules that decide whether a return is possible at all. Price covers the pension, pay-step and subsidy settings that decide what a return produces financially, and the two families together settle what a system's own rules charge a returner, recorded in Section 3 as four regulatory dials of pay and pension, waits and thresholds, caps on quantity and licence status. The job covers the intensity and definition of the post that is offered, and reach covers the contact and accompaniment through which former teachers learn that any of this exists. The families are mutually exclusive, and each measure is assigned to the family whose question it answers, so that instruments adopted in unlike systems can be compared by the component of the return decision they alter. The population concerned justifies attention beyond the vacancies it might fill, since experienced returners carry teaching capacity that education systems themselves financed, of a kind shown to spill over to colleagues and to their students (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009).

The material available for this exercise is administrative rather than experimental, consisting mainly of statutes and take-up counts, with a small number of independent audits and randomised trials, so the analysis extends only as far as such material allows and stops short of estimating what any instrument added. Section 2 examines who leaves the profession, for which motives, into which destinations and with what remaining capacity to teach; Section 3 analyses the four policy families and the take-up and evaluation record each has produced; and Section 4 sets out the conclusions and recommendations that this record supports.

## Notes

[^1]: Across the 19 OECD education systems with comparable data, the leaving rate for fully qualified teachers remained below 3% in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel and exceeded 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania in 2022/23 (OECD, 2025a, Table D8.4). In England, the Department for Education counted 40 800 full-time-equivalent leavers from state schools in 2023/24, 9.0% of the qualified workforce (Department for Education, 2025a). In the United States, the leaving rate stood at 8% of public school teachers in 2021, unchanged since 2012 (NCES, 2023), and pooled Current Population Survey estimates for 2015-2024 average 7.6% a year (Aldeman and Yi, 2025). In Australia, household panel data record a leaving rate of 11.6% in 2022 (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). Of particular interest is the case of Austria, where the rate nearly doubled within a decade, from 3.2% in 2014/15 to 6.2% in 2022/23 (OECD, 2025a). At the global level the movement has been upward, with attrition among primary school teachers worldwide rising from 4.62% in 2015 to 9.06% in 2022 (UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024).
