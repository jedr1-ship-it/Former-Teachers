## 1. Register: academic OECD, without exception
- Impersonal institutional subjects; hedged claims ("the evidence suggests", "appears to",
  "there is suggestive evidence that"); flat declarative verbs; scope adverbials
  ("Across OECD countries...").
- FORBIDDEN: narrative dramatisation; extended metaphor as argument (deal/price-as-imagery/
  sell/bill/postage/stock); aphorisms and punch-lines; one-line rhetorical paragraphs;
  anthropomorphism; body-prose imperatives; second-person-adjacent phrasing; rhetorical
  questions as transitions; emotive adjectives.
- "Price of return" MAY survive only as a technical term defined once (the sum of
  salary-step placement, pension-salary concurrency, waiting periods and licensing costs)
  and used with that referent only.
- Recommendations: actor + can/should modality (~5:1), "could benefit from" where evidence
  is thin. No bare imperatives outside bold recommendation leads if the format uses them.

## 2. The lead author's tone templates (verbatim; these ARE the target register)
- Comparisons (C13): "Entre las múltiples campañas implementadas para reatraer profesores,
  tanto las que proveen incentivos como las que facilitan certificación, hay evidencia
  sugestiva de que pesan menos que los cambios legales en las reglas de juego, que parecen
  ser más exitosos en reatraer profesores." → English equivalent: "Among the many
  campaigns implemented to attract teachers back, both those providing incentives and
  those easing certification, there is suggestive evidence that they weigh less than
  changes to the legal rules of the game, which appear more successful in re-attracting
  teachers." NEVER raw number-vs-number rhetoric.
- Openings (C17): general statement of the phenomenon with magnitudes; country rates with
  institute/year in an ENDNOTE ("in England, [institute] put it at X% for 2024; in
  Austria..."); a case of particular interest flagged ("Of particular interest is the case
  of X, where..."); global framing (UNESCO projection), TALIS on young teachers, new
  pathways; shortage as context only, never the hook.
- Paragraph blocks (C18): "Not only does the leaving rate change across countries; so does
  the composition of where they go. In the United States, for example, several studies
  show that... In other OECD countries... This phenomenon is particularly present in X
  and Y." General idea and its learning first, then landed with cases.
- Motives (C11): "Among the main motives for which teachers leave, X, Y and Z stand out" —
  typology form, never sensational headline.

## 2bis. TONE GRAMMAR FROM REAL OECD PROSE (verbatim-verified; EPO 2024 ch.2 "Addressing
teacher shortages" and OECD Education Policy Perspectives No. 122; full extracts at
research/epo2024.pages.txt and research/epp122_digital.pages.txt — WRITERS MUST READ BOTH)
- SECTION OPENINGS: present-tense thesis about the policy phenomenon with a system-level
  subject; sentence two attaches data or mechanism. Model: "Encouraging re-entry of
  teachers who have transitioned to other careers aligns with a broader vision of teaching
  as a dynamic profession. While education systems should aim to reduce attrition..., not
  all types of departures should be viewed as problematic." Never an anecdote, never a question.
- FINDINGS: "evidence" as grammatical agent; verbs suggests/indicates/points to; scope
  adverbials fronted ("On average across OECD countries, 43% of teachers..."); strong
  claims carry a concessive frame ("While these initiatives seem promising..., evidence on
  the long-term effectiveness... is still inconclusive"); null results stated flatly.
- COUNTRY EXAMPLES: "In [Country], [instrument] + purpose infinitive" + precise magnitudes
  + outcome/evaluation honesty clause + citation. Models: "In Portugal, 'exceptional and
  temporary' measures are being introduced to encourage the return of teachers who have
  left the profession."; "In Ireland, a pilot teacher-sharing scheme was introduced in 2023
  to offer... While there is no evidence yet on the scheme's effectiveness, insights gained
  from this pilot will be useful..."; chaining: "Similarly, Germany has also adopted...";
  flagged interest: "In Singapore, a notable example of how supporting career progression
  improves retention, ...". Native-language programme names in quotes.
- PARAGRAPH TYPES (mix them; typical 3-6 sentences, 60-130 words): thesis paragraphs
  (open a subsection); development/caveat paragraphs attached by However/While/Although/
  Despite/At the same time and often closing on an obligation ("Policymakers must
  therefore balance..."); case paragraphs (instrument → detail with numbers → outcome);
  two-sentence bridge paragraphs restating the pivot ("Addressing teacher shortages
  requires more than bringing new teachers into the profession. It also involves...").
- RECOMMENDATIONS: generic actor (policymakers, education systems); modality ladder
  could benefit from → should (therefore) → it is crucial that → must (reserved for
  balance warnings); drawn as inferences at paragraph ends, never numbered directives.
- DISPLAYS: cited parenthetically mid-sentence "(Table 3)"; rarely "as Figure 2.9 shows";
  table titles may state the finding as a sentence; numbered Note:/Source: lines.
- RHYTHM: dominant sentence 25-40 words with one subordinate clause and paired qualifiers;
  short sentences (<12 words) roughly one per page, doing verdict/pivot work ("That said,
  caution is needed."); numbers to the decimal; thin-space thousands ("GBP 30 000").

## 3. Paragraph doctrine (corrected by the lead author)
The unit of the message is the ARGUMENTATIVE BLOCK, not the paragraph. Within a block:
a strong lead paragraph plants the general idea and its learning; development paragraphs
extend it (secondary ideas, qualifications, contrasts, cases) WITHOUT re-announcing it.
Attack varies: some paragraphs open on the idea; some continue a running argument
("A second mechanism operates through..."; "This pattern is not uniform, however");
some open directly on a case because the lead paragraph did the work. A single hammered
paragraph template is itself an AI tell and is FORBIDDEN. Non-negotiable: no orphan
paragraphs — each serves a known idea and adds something; each block arrives somewhere.

## 4. Content rules
- TYPOLOGY BEFORE GENERALISATION: former teachers are three populations (switchers to
  other jobs; exits from the labour market; retirements) with different motives by type.
  No claim about "former teachers" as a bloc that the evidence only supports for a subset.
- Examples are POSTULATED as examples with country + brief system context: "A notable
  example is the case of Austria, in which..."; "Others are analysed below." Never
  "In Austria..." cold.
- One idea per sentence. If "what is the idea here?" has no one-line answer, rewrite.
- Country-neutral language: governments, systems, public authorities — never "the state"
  (reads as US states).
- The text NEVER narrates itself ("the paper says so plainly", "this paper now does X"):
  the text does, it does not announce. No self-referential authorial staging anywhere.
- Facts only from the base (fix files and ch5_synthesis supersede); scale figures always
  with denominator and outcome definition or not at all; never force a number.
- Lexicon: "Most OECD countries face serious teacher shortages", never "are short of
  teachers"; no "honestly counted"; no imagery (keys, register rows, doors, knocking).
- British spelling; no rhetorical colons; no em dashes in body prose; no triads;
  endnotes (never footnotes, never for sources); affirmative grammar in headings/ledes.

## 6. OECD Policy Papers Standard mould (from the uploaded standard)
Element order: cover (series, title, number) → copyright/disclaimers page (incl. CC BY 4.0
attribution + territorial disclaimers, flagged for team confirmation) → Abstract (<150
words; optionally Keywords + JEL after) → Acknowledgements → Table of contents →
Abbreviations and acronyms (alphabetical) → Executive summary (≤1,000 words; key findings,
conclusions, recommendations; NO figures/tables/citations/notes) → numbered sections 1, 2,
3... with 1.1-style subheadings → Conclusions and/or recommendations under its own
numbered section heading → References (full details, all cited) → endnotes if any.
Figures/tables/boxes numbered continuously from 1 across the whole document (Table 1,
Table 2... NEVER by section). Main text < 30 pages. Non-technical language for
policymakers, practitioners, journalists. Annexes only if essential, never as overflow.

## 9. SECTION 3 STRUCTURE (added after the lead author judged the section "a soup")
The section must be visibly structured. Fixed skeleton, no deviation:

**Per family subsection (3.1 to 3.5):**
1. LEAD (two paragraphs, no more): what governments seek with this family; what component of
   the return decision it changes; which systems have used it. No cases yet.
2. CASES, each as its own paragraph block opened by a bold run-in country name
   ("**North Carolina.**" / "**Portugal.**"), ordered from best-documented to least. Each case
   block follows the same internal order, in flowing prose, never as labels or bullets:
   (a) one sentence of system context (why this system did this, when);
   (b) the mechanism, precisely, with its legal instrument;
   (c) what was counted, with its denominator, or "no take-up has been published";
   (d) what the evidence supports, hedged, including any evaluation, audit or study with its
       method; and what it cannot support.
   Length per case: 120-220 words. The best-documented two or three cases per family may run
   longer; marginal cases may be one or two sentences inside a grouping paragraph
   ("Comparable provisions operate in X, Y and Z, none of which publishes take-up.").
3. CLOSING PARAGRAPH: what the family as a whole teaches, formulated academically, and the
   hand-off to the next family. One paragraph.

**Rules that kill the soup:**
- No case appears outside its family. No family's argument is made twice.
- Every case block answers the same four questions in the same order, so a policymaker can
  compare across cases by position in the paragraph.
- Numbers in prose only when they carry the case's point; parameters go to Table 3, counts to
  Table 4; each display cited parenthetically once.
- Cross-references to other families use the family letter ("as in Family A").
- Tables are SIMPLE by order of the lead author: motives table carries motives only; every
  other table carries at most three or four short columns, cells of a few words. Everything
  else lives in the prose.


## 10. CLASSIFICATION OF POLICIES (replaces the rejected five families A-E)
Primary axis: the sequence of the return decision. Four families, mutually exclusive:

1. PERMISSION — whether a former teacher may return at all: licence validity, lapse and
   refresh requirements, waiting periods, service thresholds, subject or geography
   restrictions, and day or earnings caps that rule out a full return.
   Owner: professional regulators and pension legislation. Motive addressed: life-cycle and
   administrative barriers.
2. PRICE — what returning produces financially: pension-salary concurrency and abatement,
   the salary step on re-entry (seniority crediting), bonuses, subsidies and allowances,
   paid to the teacher or to the school. Owner: pension, civil-service and finance
   authorities. Motive: economic.
3. THE JOB — the shape of what is offered: full-time against part-time, job-share, supply
   and reserve pools, and defined roles such as tutoring or mentoring. Owner: employers and
   schools. Motive: environmental (workload, hours, control of time), the motive the
   evidence ranks highest.
4. REACH — how former teachers learn of the offer and are helped through it: contact from
   employer records, return advisers, refresher training, application support. Owner:
   education ministries and their agencies. Motive: none; it is the channel, which is part
   of why its measured yield is weak.

Secondary attributes, carried as COLUMNS in the navigation table, never as families:
beneficiary (the returning teacher, the school, students, new teachers) and institutional
owner of the lever. The closing of Section 3 ranks the families by documented scale of
return; the subsections themselves run in decision order (1 to 4).
Cases are assigned to the family whose question they answer. A case touching two families
(a statute that both permits and prices) is discussed in full where its principal effect
lies and cross-referenced once from the other.
