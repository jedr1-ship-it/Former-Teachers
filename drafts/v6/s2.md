# 2. Why teachers leave, where they go and what they can bring back

Policies to bring former teachers back into classrooms rest on prior empirical questions about who leaves the profession, for which motives, into which destinations and with what remaining capacity to teach. The evidence on each of these questions has strengthened considerably over the past two decades, as administrative registers and linked employer records have begun to supplement survey self-reports, and it does not describe a homogeneous population.

## 2.1 Three populations of leavers

Teachers who leave the profession form three distinct populations: those who move to other jobs, those who withdraw from the labour market and those who retire. Across the 19 OECD education systems with comparable data, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23, and on average 51% of these leavers resigned rather than retired (OECD, 2025a).[^1] The balance between the two margins differs widely across systems. Resignations accounted for over 80% of departures in Denmark, England (United Kingdom) and Estonia, while retirements exceeded half of all departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a). England, where 40 800 full-time-equivalent teachers, 9.0% of the qualified workforce, left state schools in 2023/24, is the clearest case of a resignation-dominated system (Department for Education, 2025a).

The split matters for policy because the two margins respond to different levers. Systems in which most leavers resign face a working-age population whose departure reflects, at least in part, a judgement about the job itself; systems in which most leavers retire face an actuarial margin governed by pension rules more than by workplace motives. The resignation margin is also disproportionately young. Among the seven OECD countries reporting the seniority of resigning teachers, between 16% and 68% had less than five years of experience, one in three on average (OECD, 2025a).[^2]

Within the resignation margin, a further distinction separates teachers who take other jobs from teachers who leave paid work altogether. In the United States, school district records linked to tax and census data indicate that about one fifth of leavers exit the labour force entirely (Brummet et al., 2024). Australian panel data show a similar order of magnitude, with 28.0% of leavers not in the labour force in the first year after exit and a further 13.9% retired (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The composition has shifted over time. In a cohort of certified American women followed between 1973 and 1986, around two thirds of those who left teaching withdrew from the labour force entirely, with the arrival of a newborn child the dominant factor, ahead of marriage or pay (Stinebrickner, 2002); in the contemporary data, most leavers keep working.

Most departures on the resignation margin also appear voluntary. In the United States, roughly 74% of all departures in 2021/22 were voluntary and unrelated to retirement, and leaving rates are markedly higher among uncertified teachers, in special education and in schools serving the largest concentrations of low-income students (Tan et al., 2026). No source yet provides a single cross-country decomposition of departures into the three populations; the shares reported here combine the system-level resignation and retirement split with national studies of labour-market status after exit, so comparisons of the labour-force-exit share across countries are only indicative.

## 2.2 The motives behind departure

Among the main motives for which teachers leave, four categories stand out: intrinsic motives such as stress and declining satisfaction with the work itself; environmental motives rooted in workload, working hours and school leadership; economic motives concerning salary and pension design; and life-cycle motives linked to family formation, early-career exploration and retirement timing. This categorisation adapts the grouping established in the meta-analytic literature, in which the correlates of turnover cluster into personal characteristics, school factors and external policy factors (Nguyen et al., 2020). Each category weighs differently across the three exit populations and across countries, and each points towards a different family of re-entry policies (Table 1).

Intrinsic motives are the best measured internationally, though mostly as intentions. Across OECD countries, 19% of teachers report experiencing a lot of stress at work, and stress arising from student intimidation or from maintaining classroom discipline is more closely associated with the intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures (OECD, 2025b).[^3] Intentions overstate exits by a wide margin, with around 25% of teachers stating an intention to leave within five years against realised leaving rates of 5% to 11%, so intention data are informative about the patterning of dissatisfaction rather than about future attrition (OECD, 2020; Nguyen et al., 2025). Revealed trajectories point the same way. In the Australian panel, job satisfaction declines in the years before exit and rises afterwards, by 0.13 standard deviations at one year and 0.19 at three, while overall life satisfaction does not change significantly (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); the pre-exit decline suggests that dissatisfaction is measurable in advance of departure.

Environmental motives carry the strongest revealed-preference support. Fixed-effects estimates from two decades of Australian panel data identify workload, stress and work-life balance, and not salary, as the drivers of exit (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025), and English leavers accept an average wage cut of around 10% relative to comparable stayers in exchange for shorter hours and higher job satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). Stated reasons align with these estimates, with school leadership issues (17.7%) and workload (16.6%) leading the accounts of 256 Australian leavers surveyed between 2016 and 2022 (Brandenburg et al., 2024), and supportive school leadership is the key non-pay correlate in the quasi-experimental retention literature (Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026). A notable example is the case of the Netherlands, where the qualified teachers who constitute the country's hidden reserve were asked directly what would bring them back. Lower work pressure, more control over working hours and greater autonomy ranked ahead of salary, and inactive teachers rated several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rated theirs (Somers et al., 2024a).

The economic evidence is substantial but geographically skewed. In the United States, each additional USD 1 000 of cost-of-living-adjusted salary is associated with a turnover reduction of 0.34 percentage points (Tan et al., 2026), and 33% of Michigan certificate-holders outside the classroom placed higher salary among their top three reasons for not teaching, in a survey whose 30% response rate its authors describe as unrepresentative (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Causal estimates indicate that targeted payments do retain teachers. In England, a payment of GBP 2 000 a year after tax made eligible mathematics and physics teachers 23% less likely to leave (Sims and Benhenda, 2022), and bonus and loan-forgiveness schemes in Florida and North Carolina cut attrition in shortage subjects by roughly 9% to 17% (Feng and Sass, 2018; Clotfelter et al., 2008). These estimates price retention, however, and no quasi-experimental study anywhere directly prices re-entry incentives, which concern people who have already revealed a preference against current conditions. The weight of the evidence is also American, since 72 of the roughly 86 studies in the causal base are from the United States (Fleck et al., 2025), which cautions against reading the salary margin as universal.

Life-cycle motives operate at both ends of the career. Early-career resignation partly reflects ordinary labour-market behaviour, as young teachers change jobs at rates comparable to young professionals in similar occupations (Harris and Adams, 2007), and one in five teachers under 30 intends to leave within five years, rising to around half in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania (OECD, 2025b). At the other end, what distinguishes teacher turnover from that of comparable professions is early, pension-driven retirement. Pension wealth in six United States state systems accrues in spikes in the mid-to-late 50s and then declines, encouraging exit at ages young for professionals (Costrell and Podgursky, 2009; Harris and Adams, 2007), while pension enhancements that reward delayed retirement have been found to cut retirements by 4 to 10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). Between the two ends lies the family margin. The segment of registrants for an English returner programme labelled career breakers returned at about double the rate of the other segments and sought part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018).

Entry motives, by contrast, appear broadly similar across countries; what differs is what the profession offers. In comparative applications of the FIT-Choice scale, social utility and intrinsic value rank highest everywhere, while perceptions of salary, status and demand carry the country differences (Watt et al., 2012). A parallel reading fits the exit evidence. Where the pay gap with alternative graduate employment is wide, as in the United States, the salary motive weighs most; where part-time work inside teaching is scarce, as in the Netherlands and England, hours and working conditions weigh most; and where retirement dominates departures, as in France, Greece and Ireland, pension rules matter more than any workplace motive. Policies to re-attract former teachers therefore require a diagnosis of the local exit mix before an instrument is chosen.

## 2.3 Where leavers go and what they can bring back

Not only does the leaving rate vary across countries; so does the composition of destinations. In the United States, district records linked to tax and census data show that more than half of leavers remain in education broadly defined, about one fifth exit the labour force entirely and fewer than one quarter take jobs outside education, while mean earnings remain slightly below pre-exit levels even eight years later (Brummet et al., 2024). Australian panel data draw a comparable picture, with 22.1% of leavers in non-teaching roles within school education and a further 21.8% elsewhere in education, health care and social assistance in the first year after exit (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); in a national survey of Australian leavers, 62% reported using their teaching skills a great deal in their new work (Brandenburg et al., 2024). In England, more than half of non-retiring leavers take jobs in the education sector, at the wage cut of around 10% noted above (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015).

The stock that these flows accumulate into is large, mostly of working age and formally still qualified. In Michigan, 61 252 of 141 810 certified teachers, 43%, were not teaching in a public school in 2017/18; 93% of them were under 60, and although 44% to 49% held expired certificates, against 6% of active teachers, an expired certificate can be restored (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). England counted around 260 000 qualified teachers of working age out of service in 2018, alongside 106 000 who never entered service (Worth, 2023). These stocks already generate substantial return flows. Roughly 17 000 teachers return to English state schools each year, about one third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023), and re-entrants have made up between 37% and 49% of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), although only a minority of leavers, between one fifth and one half depending on the cohort, ever return (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022).

What returners could contribute rests, in the first instance, on experience. The best causal footing for the claim that exposure to experienced educators transfers skill comes from peer effects among serving teachers. A one standard deviation increase in the quality of a teacher's peers raises her students' test-score growth by 0.02 standard deviations in reading and 0.04 in mathematics, about one third of the own-teacher effect, with the strongest spillovers accruing to less experienced colleagues (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009). Experience is also what returns when rules change. When North Carolina allowed retirees to teach full-time without pension penalty, the roughly 3 500 teachers who returned over the decade, 2.1% of the sampled workforce, averaged 31 years of experience and went disproportionately to high-need schools (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

A second line of evidence concerns structured tutoring, the designed role in which older and former educators have been tested most rigorously. Across randomised trials of tutoring from pre-school to secondary school, the pooled effect is 0.288 standard deviations, with the largest effects for teacher and paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades and at doses of three or more days per week (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024). In the Number Rockets replication, 86 tutors drawn from retired and substitute teacher pools delivered a first-grade mathematics protocol to 994 students across 76 schools in four urban districts, reproducing at scale an effect of 0.34 standard deviations first obtained with graduate students (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). These trials do not isolate former teachers as a tutor class; the Number Rockets pool mixed retirees with substitutes, and the meta-analytic tutor categories are broader still, so the inference to returners specifically remains indirect. Dose also recurs as a condition of effect across this literature, and recruitment at token hours should not be expected to reproduce these results.

The mentoring record is thinner. The one randomised trial of retired educators as mentors to new teachers, rated by the What Works Clearinghouse as meeting standards without reservations, found indeterminate effects in all three reviewed domains of reading, mathematics and retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; What Works Clearinghouse, 2019), and the largest trial of comprehensive induction found no effect on the retention of the teachers mentored (Glazerman et al., 2010). The asymmetry between the tutoring and mentoring records is one of measurement effort rather than a verdict on returning teachers' craft. The tutoring role was specified, dosed and tested at scale; the mentoring role was tested once, without a specified dose. More generally, no study in the international record links an individual returner to the outcomes of the students or colleagues they work with, and the authors of the largest re-entry episode note that their data did not permit the attempt (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). What the record does establish is narrower. A former teacher holds a valid or restorable licence, can be deployed without consuming a place in initial teacher education, and brings institutional familiarity while standing outside the school's evaluation hierarchy (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). Settling the wider question would require linking returner records to the classes and colleagues they join, and trials of designed roles at specified doses.

The destination and contribution evidence therefore describes a population that is mostly employed, mostly close to education and formally qualified, with tested usefulness in at least one specified role. Whether any part of it returns depends on conditions that governments set, namely the pension and employment rules, licensing requirements, financial incentives, outreach efforts and role designs that determine what a return costs the returner and what it offers. Across OECD countries these instruments have been deployed in varying combinations over the past two decades, with results that range from audited take-up at scale to independently evaluated failure.
