## (a) TYPOLOGY OF LEAVING — three exit types

The base supports the Chapter-1 population definition (TEAM_INPUT §3): former teachers are (i) job switchers, (ii) exits from the labour market, (iii) retirees. What the base holds on shares and profiles, per source:

**The resignation/retirement split, cross-country (the system-level anchor).**
- Across 19 OECD systems, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23; on average 51% of leavers resigned rather than retired. Over 80% resigned in Denmark, England (UK) and Estonia; retirements exceed 50% of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland, Israel (and Argentina, primary). By level: pre-primary 7.3%, primary 5.8%, secondary 5.9%. — OECD (2025a), Chapter D8, pp. 484, 493-495, Table D8.4 (pinpoints per fix_oecd_primary A1.2).
- Seniority of resigners: among seven OECD countries with data, 16-68% of resigning teachers had under five years' experience (Austria ≈58%, Israel ≈68%, Slovak Republic 40%, Poland 32%, Estonia 30%, Sweden 23%, Denmark 16%); on average one in three (34%). — OECD (2025a), p. 495.
- Policy reading (ch5_synthesis Lesson 2): resignation-heavy systems face working-age resigners; retirement-heavy systems face an actuarial, not motivational, margin.

**Exits from the labour market.**
- Historic US benchmark: ~67% of exiting certified female teachers (NLS-72 cohort, 1973-1986 follow-ups) left the labour force entirely rather than switching occupations; a newborn child is the dominant exit factor, ahead of marriage or pay. — Stinebrickner (2002). Vintage caveat binding: 1970s-80s cohort, treat as historical.
- Modern US: about a fifth of leavers exit the labour force entirely (linked district-IRS/Census records, 2003/04-2014/15). — Brummet et al. (2024).
- Australia: within the first year after exit, 28.0% not in the labour force, 13.9% retired, 2.9% unemployed (flagged non-reliable, n<30). — Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025), Table 2, p. 7 (verified full text per fix_hilda_fleck).

**Switchers and the voluntary/forced margin.**
- US 2021-22: total turnover 15.1% (8.0% movers between schools, 7.1% leavers); ~74% of departures voluntary and non-retirement; NCES's own rounded First Look figures are 84/8/8, "not statistically different" from 2012-13 — cite LPI for the decimal split, NCES for the official figures. — Tan et al. (2026); NCES (2023).
- England: 40,800 FTE left in 2023/24 (9.0% of qualified teachers); in the year to November 2023, a record-high 8.8% left for reasons other than retirement and a record-low 0.7% retired — England is the type case of a resignation-dominated system. — Department for Education (2025a); Schools Week reporting of DfE data [KEY MISSING — Schools Week (2024) not in references.md; the EAG D8 80%-resigned figure for England (OECD, 2025a) covers the same point with a listed key].
- Turnover concentrates, not spreads: US uncertified teachers 20.1% vs 14.7% certified; special education/ESL 16.4-19.0%; city 17.7% and charter 17.8%; schools with the largest concentrations of low-income students and students of colour 35-37% higher; Black teachers 18.1% vs 14.7% White. — Tan et al. (2026).

**Who the out-of-service stock are (register profiles).**
- Michigan: of 141,810 certified teachers, 61,252 (43%) not teaching in a public school in 2017/18; of these, 70% had not taught at any point in the previous five years, 44-49% held expired certificates (vs 6% of active teachers), 93% under 60, 76% women, 57% elementary-certified. — Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan (2021), pp. 4-5, Table 1. (Never cite "2020 Preliminary Findings" — phantom source, ch5_synthesis B1.)
- England: 260,000 qualified teachers of working age out of service plus 106,000 never in service (2018) — the served/never-served cut held as separate series. — Worth (2023), citing DfE.
- Returner selection: returners are disproportionately younger, more experienced, better paid pre-exit and female, but less likely to return with young children at home (NLSY79); the NLSY97 replication finds only about one fifth of 2000-2019 leavers ever returned (older cohorts: one quarter to one half) and no remaining gender/child gradients. — Grissom and Reininger (2012); Moyer (2022).
- NFER returner typology (England pilot registrants): idealists 35%, pragmatists 24%, opportunists 16%, movers 13%, career breakers 11% — career breakers returned at about double the rate of the other types and want part-time work schools rarely advertise. — Buchanan et al. (2018); Worth and Sharp (2018).

**THIN:** no source gives a single cross-country decomposition of leavers into the three types; the typology must be assembled from the D8 resignation/retirement split plus the US/Australian labour-force-exit shares. Cuervo's unemployment cell is n<30; Stinebrickner is a half-century-old cohort.

## (b) MOTIVES BY CATEGORY

**Taxonomy anchors (both in the base and in references.md).** Nguyen et al. (2020): meta-analysis of 120 US studies; correlates cluster into personal characteristics, school factors (leadership, working conditions) and external/policy factors (accountability, pay structures) — the framing citation. Watt et al. (2012): FIT-Choice in Australia, the US, Germany, Norway — motivations for entering are "more similar than different" (social utility and intrinsic value rank highest everywhere), while perceptions of the profession (salary, status, demand) carry the country differences. Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026) supply the interdisciplinary model of the decision (not) to teach, tested against survey experiments on job preferences (identity per TEAM_INPUT §1).

### Intrinsic — burnout, stress, perceptions of the profession
- 18% of lower-secondary teachers across the OECD report "a lot" of stress at work; stressed teachers are about twice as likely to say they will leave within five years; ~25% of teachers want to leave within five years (intentions, not exits). — OECD (2020), TALIS 2018 Vol. II.
- TALIS 2024 update: 19% report high stress (over 30% in Alberta, Australia, Bahrain, Costa Rica, Malta, New Zealand; under 5% in Albania, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Viet Nam); stress from student intimidation/verbal abuse or maintaining discipline doubles leave-intention and is more closely associated with intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures; ~90% report overall job satisfaction but only about 2 in 5 are satisfied with salary and fewer than 30% feel their views are valued by policymakers; satisfied teachers are ~5 times less likely to want to leave. — OECD (2025b).
- Job satisfaction declines in the years *before* exit (falling pre-exit trend — usable as an early-warning finding), then rises after it: +0.13 SD at one year, +0.19 SD at three years vs stayers; overall life satisfaction shows no significant change (do not conflate). — Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025), p. 11, Figure 2, Table A4 (exact anchoring per fix_hilda_fleck).
- Leaver self-reports: lack of respect 6.86%, lack of personal satisfaction 5.71% among most-cited reasons (256 Australian leavers, 2016-2022 — window corrected per ch5_synthesis D5). — Brandenburg et al. (2024).
- Intentions ≠ exits: TALIS leave-intentions (~25%) vastly exceed realised attrition (5-11%); use intention data only for patterning dissatisfaction. — Nguyen et al. (2025).

### Environmental — workload, leadership, collegial support
- Australia (revealed, panel): workload, stress and work-life balance — not salary — drive exit (HILDA fixed-effects, 2001-2022). — Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025).
- Australia (stated): most frequent reasons among 256 leavers — school leadership issues 17.71%, workload 16.57%, workplace environment 9.7%, student behaviour 7.43%, administrative overload 5.14%. — Brandenburg et al. (2024).
- England: teachers averaged 48.1 hours/week in 2024 (down from 49.4 in 2023); 43% felt workload was not acceptable (57% in 2022). — Department for Education (2025b).
- US (correlational): moving from low to high perceived leadership effectiveness cuts turnover 18.7% → 9.0% (-52%); low to high job satisfaction 22.0% → 8.0% (-64%) — linear probability model, no identification. — Tan et al. (2026).
- Synthesis: supportive school leadership is the key non-pay correlate in the quasi-experimental literature. — Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026).
- Australia intentions: 39% intended to leave before retirement in 2023; drivers workload (75%), work-life balance (69%), wellbeing/stress (68%). — AITSL (2025).
- Netherlands (the reserve's own answer): what would bring the hidden reserve back is lower work pressure, more control over hours, more autonomy — more than salary; inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs. — Somers et al. (2024a).
- Where it weighs more: the conditions-first pattern is *revealed* in Australia, England and the Netherlands (see wage evidence under Economic).
- **THIN:** collegial support has no direct exit evidence in the base beyond leadership/environment items; the nearest is the induction/mentoring literature (Smith and Ingersoll-type findings live only in lit_retiree_mentoring with unverified point estimates — do not quote numbers).

### Economic — salary vs alternatives, pension design, contract
- US salary gradient: each USD 1,000 (cost-of-living-adjusted) is associated with -0.34 pp turnover; predicted turnover falls ~40% from a USD 50,000 to a USD 70,000 salary (correlational). — Tan et al. (2026).
- US stated: Michigan certificate-holders lead with money — 33% put higher salary among their top three reasons for not teaching; a salary increase is the most frequently selected return incentive, with retirees-keep-benefits, better benefits and loan forgiveness also high (30% response rate; authors call the sample not representative). — Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan (2021), Figure 1.
- But leavers rarely out-earn teaching: very few Georgia leavers took jobs paying more than teaching (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006); US leavers' mean earnings remain slightly below pre-exit even 8 years later (Brummet et al., 2024); Australian leavers' weekly wages are -6.2% at one year and -9.4% at three, hourly wages lower for a decade — only continuously full-time leavers gain, by working more hours (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, Table 4 — per fix_hilda_fleck); English leavers accept a ~10% wage cut for shorter hours and higher satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015).
- Pension design: pension wealth accrual in six US state systems is highly nonlinear — spikes in the mid-to-late 50s then dramatic decline or negative returns — creating pull-then-push retirement incentives at ages young for professionals (Costrell and Podgursky, 2009); what is distinctive about teacher turnover vs comparable professions is precisely early, pension-driven retirement (Harris and Adams, 2007). Teachers value current cash roughly five-to-one over deferred pension wealth (willing to pay ~20 cents per dollar of present-value pension wealth) (Fitzpatrick, 2015). Pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4-10 pp (Fleck et al., 2025).
- Causal retention prices (transfer to re-entry only with the C1 caveat, verbatim: no quasi-experimental study anywhere directly prices *re-entry* incentives; leavers have already revealed a preference against current conditions): England GBP 2,000/yr after tax made eligible maths/physics teachers 23% less likely to leave (Sims and Benhenda, 2022); Florida loan forgiveness cut maths/science attrition 10.4%/8.9%, one-time bonus up to ~32% short-run (Feng and Sass, 2018); North Carolina USD 1,800 bonus cut target-group turnover ~17% (Clotfelter et al., 2008).
- Contract insecurity: only 48% of Australian early-career teachers hold ongoing contracts. — AITSL (2025).
- Where it weighs more: the salary margin is US-weighted — both the survey (Michigan) and the correlational gradient (Tan) are American, and 72 of ~86 studies in the causal base are US (Fleck et al., 2025).

### Life-cycle — family, youth exploration, retirement
- Family: the presence of a newborn is the dominant exit factor for the historic US female cohort, ahead of marriage or pay; many later return (Stinebrickner, 2002). Returners are less likely to come back with young children at home (Grissom and Reininger, 2012). Family-friendly policies (childcare support, part-time work) help women return later; science/high-credential leavers are less likely to ever return (OECD, 2005, pp. 176-177). Career breakers — the most return-committed NFER segment — are the policy face of this margin (Buchanan et al., 2018).
- Youth exploration: early-career teachers behave like early-career workers in comparable professions (Harris and Adams, 2007); one in three resigning teachers across seven OECD countries had under five years' experience (OECD, 2025a, p. 495); one in five under-30 teachers intends to leave within five years, more than four in ten in Latvia (53%), Lithuania (50%) and Estonia (49%) (OECD, 2025b, p. 232).
- Retirement: retirements exceed half of all departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a, Table D8.4); the pension spike mechanics above (Costrell and Podgursky, 2009); US older teachers are now slightly *less* likely to leave than in the 1990s (Aldeman and Yi, 2025).
- Where it weighs more: retirement-heavy continental systems (FR, BE-fr, EL, IE, IL) vs family/switching margins in Anglophone resignation-heavy systems.

**Cross-country variation the base documents (state as a finding with numbers, per TEAM_INPUT §2):** conditions-first evidence is revealed (Australian wages fall while satisfaction rises; English leavers accept -10%; Dutch inactives already rate outside conditions better); pay-first evidence is stated, from one survey with a 30% response rate its own authors call unrepresentative, whose incentive list is dominated by financial items (spine, Tension B). Entry motives are similar everywhere; what differs is what the profession offers (Watt et al., 2012; OECD, 2019: ~90% cite social contribution, teaching first-choice career for 2 in 3).

## (c) WHERE THEY GO — destinations

- **US, linked administrative records (the anchor):** more than half of leavers remain in education broadly defined, about a fifth exit the labour force entirely, fewer than a quarter take jobs outside education; mean earnings remain slightly below pre-exit even 8 years later (district records linked to IRS/Census, 2003/04-2014/15). — Brummet et al. (2024).
- **US, historic cohort:** ~67% of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely (Stinebrickner, 2002); very few leavers out-earn teaching (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006).
- **Australia (HILDA, Table 2, verified full text):** first year after exit — 22.1% in non-teaching roles within school education; 21.8% in other Education, Health Care and Social Assistance (higher education, adult education, educational support, childcare, social assistance); 11.4% other sectors; 28.0% not in the labour force; 13.9% retired; 2.9% unemployed (n<30). Around 20% remain in education or care industries five years out. Phrase the caring-fields claim as the combined Education/Health/Social Assistance category, never "healthcare" alone. — Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025), Table 2, p. 7 and p. 14 (per fix_hilda_fleck).
- **Australia (leaver survey):** 51.8% work in education-related fields (coaching, counselling, social work); 36% in education sectors (policy, publishing, higher education, museums); 20% moved to higher education; 62% use their teaching skills "a lot". — Brandenburg et al. (2024).
- **England:** more than half of leavers (excluding retirees) take jobs in the education sector, at a ~10% average wage cut vs comparable stayers, gaining shorter hours and satisfaction. — Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015).
- **Cross-country composition point:** in the older US cohort most exiting women left employment entirely; in contemporary Australia, England and the US most leavers keep working, usually inside or adjacent to education, at flat-to-lower earnings. The modern former teacher is typically employed, nearby and reachable — a genuine population, not a vanished one — and the composition shifts with cohort and system (labour-force exit dominant historically and still ~20-28% today; education-adjacent employment dominant now). — Stinebrickner (2002) vs Brummet et al. (2024), Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025), Brandenburg et al. (2024), Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015).

**THIN:** only the US has linked tax-record destination evidence (Brummet); Australia's is panel-survey; England's is one 2015 NFER analysis; no destination evidence exists in the base for any non-Anglophone system except the Dutch reserve's sectoral composition (19.8%/22.7% of post-2000 qualifiers in other sectors; 7.4%/10.2% unemployed/on benefits — Somers et al., 2024b).

## (d) WHAT THEY CAN BRING BACK

**Peer spillovers — the endogenous-growth thread (NARRATIVE_REFORM thread 5).**
- A 1 SD increase in the quality of a teacher's peers raises her students' test-score growth by 0.02 SD in reading and 0.04 SD in maths (about one-third of the own-teacher effect); spillovers are strongest for less-experienced teachers and persistent; historical peer quality explains ~20% of the own-teacher effect. This is the best causal footing for the claim that exposure to experienced educators transfers skill. — Jackson and Bruegmann (2009).
- Experience returns at scale when rules change: North Carolina's returners averaged 31 years of experience and went disproportionately to high-need schools. — Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024).
- Supply-side interest (secondary, cite as such): at least 1.8 million US K-12 teachers and leaders reached retirement age 2010-2020; more than half of baby-boomer teacher retirees expressed interest in further employment, ~70% of those in education roles. — Foster (2010), as cited in DeCesare, McClelland and Randel (2017) [KEY MISSING — secondary citation only].

**The tutoring effect evidence.**
- Pooled preK-12 tutoring RCT effect: 0.288 SD (SE 0.029) in the published meta-analysis; effects largest for teacher and paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades, at ≥3 days/week, during school; volunteer/parent tutoring smaller, mainly in reading. — Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2024).
- Number Rockets: 86 tutors from retired and substitute pools reproduced at scale (994 grade-1 students, 76 schools, four urban districts in four REL Southwest states) an effect first obtained with graduate students: +0.34 on TEMA-3. — Gersten et al. (2015); Rolfhus et al. (2012).
- Experience Corps: ES 0.13 (passage comprehension) and 0.16 (teacher-rated reading), gains larger at ≥35 sessions; volunteers aged 50+, former-teacher share unpublished. — Morrow-Howell et al. (2009); What Works Clearinghouse (2010).
- Mentoring: the one RCT of retirees as new-teacher mentors is a qualified null — WWC "meets standards without reservations", all three reviewed domains (reading, maths, retention) indeterminate. — DeCesare, McClelland and Randel (2017); What Works Clearinghouse (2019). Mentoring capacity is scarce (22% of novices have an assigned mentor; 38% induction participation — OECD, 2019), and the biggest induction RCT found no retention effect (Glazerman et al., 2010).
- Dosage thresholds recur: ≥35 sessions (Experience Corps), ≥3 days/week (Nickow), >25 mentoring hours (Aurora exploratory). Token-hours recruitment should expect no measured effect.

**The measurement gap — P4 exactly as the base's amended form states it.**
- P4 (ARGUMENT_SPINE, as approved): "What former teachers can be asked to do is a designed role at a specified dose, and where a system has specified the role it has also measured it: structured tutoring at a stated protocol and dose carries trial evidence at scale, while the mentoring role has one small trial that set no dose and was rated indeterminate — so the craft rationale stands as an open question with a price attached, and the asymmetry between the two is one of measurement effort."
- Amendment 1 (TEAM_INPUT §6, binding): the tutoring/mentoring asymmetry must NOT be written as evidence that former teachers' accumulated judgement is worth little, nor as a verdict against the craft rationale. It is a gap in what has been measured about the DESIGNED ROLE: the trials tested one deployment well and the other badly or not at all; the craft rationale remains unevaluated, not refuted. The paper states what would settle it — linking returner records to the classes and colleagues they work with, and trials of designed roles at specified dose — and asks for that measurement.
- The hole, stated plainly (spine P4 answer): no study anywhere in the base links a returner to a student outcome or a colleague's; the authors of the largest re-entry episode say they could not attempt it (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Meanwhile the per-se case is carried by what the evidence does carry: a valid or restorable licence; immediate deployability consuming no ITE place; institutional fluency plus standing outside the school's evaluation hierarchy — the trust advantage Aurora's own report identifies.
- Attribution caveats to concede once, early: Number Rockets tutors were retired AND substitute teachers, no split; Nickow does not isolate retired educators as a tutor class; Experience Corps is evidence about older volunteers, not former teachers.

**THIN:** the entire "bring back" case for *returners specifically* is inferential — peer spillovers are measured on serving colleagues, tutoring on mixed retiree/substitute workforces; nothing measures a returner's effect directly. That is the point of P4.

### Shortage context (use per NARRATIVE_REFORM thread 3: named honestly, from the base)
- 44 million additional primary/secondary teachers needed by 2030; ~15 million in Sub-Saharan Africa; more than half of the gap is replacement of leavers, not expansion. — UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024).
- Principal-reported shortage rose from 26% (PISA 2018) to 47% (PISA 2022) of students in affected schools, +21 pp; >30 pp increases in Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Latvia, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal (OECD countries; Cambodia and Guatemala among partners). Perception measure, per OECD's own caveat. — OECD (2025a), p. 490; OECD (2023b), p. 171, Figure II.5.3 (exact rendering per fix_oecd_primary A1.1; never mix with EPO 2024's 2015 baseline of 29%).
- Objective margins: across 14 systems, on average 1.6% of teaching positions unfilled and 4.9% of teachers not fully qualified; Austria 4,778 vacancies (4.6%), Sweden 5.0% and 18.7% not fully qualified; Austria's vacancies equalled 107% of the prior year's teaching graduates. — OECD (2025a), p. 491, Tables D8.2-D8.3.
- TALIS 2024: 23% of teachers work in schools where qualified-teacher shortage hinders quality instruction (~half in Bahrain, Latvia, Netherlands; <10% in eight systems); 17% of non-retiring teachers intend to leave within five years; one in five under-30s. Title is *Results from TALIS 2024*, not "Volume I". — OECD (2025b), pp. 41-42, 232, 234 (per fix_oecd_primary A1.3).
- Netherlands (register benchmark): 9,800 FTE primary (9.7%) and 3,800 FTE secondary (5.8%) shortage at end-2023 vs a hidden reserve of ~62,000 (report as context, never as the paper's promise — TEAM_INPUT §3). — Somers et al. (2024b); DUO (2026).
- Japan: 2,558 unfilled positions (0.31% of 836,079 required) at 5.8% of public schools, AY2021; 3,827 in the 2025 survey. — Nippon.com (2022); Nippon.com (2026).
- EU: shortages reported in nearly all Education and Training Monitor 2023 country reports; no comparative EU shortage indicator exists. — European Commission (2023).

### Ageing evidence (thread 4)
- EU: ageing worst in Greece (secondary) and Portugal (primary and secondary), with the Baltics and Hungary of concern; Italy's 55+ share significantly outweighs its under-30s; ageing worsens from primary to upper secondary. — European Commission (2023), Figure 1 discussion. (The "only ~one-third of teachers in Europe under 40" figure is Eurydice 2021 [S] and its key is [KEY MISSING] — prefer the ETM 2023 items.)
- Germany: 457,000 retired/pensioned teachers (2021) against ~800,000 serving. — Deutsches Schulportal (2023).
- Australia: 17% of teachers aged 60+; ~19% of 553,300 registered teachers not in schools; over half of non-teaching registrants would consider returning "under the right circumstances", 5% actively looking. — AITSL (2025).
- US: pension accrual spikes make teachers retire considerably earlier than comparable professionals. — Costrell and Podgursky (2009); Harris and Adams (2007). Secondary: 1.8 million reaching retirement age 2010-2020 (Foster, 2010, via DeCesare et al., 2017) [secondary citation].
- Retiree pools self-deplete: only ~35% of Japanese age-based elementary retirees (55% junior high) now seek full-time re-employment; Austria's audited intakes peaked in 2021/22-2022/23 and fell. — Sakuma and Shimazaki (2024), p. 201; Rechnungshof (2025), pp. 24-25.

### Returners as a share of entrants (the flow that motivates the paper)
- UK 2000/01: returners were ~25% of the full-time inflow into maintained schools and more than 50% of the part-time inflow. — OECD (2005), p. 68.
- England now: ~17,000 teachers return to state schools each year, about one third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023); annual returners rose from 16,010 (2011) to 17,230 (2016) (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 16); ~14,500/year against ~250,000 working-age former teachers (Worth and Sharp, 2018); in 2024/25, 41,700 FTE entered (9.2%), "returners have increased" while other entrant categories fell — the exact returner FTE for 2024/25 is [UNVERIFIED in the headline release; underlying table required] (Department for Education, 2025a).
- United States: re-entrants made up 37-49% of new hires, "one-third to one-half of each year's supply"; yet only around a third of leavers ever return (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016) — one quarter to one half in older cohorts (Grissom and Reininger, 2012), about one fifth for 2000-2019 exits (Moyer, 2022). About half of leavers in the last TFS with the item said they would consider returning (NCES 2013, as cited in Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, p. 3).
- OECD's own hook: "in most countries there are pathways for teachers to return to the profession if they wish" (OECD, 2025a, p. 495); re-entry is one of EPO 2024's four supply levers, with the data-gap sentence for the accountability lesson (OECD, 2024, Figure 2.5, p. 48; pp. 50-51).
