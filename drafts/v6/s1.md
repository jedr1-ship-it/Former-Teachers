# 1. Introduction

Every year, substantial numbers of teachers leave the profession, whether for other work, for time outside the labour market or for retirement. Across the 19 OECD education systems with comparable data, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left teaching in 2022/23, and national leaving rates range from under 3% to over 10%.[^1] Attrition on this scale is a standing feature of school systems rather than an episode in any one labour market. In the United States, the share of teachers leaving the occupation each year has been broadly stable for four decades, at rates comparable to those of nurses and accountants (Aldeman and Yi, 2025; Harris and Adams, 2007). At the global level the movement has been upward, with the attrition rate among primary school teachers worldwide nearly doubling between 2015 and 2022, from 4.62% to 9.06% (UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024).

The composition of these departures varies as much as their level. Teachers who leave form three distinct populations, those who move to other jobs, those who withdraw from the labour market and those who retire. On average across the same 19 systems, 51% of leavers resigned rather than retired, a balance that runs from resignation-dominated systems such as England (United Kingdom) and Denmark, where resignations exceed 80% of departures, to systems such as France, Greece and Ireland, where retirements account for more than half (OECD, 2025a). Which population dominates matters for policy, because the three populations leave for different motives and face different obstacles to coming back, and each is reached by different instruments.

Recruitment difficulties raise the stakes of this outflow. Most OECD countries face serious teacher shortages. The share of 15-year-old students in schools whose principals reported that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff rose from 26% in 2018 to 47% in 2022, a perception measure, but one that rose by more than 30 percentage points in eight OECD countries (OECD, 2023b), and UNESCO projects a need for 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers worldwide by 2030, more than half of which is replacement of teachers who leave rather than expansion of the workforce (UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024). The workforce doing the replacing is meanwhile ageing. Among European Union systems, ageing is most acute in Greece and Portugal, and in Italy teachers aged 55 and over significantly outnumber those under 30 (European Commission, 2023); in Germany, the 457 000 retired or pensioned teachers counted in 2021 stand against a serving workforce of roughly 800 000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Among serving teachers, 19% across OECD countries report a lot of stress at work, and one in five teachers under 30 intends to leave within five years (OECD, 2025b).

The same outflow builds the stock from which part of the replacement could come. England counted around 260 000 qualified teachers of working age out of service in 2018 (Worth, 2023); in Michigan (United States), 43% of the 141 810 teachers holding certificates were not teaching in a public school in 2017/18 (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021); in Australia, around 19% of the country's 553 300 registered teachers were not working in schools, and more than half of these registrants reported that they would consider returning under the right circumstances (AITSL, 2025). Return flows from such stocks are already a substantial supply channel. Roughly 17 000 teachers return to English state schools each year, about one third of all new entrants (Department for Education, 2023), and re-entrants have made up between 37% and 49% of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016), although only a minority of leavers, between one fifth and one half depending on the cohort, ever return (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022). Movement at this scale is consistent with teaching functioning as a profession that people leave and rejoin over a working life, and most countries already maintain pathways for teachers to return if they wish (OECD, 2024; OECD, 2025a).

Governments have implemented active policies to re-attract former teachers for decades, as Section 3 documents. As early as 2005, the OECD recommended that education systems maintain contact with former teachers, keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities, and provide targeted retraining (OECD, 2005). The statutory record begins earlier and continues later, from the return-to-work window that North Carolina (United States) opened to retired teachers in 1999 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024), through returner subsidies the Netherlands regulated in 2017 (Ministerie van OCW, 2017), to the returner contracts Portugal created in 2024 (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024) and the outreach campaigns mounted in Austria, Germany and New South Wales (Australia) in the 2020s. Most of the instruments now in force were adopted or expanded under post-pandemic staffing pressure, and few of them have been evaluated.

This policy paper compiles and analyses the international record of policies to re-attract former teachers, organised into five instrument families, together with the evidence on why teachers leave, where they go and what they can bring back. The families are, in the order in which Section 3 treats them, pension and employment rules governing re-employment; certification and licensing routes back; money attached to the return; active recruitment and accompaniment; and return as a defined role. The first two families lower what a return costs the returner, in salary-step placement, pension-salary concurrency, waiting periods and licensing costs, a sum Section 3 defines as the price of a return; the other three add money, information or a different job on top of whatever that price is. The population concerned justifies attention beyond the vacancies it might cover, since experienced returners carry teaching capacity that school systems themselves financed, of a kind shown to spill over to colleagues and their students (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009).

The compiled record is administrative rather than experimental, consisting mainly of statutes and take-up counts with a small number of independent audits and randomised trials, and the analysis extends only as far as such material allows. Section 2 examines who leaves the profession, for which motives, into which destinations and with what remaining capacity to teach; Section 3 analyses the five policy families and the take-up and evaluation record each has produced; Section 4 sets out the conclusions and recommendations that this record supports.

## Notes

[^1]: Across the 19 OECD education systems with comparable data, the leaving rate for fully qualified teachers remained below 3% in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel and exceeded 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania in 2022/23 (OECD, 2025a, Table D8.4). In England, the Department for Education counted 40 800 full-time-equivalent leavers from state schools in 2023/24, 9.0% of the qualified workforce (Department for Education, 2025a). In the United States, the National Center for Education Statistics put the leaving rate at 8% of public school teachers for 2021, unchanged since 2012 (NCES, 2023), and pooled Current Population Survey estimates for 2015-2024 average 7.6% a year (Aldeman and Yi, 2025). In Australia, household panel data record a leaving rate of 11.6% in 2022, below its 2012 peak of 16.3% (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). Of particular interest is the case of Austria, where the rate nearly doubled within a decade, from 3.2% in 2014/15 to 6.2% in 2022/23 (OECD, 2025a).
