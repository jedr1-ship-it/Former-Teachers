# LITERATURE REVIEW CORE — Why teachers leave the profession

Prepared 24 August 2026 for the literature-review section of the OECD-style policy paper.
Scope: the canonical and current international evidence base on teacher exit, with priority to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, large-scale panel and administrative-data studies, and internationally comparative work.

**Relationship to the existing files.** This document is additive to `drafts/v6/evidencia_s2.md` and `research/lit_*.md`, which remain the floor. Studies already held there (Ingersoll and Strong 2011; Smith and Ingersoll 2004; Nguyen et al. 2020; Tan et al. 2026; Stinebrickner 2002; Harris and Adams 2007; Costrell and Podgursky 2009; Clotfelter et al. 2008; Feng and Sass 2018; Sims and Benhenda 2022; Fitzpatrick 2015; Glazerman et al. 2010; Worth et al. 2015; Worth and Sharp 2018; Cuervo and Vera-Toscano 2025; Brandenburg et al. 2024; Somers et al. 2024; Sutcher et al. 2016; UNESCO 2024; the TALIS volumes) are cross-referenced but not re-described in full unless this session added materially to them. Every entry below that is **NEW** to the paper's base is flagged and appears in §14.

**Reading conventions used throughout.**
- `[CAUSAL]` — random assignment, or a credible quasi-experiment (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables, structural model with out-of-sample validation).
- `[ASSOC]` — regression on observational data without an identification strategy, including most survey-linked and meta-analytic correlational work.
- `[DESCRIPTIVE]` — counts, rates, shares.
- `[QUAL]` — interview, narrative-biographical or open-response evidence.
- Where a study measures **intention** rather than **exit**, this is stated in the finding line. The two are not interchangeable (see §10).

**Count.** 63 studies and official sources are characterised below; 47 of them are new to the paper's evidence base.

## 1. Foundational and framing work

**State of the evidence.** Strong and settled at the level of framing, thin at the level of numbers. The field's organising claim — that teacher shortage is largely an organisational and retention problem rather than a supply problem — rests on one influential 2001 analysis of US survey data that is descriptive and correlational, not causal; it has been enormously productive as a research programme but has never been directly tested by experiment. The two canonical reviews (2006, 2008) are now old enough that their headline results have been partly overturned by the 2020 and 2024 updates, particularly on gender. The interpretive tradition (Kelchtermans) is the only strand that treats leaving as a decision with a biography rather than a hazard rate, and it is almost entirely qualitative and Flemish/European in origin.

**1.1 Ingersoll, R. M. (2001), "Teacher Turnover and Teacher Shortages: An Organizational Analysis", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 38/3, pp. 499–534.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* multivariate analysis of a nationally representative cross-section with a one-year follow-up. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* United States; Schools and Staffing Survey (SASS) 1990–91 and its Teacher Follow-up Survey (TFS) 1991–92; teacher-level analysis nested in schools.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* school staffing problems are "not primarily due to teacher shortages, in the technical sense of an insufficient supply of qualified teachers"; they are driven by **excess demand generated by a "revolving door"** — large numbers of qualified teachers departing for reasons other than retirement. Total annual turnover of the order of 15% at the time of writing, of which retirement is a minority. Organisational conditions of schools — inadequate administrative support, student discipline problems, limited faculty influence over decisions, low salaries — predict departure after controlling for teacher and school characteristics. Small schools and private schools have markedly higher turnover, which the paper reads as evidence against a pure demographic account.
- *Limitation the paper states:* the data cannot separate voluntary from involuntary departure cleanly, and the cross-sectional design cannot establish that improving conditions would reduce turnover — the association is compatible with sorting of dissatisfied teachers into poorly-run schools.
- *Why it matters for policy:* this is the source of the "revolving door" and "shortage-as-organisational-problem" framing that the paper adopts. It licenses the move from *recruit more* to *retain and re-attract*.

**1.2 Guarino, C. M., L. Santibañez and G. A. Daley (2006), "Teacher Recruitment and Retention: A Review of the Recent Empirical Literature", *Review of Educational Research*, Vol. 76/2, pp. 173–208.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* narrative and evaluative review, structured by evidence quality. `[ASSOC]` (reviewing associational primary work)
- *Sample and country:* 46 empirical studies, overwhelmingly United States.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* attrition follows a **U-shaped age/experience curve** — high in the first five years, falling through mid-career, rising again as retirement approaches. Teachers with higher measured academic ability and those in mathematics and science are more likely to leave. Schools with higher proportions of minority, low-income and low-achieving students have more difficulty retaining teachers. Higher salaries and better working conditions are associated with retention, but the review judges the identification in the underlying studies to be generally weak and warns that most compensation studies cannot separate the salary effect from the effects of the districts that pay those salaries.
- *Limitation the paper states:* almost no experimental or quasi-experimental evidence existed at the time; the review explicitly declines to make causal policy claims.

**1.3 Borman, G. D. and N. M. Dowling (2008), "Teacher Attrition and Retention: A Meta-Analytic and Narrative Review of the Research", *Review of Educational Research*, Vol. 78/3, pp. 367–409.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* meta-analysis of 34 studies covering 63 attrition moderators, plus narrative review. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* United States.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* both **personal characteristics** (age, gender, race, ability, subject specialism, marital and family status) and **school attributes** (organisational characteristics, student body composition, resources including instructional spending and teacher salaries) independently moderate attrition. The review reported women, younger teachers, White teachers, and teachers with higher measured ability as more likely to leave, and identified school organisational conditions — collegial support, administrative support, teacher influence — as the school-level factors with the most consistent protective association.
- *Limitation the paper states:* heterogeneity across studies in how "attrition" is operationalised (leaving a school versus leaving the profession) limits pooling; most included studies are cross-sectional.
- *Superseded in part:* the gender result and several others are reversed or dissolved by Nguyen et al. (2020) — see §2.1 and §12.

**1.4 Kelchtermans, G. (2017), "'Should I stay or should I go?': unpacking teacher attrition/retention as an educational issue", *Teachers and Teaching*, Vol. 23/8, pp. 961–977.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* conceptual and integrative essay drawing on two decades of narrative-biographical fieldwork. `[QUAL]`
- *Sample and country:* Flanders (Belgium), with international referencing.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* attrition/retention is a "wicked issue" that lacks a clear definition; the author proposes that, framed *educationally*, the problem is "the need to prevent good teachers from leaving the job for the wrong reasons" — not the minimisation of all exit. Three lessons are foregrounded: (i) the centrality of **social relationships, recognition and belonging**; (ii) the corrosive effect of **performativity policies** on teachers' sense of professional self; (iii) attrition must be read **within a career and work-life perspective**, not as a discrete event.
- *Limitation the paper states:* it is explicitly interpretive; it makes no claim to estimate magnitudes and warns against reading the qualitative categories as a causal model.

**1.5 Kelchtermans, G. (2005), "Teachers' emotions in educational reforms: Self-understanding, vulnerable commitment and micropolitical literacy", *Teaching and Teacher Education*, Vol. 21/8, pp. 995–1006; and Kelchtermans, G. (1993/2009) on the micropolitics of teacher induction.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* narrative-biographical life-history interviews. `[QUAL]`
- *Sample and country:* experienced primary teachers in Flanders (Belgium).
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **vulnerability is a structural condition of teaching**, not a personal deficit, arising at three levels: the classroom (limits on instructional impact), the school (dependence on principals, colleagues, parents) and the policy level. Reforms carrying normative beliefs about good teaching provoke strong emotions and micropolitical responses — resistance or proactive attempts to change working conditions. "Micropolitical literacy" is presented as a learnable professional competence whose absence raises the risk of early exit.
- *Limitation:* small-n, single-system, no exit outcomes measured.

**1.6 Ingersoll, R., E. Merrill, D. Stuckey, G. Collins and B. Harrison (2021), *Seven Trends: The Transformation of the Teaching Force* (updated), Consortium for Policy Research in Education, University of Pennsylvania.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* repeated-cross-section descriptive analysis of SASS/NTPS, 1987–88 to 2017–18. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Sample and country:* United States, whole teaching force.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the workforce has become **larger, greyer at one end and greener at the other, more female, more diverse, and less stable**. The modal years of experience fell sharply (the "greening" of the force), while the number of teachers near retirement also rose — a bimodal experience distribution. Minority teachers rose from 12.5% (1987–88) to about 20% (2017–18), growing more than three times as fast as White teachers, but with higher turnover, which the authors call the "minority teacher shortage" paradox.
- *Limitation the paper states:* trends are descriptive; no causal attribution to policy.

**1.7 Sutcher, L., L. Darling-Hammond and D. Carver-Thomas (2019), "Understanding teacher shortages: An analysis of teacher supply and demand in the United States", *Education Policy Analysis Archives*, Vol. 27/35.** **[NEW; the 2016 LPI companion is already in `lit_reserve.md`]**
- *Design:* national supply-and-demand projection model built on NCES series, plus descriptive analysis of TFS exit reasons. `[DESCRIPTIVE]` with modelled projections
- *Sample and country:* United States, 1988–2025 projections.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the annual attrition rate is about **8%**, up from below 6% in 1989; **retirement accounts for only about one third of leavers, so roughly two thirds of attrition is pre-retirement** and hence potentially policy-responsive. Among 2012–13 leavers, only **13%** named retirement as the most important reason; **55%** cited some form of dissatisfaction, with **accountability and testing pressure the single most frequently named area (25% of leavers)**. Reducing attrition from 8% to 4% — the rate observed in Finland, Singapore, Ontario and in several New England states — would cut annual hiring need by roughly 130,000 teachers and "virtually eliminate" the national shortage.
- *Limitation the paper states:* the projections are sensitive to the attrition assumption itself; "shortage" is defined against a normative standard of fully qualified supply, and localised shortages would persist even under national balance.
- *Caution for our paper:* the 4% international benchmark is drawn from selected comparators and is not like-for-like with the OECD attrition definition (see §11.1).

## 2. Recent meta-analyses and systematic reviews

**State of the evidence.** This is now the strongest part of the field. Four large syntheses published since 2020 converge on the same ordering — school organisational conditions and administrative support dominate the modifiable school-level factors; personal characteristics matter but less than the older reviews claimed; pay has a real but small average association with *exit* while having a large association with *choice* in experimental settings. The critical fault line is that the psychological meta-analyses (Madigan and Kim; Li and Yao) pool almost exclusively **intention** outcomes, while the economics and education-policy syntheses (Nguyen et al.; Gundlach et al.; See et al.) pool **behaviour**; the two literatures give different-looking answers partly because they measure different things. The intervention review (See et al.) is the only one that quality-appraises for causal identification, and its headline conclusion is largely negative: very little is known to work.

**2.1 Nguyen, T. D., L. D. Pham, M. Crouch and M. G. Springer (2020), "The correlates of teacher turnover: An updated and expanded meta-analysis of the literature", *Educational Research Review*, Vol. 31, 100355.** *(In the base; substantially extended here from the full working-paper text, EdWorkingPaper 19-149.)*
- *Design:* meta-analysis, random-effects, primarily odds ratios with hazard-ratio robustness checks and robust variance estimation. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* 120 studies, United States, published from roughly 2006 onward (updating Borman and Dowling).
- *Findings, precisely stated (odds ratios unless noted):*
  - **Age:** teachers older than 28 have **30% lower odds** of leaving their school than those aged 28 or younger (OR 0.70).
  - **Gender:** across **37 studies, no difference** between female and male teachers — directly contradicting Borman and Dowling (2008).
  - **Race:** no evidence Black teachers are more likely to leave than White teachers; Hispanic teachers' odds of leaving teaching are **53% lower** than White teachers'; non-White minority teachers generally less likely to leave.
  - **Certification:** standard certification cuts the odds of attrition by **47%** (OR 0.53, 16 studies).
  - **Experience:** an additional year of experience is not associated with leaving, but **new teachers relative to veterans have 54% higher odds** of attrition.
  - **Working conditions:** teachers in schools with better working conditions have odds of leaving lower "by nearly a factor of two" (5 studies).
  - **Administrative support:** OR **0.80** (13 studies) — stronger administrative support, lower attrition.
  - **Induction/mentoring and good in-service professional development:** both associated with lower attrition for beginners.
  - **Leadership and collaboration measured directly:** *not* significantly associated with attrition, but imprecisely estimated on few studies — a null of low power, not a demonstrated absence.
  - **School resources:** **no evidence that reducing class size or adding teacher aides reduces attrition** in the odds-ratio models; including hazard-ratio studies, classroom assistants do become protective.
  - **Student body:** attrition lower in higher-achieving schools; percent Black and percent Hispanic students **not** significantly associated with attrition once other factors are held constant.
  - **Salary:** across 18 studies an increase in salary (usually operationalised as +USD 1,000) **slightly** reduces the odds of leaving; the point estimate is close to 1 and larger only for larger salary comparisons.
  - **Merit pay:** reduces the probability of attrition by **1.6 percentage points** on average (linear probability estimates); one study finds ~15 percentage points in hard-to-staff schools.
  - **Retention bonuses:** reduce the odds of leaving by about **8%** (hazard-inclusive specification).
  - **Teacher effectiveness:** more effective teachers are **not** more likely to leave; if anything slightly less likely.
  - **Teacher evaluation:** the practice of being evaluated is associated with **decreased** odds of attrition — being assessed does not by itself drive teachers out.
  - **Union membership:** OR 0.75, only marginally significant, 3 studies.
- *Limitations the paper states:* mixing of hazard ratios and odds ratios; studies that combine leavers and school-switchers; small study counts in several categories make robust variance estimation unreliable; US-only, so external validity to other systems is untested.

**2.2 Gundlach, H. A. D., G. R. Slemp and J. Hattie (2024), "A meta-analysis of the antecedents of teacher turnover and retention", *Educational Research Review*, Vol. 44, 100606.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* large-scale meta-analysis over a database of nearly 70 antecedents and **3,430 correlation coefficients**, distinguishing attrition, migration, and retention, and separately intentions versus behaviours. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* international, English-language.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* lower job satisfaction, lower commitment, lower motivation and lower self-efficacy are all associated with intention to leave; **affective commitment shows the strongest single association with turnover intention (r = −0.67)** in school settings. Schools with higher principal effectiveness, regular and supportive communication, and better facilities and resources show lower attrition. Crucially, the authors document **systematic differences in the strength of association between a given antecedent and *actual* turnover versus the same antecedent and *intended* turnover** — the same variable does not have the same predictive weight for the two outcomes, partly because real turnover contains involuntary separations.
- *Limitation the paper states:* correlational throughout; heterogeneity in construct measurement across the 70 antecedents; the intention/behaviour asymmetry is documented but not explained.
- *Why it matters:* this is the single best citation for the proposition that the psychological antecedents literature cannot be read directly as a model of exit behaviour.

**2.3 Madigan, D. J. and L. E. Kim (2021), "Towards an understanding of teacher attrition: A meta-analysis of burnout, job satisfaction, and teachers' intentions to quit", *Teaching and Teacher Education*, Vol. 105, 103425.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* random-effects meta-analysis with meta-regression, subgroup analysis, publication-bias testing, and a multiple regression / relative weights analysis on the pooled correlation matrix. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* 24 studies reporting 38 effect sizes — 10 studies (11 samples, **N = 3,842**) on burnout and intentions to quit; 14 studies (**N = 6,678**) on job satisfaction and intentions to quit. Six of the burnout studies are North American (USA, Canada), five from elsewhere (China, New Zealand, Belgium); the job-satisfaction studies include Pakistan and Norway. Mostly cross-sectional.
- *Findings, precisely stated (all outcomes are **intention to quit**, not exit):*
  - Emotional **exhaustion**: r+ = **.41** (95% CI .32 to .50).
  - **Depersonalisation**: r+ = **.32** (95% CI .13 to .49).
  - **Reduced accomplishment**: r+ = **.21** (95% CI .04 to .36).
  - **Job satisfaction**: r+ = **−.40** (95% CI −.47 to −.32).
  - Burnout dimensions and job satisfaction jointly explain **27% of the variance** in intentions to quit; **burnout accounts for 63% of that explained variance**, exhaustion and job satisfaction each accounting for roughly 37%. Burnout is therefore a *more* important predictor of quit intentions than job satisfaction.
  - **Publication year is a significant moderator** (exhaustion β = .78, p < .001): the burnout–intention link has **strengthened over time**. Age, sex composition, experience, burnout instrument, country and school phase do **not** moderate; effects in North America and elsewhere are statistically indistinguishable.
  - Little evidence of publication bias.
- *Limitations the paper states, verbatim in substance:* the meta-analysis is built on **cross-sectional** studies, so it "provide[s] limited evidence in regard to causation"; the authors **relied on intentions as a proxy for attrition** because the literature search "found very few studies that had measured actual attrition"; a substantial share of variance in intentions remains unexplained.

**2.4 Li, R. and M. Yao (2022), "What promotes teachers' turnover intention? Evidence from a meta-analysis", *Educational Research Review*, Vol. 37, 100477.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* meta-analysis with moderator analysis. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* **94 studies, 99 independent samples, 269 effect sizes, n = 39,508**; international, including a large non-Anglophone (notably Chinese) component.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **burnout, workload and stress are positively related to turnover intention, and burnout is the single strongest positive predictor**. Twelve antecedents are negatively associated with turnover intention, nine of them strongly; the three most important protective factors are **trust, professional identification and organisational commitment**, followed by job satisfaction, school climate, organisational justice, support, work engagement and intrinsic motivation. Most associations are stable across age, gender, cultural context and school level; the exception is that **burnout and (low) intrinsic motivation predict turnover intention more strongly among younger teachers**.
- *Limitation:* outcome is intention throughout; cross-sectional primary studies; the pooled sample is culturally broad but the strongest single national contribution is not the OECD median system.

**2.5 See, B. H., R. Morris, S. Gorard, D. Kokotsaki and S. Abdi (2020), "Teacher Recruitment and Retention: A Critical Review of International Evidence of Most Promising Interventions", *Education Sciences*, Vol. 10/10, 262.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* systematic review restricted to experimental and quasi-experimental designs, with each study **quality-appraised and weighted by strength of evidence** ("the sieve", 1* to 4*). Over 7,000 records screened from 13 databases; **120 pieces of research** synthesised. `[CAUSAL]` at the level of the included studies
- *Sample and country:* international, predominantly United States and England.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - The strongest evidence is that **targeted money can bring people *into* teaching but does not reliably keep them there**. For financial incentives to work at all they must be **large enough to offset the disadvantages of the school or area and the opportunity cost of a more lucrative occupation**, and their retention effect is **short-term** — teachers "only stay while the incentive is available".
  - Discriminatory (narrowly targeted) incentives **may worsen overall retention**; eligibility for a small incentive makes little difference.
  - Many apparently successful monetary schemes work through **contractual tie-ins with penalties for breaking service commitments**, raising the question of whether the mechanism is retention or enforced retention.
  - **CPD and early-career support are "promising" for retention but the evidence for them is weak**: the evidence for mentoring and professional development is "uniformly positive for mentees" only in weaker studies; "the stronger studies do not show consistent positive effects", and several use *intention* rather than actual attrition as the outcome.
  - **"There is no evidence that any other approaches work, largely because of the lack of robust studies."** The review found very few rigorous evaluations of interventions on accountability, teacher stress, working conditions, behaviour, workload or leadership support — the very factors the correlational literature identifies as most important.
- *Limitation the authors state:* restricting to experimental and quasi-experimental designs means "relevant and potentially useful studies may have been missed or excluded"; measuring school-level approaches at scale is harder than evaluating government-administered financial incentives, which biases the evidence base towards money.
- *Policy reading:* this is the sharpest available statement of the paper's central asymmetry — **we have causal evidence mainly for the lever that the descriptive evidence says matters least.**

**2.6 Thompson-Lee, S., B. H. See and R. M. Klassen (2025), "A systematic review of STEM teacher recruitment and retention interventions", *International Journal of STEM Education*, Vol. 12/1, 33.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* systematic review, nine databases, 5,723 records screened, 112 full texts, **25 studies included**, quality-appraised on the same 1*–4* sieve. `[CAUSAL]`/`[ASSOC]` mixed
- *Sample and country:* mainly United States, 4 from the United Kingdom; 2000–2024.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* financial incentives dominate the recruitment evidence (8 of 14 outcomes) but perform weakly there — the **highest-quality recruitment study finds bonuses have no effect on recruitment**. For **retention**, financial incentives look better: three 3*-rated studies find **reductions in attrition in the range of roughly 20–25%**. Alternative certification pathways show minimal long-term retention effects. Realistic job previews show promise for raising interest but were never followed through into training or employment.
- *Limitations the authors state:* **no study reached 4\* (RCT standard)** and **no randomised controlled trials were identified**; English-language restriction produces "over-representation of studies from the USA"; most studies fail to distinguish leaving a school from leaving the profession; limited long-term follow-up; bundled interventions prevent isolation of mechanisms.

**2.7 Systematic review of Australian evidence: "A systematic review of risk and protective factors for teacher attrition and intent to leave the workforce in the Australian context" (2026), *Teachers and Teaching*, DOI 10.1080/13540602.2026.2630394.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* PRISMA systematic review, **29 quality-appraised studies, 2014–2024**, organised on a consolidated Bronfenbrenner ecological-systems framework. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* Australia.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the review's principal finding is negative — "existing research lacks clarity on why teachers choose to stay or leave their roles in Australia". Risk and protective factors are distributed across micro (classroom, teacher history), meso (school, leadership, colleagues) and macro (policy, status) levels, with no single dominant driver identifiable from the Australian evidence as it stands.
- *Limitation:* review-level; heterogeneous primary designs; intention and behaviour outcomes are pooled.
- *Use:* cite as evidence that a whole OECD country's literature is not yet in a state to support causal policy claims.

**2.8 Räsänen, K., J. Pietarinen, K. Pyhältö, T. Soini and P. Väisänen (2020), "Why leave the teaching profession? A longitudinal approach to the prevalence and persistence of teacher turnover intentions", *Social Psychology of Education*, Vol. 23/4, pp. 837–859.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **longitudinal** survey with a five-year follow-up; mixed-method coding of open responses into five categories. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* Finland, comprehensive-school teachers; **T1 (2010) n = 2,310; T2 (2016) n = 1,450**.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - **Half of teachers (49.5%, n = 707) reported turnover intentions** — 25.5% momentarily, 24.1% persistently. In the whole samples, 39% (2010) and 35% (2016) reported turnover intentions, against a Finnish OECD-reported baseline of 23–25%.
  - Intentions were **highly persistent**: 62% of those with intentions at T1 still had them at T2.
  - **The reasons, however, were not persistent: only 40% of teachers named the same factor at both time points** (Kappa 0.24). Persistence of reason was greatest for "lack of commitment" (15%).
  - Category shares: **the school system** (top-down reform, administrative accretion, low appreciation, low salary, resource cuts, class size, temporary contracts) 21.7% at T1 and 24.6% at T2; **multiple cumulative factors** 14.1% and 16.9%; **challenges of interaction** (with leaders, colleagues, students, parents) 16.3% falling to 10.5%; **workload** and **lack of professional commitment** the remaining categories, with lack of commitment the largest single reason at T1.
  - **Gendered pattern:** men named lack of commitment far more often (39.3% vs 21.9% of women at T1; 37.5% vs 25% at T2); women named workload (26% vs 9%) and interaction difficulties (18.4% vs 7.1%) far more often.
  - Teachers who named **lack of commitment** at T1 were the most likely to be non-respondents at T2 (32.8% attrition from the panel), which the authors read as a hint that personal-level reasons are the ones that convert intention into behaviour.
- *Limitations the authors state:* Finnish institutional distinctiveness (notably high teacher autonomy) limits generalisation; the sample **over-represents women and teachers under 40** at both waves; no administrative link to actual exit.
- *Why it matters:* the "reasons change while the intention persists" result is a direct challenge to any policy that treats a survey-stated reason as the target to be fixed.

**2.9 Perryman, J. and G. Calvert (2020), "What motivates people to teach, and why do they leave? Accountability, performativity and teacher retention", *British Journal of Educational Studies*, Vol. 68/1, pp. 2–23.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* survey of a defined graduate cohort with substantial open-text analysis. `[ASSOC]`/`[QUAL]`
- *Sample and country:* England; teacher-education graduates of the UCL Institute of Education over the preceding five years.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **workload was the most frequently cited reason both for having left and for anticipating leaving** — but the decisive factor was the **nature rather than the quantity** of the work: the accountability- and performativity-driven components (data entry, evidencing, inspection preparation, monitoring). Respondents reported having been *aware* of workload before entry, yet found the lived reality worse than expected. Original motivations were overwhelmingly intrinsic and altruistic; the gap between those motivations and performative demands is the paper's explanation of exit.
- *Limitation:* single-institution cohort, self-selected respondents, England-specific accountability regime, no administrative exit outcome.

**2.10 Perryman, J., A. Bradbury, G. Calvert and K. Kilian (2025), "'A Tipping Point' in Teacher Retention and Accountability: The Case of Inspection", *British Journal of Educational Studies*, Vol. 73/2, pp. 181–200.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* large-scale survey within the "Beyond Ofsted" (2023) project. `[ASSOC]` — stated attitudes and intentions
- *Sample and country:* England; teachers and school leaders.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **76% of respondents thought Ofsted had a negative effect on retention, and 30% had considered leaving as a result of their most recent inspection.**
- *Limitation:* self-selected respondents in a project with a stated reform purpose; the outcome is *considering* leaving, not leaving. Pair with Sims (2016/2017) for the behavioural estimate (§3.4).

## 3. Working conditions and job design

**State of the evidence.** Rich, and mostly associational. The single best-identified body of work in this section concerns **accountability and inspection**, where there is one credible quasi-experimental estimate on behaviour and a large international correlational literature on stress. Workload is the most-cited driver in every survey in every country, and yet is the factor for which the linked survey-plus-administrative evidence is weakest and most inconsistent — the England TALIS-linked study cannot confirm a robust workload–attrition relationship at all. Class size and teaching assistants have effectively **no** demonstrated retention effect. Autonomy and part-time availability are policy-tractable and evidentially under-served.

**3.1 Sims, S. and J. Jerrim (2020), *TALIS 2018: Teacher Working Conditions, Turnover and Attrition*, Statistical Working Paper, Department for Education, London (March 2020).** **[NEW — this is the highest-value single working-conditions study for our purposes]**
- *Design:* **survey data linked at individual level to administrative outcomes** — TALIS 2018 responses matched to the School Workforce Census, so the outcomes are *observed* turnover (leaving the school) and *observed* attrition (leaving the profession) in the following academic year, not self-report. Five working-conditions factors entered jointly, controlling for the other four, phase, gender, experience, pupil deprivation and indicators of outside earnings potential; results split for novice (<5 years) and experienced (>5 years) teachers; within-school and within-department robustness checks. `[ASSOC]`, but unusually well-specified
- *Sample and country:* England; TALIS 2018 primary and lower-secondary teachers consenting to data linkage.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - **Leadership/Management** (supportive culture, recognition for good work, participation in decision-making, autonomy to do the job): a one-standard-deviation increase is associated with a **half-standard-deviation increase in job satisfaction**; for an **experienced** teacher it cuts the probability of leaving the school from **4.1% to 2.3%** and of leaving the profession from **1.0% to 0.5%**; for a **novice** teacher, from **12.5% to 8.0%** (school) and **7.1% to 5.2%** (profession).
  - **Discipline** (consistent enforcement of behaviour standards, ability to control disruption, absence of verbal or physical abuse): a one-SD increase cuts an experienced teacher's probability of leaving the school from **3.9% to 3.0%** and of leaving the profession from **1.0% to 0.5%**; for a novice, **12.2% to 9.0%** and **7.0% to 5.4%**. Discipline is **not** robustly associated with job satisfaction — it affects exit without passing through satisfaction.
  - **Workload:** "shows an inconsistent relationship with attrition — some models and ways of measuring workload suggest there is a relationship and others do not. This research is therefore **inconclusive** on this point." Workload shows **no robust relationship with turnover or with job satisfaction**. In one specification, hours worked is associated with a **33% reduction in the odds of attrition** — the opposite of the expected sign — while being unrelated to turnover or satisfaction.
  - **Collaboration** and **Preparation** show no robust relationship with any of the three outcomes.
  - **Job satisfaction** is strongly predictive of behaviour: a one-SD increase is associated with a **40% reduction in the odds of turnover and a 57% reduction in the odds of attrition**, consistent with satisfaction mediating between leadership and exit.
- *Limitations the authors state:* the identification relies on the models containing the full set of relevant variables; working conditions are "subtle and multifaceted constructs... likely measured with some degree of error". They note the main findings survive within-school and within-department comparison and adjustment for outside earnings potential.
- *Why it matters:* it is the cleanest demonstration in any system that **leadership and pupil behaviour predict observed exit, while self-reported workload does not robustly do so** — a direct challenge to the survey-based consensus.

**3.2 Sims, S. and J. Jerrim (2017), *TALIS 2013: Working Conditions, Teacher Job Satisfaction and Retention*, Statistical Working Paper, Department for Education, London.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* cross-national multilevel regression. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* over **50,000 teachers in 34 countries**, TALIS 2013; outcome is job satisfaction and *desire to move school*.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* job satisfaction among teachers in England is **below** that of comparable TALIS nations. More cooperation between teachers and more effective professional development are associated with higher job satisfaction. Notably, **a school's inspection grade and the disadvantage of its intake are *not* negatively associated with either job satisfaction or turnover intentions once working conditions are controlled for** — the interpersonal aspects (leadership, cooperation) carry the association.
- *Limitation:* cross-sectional, self-reported intention outcome, no administrative linkage (unlike its 2020 successor).

**3.3 Jerrim, J. and S. Sims (2022), "School accountability and teacher stress: international evidence from the OECD TALIS study", *Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability*, Vol. 34, pp. 5–32.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* cross-national multilevel analysis with within-school comparisons. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* **more than 100,000 teachers in over 40 countries**, TALIS 2018. Accountability measured from headteacher reports (use of assessment data, public availability of results/league tables, existence of an inspectorate).
- *Finding, precisely stated:* a **modest positive correlation** between the strength of a school system's accountability arrangements and how stressed teachers and headteachers are about that aspect of the job. Within schools, there is **little evidence** that headteachers' own accountability stress changes their management practices or is transmitted to staff. There is, however, **strong evidence of "emotional contagion" of accountability stress among colleagues** within a school.
- *Limitation:* correlational and cross-sectional; accountability is measured at system and school level with coarse indicators; no exit outcome.

**3.4 Sims, S. (2016/2017), "High-stakes accountability and teacher turnover: how do different school inspection judgements affect teachers' decisions to leave their school?", QSS/UCL working paper (Department of Quantitative Social Science WP 16-14).** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **difference-in-differences** on administrative panel data. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Sample and country:* England, School Workforce Census linked to Ofsted inspection outcomes.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* an **"Inadequate" judgement raises teacher turnover by 3.4 percentage points** — roughly **25% of average turnover** across all school-year observations. An **"Outstanding" judgement produces no change** in turnover. The paper notes the theoretical ambiguity: a failing judgement may harm self-efficacy and push teachers out, but it also sends a negative signal about the teachers in that school and so reduces their outside options.
- *Limitation:* estimates turnover (leaving the school), not attrition (leaving the profession); working-paper status; the effect is identified off the lowest grade only.
- *Pairing:* use with Perryman et al. (2025) — the survey says 30% considered leaving after inspection; the administrative estimate says 3.4 percentage points actually moved. This is a clean illustration of the intention/behaviour gap (§10).

**3.5 Jerrim, J. and S. Sims (2021/2022 workload strand), including "New evidence on teachers' working hours in England" (CEPEO Working Paper 20-02) and "When is high workload bad for teacher wellbeing? Accounting for the non-linear contribution of specific teaching tasks", *Teaching and Teacher Education* (2021).** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* repeated cross-sections (TALIS 2013 and 2018, national workforce surveys) and non-linear regression on task-level time use. `[ASSOC]`/`[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Sample and country:* England, with international TALIS comparison.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* teachers in England work **longer hours than teachers in almost every other TALIS country** at both primary and lower-secondary level. Despite a decade of workload-reduction policy, there is "little sign of any substantial reduction": **marking 6.3 hours per week in both 2013 and 2018; administration 4.2 hours in 2013 and 4.0 in 2018**. The task-level analysis finds that **the association between hours and wellbeing is non-linear and task-specific** — marking in particular is identified as the key driver of work-related stress, rather than total hours.
- *Limitation:* self-reported time use; cross-sectional; no exit outcome.
- *Bridge to 3.1:* this is why "workload" and "attrition" can fail to correlate while "workload" and "misery" correlate strongly — it is composition, not volume.

**3.6 Department for Education (England), *Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders* survey series, waves 1–4 (2022–2025).** *(Already flagged in `lit_why_leave.md`; retained here for the workload series.)*
- *Design:* large repeated national survey. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Finding:* average teacher working hours **48.1 per week in 2024**, down from 49.4 in 2023, with full-time teachers still above 50; the share of teachers and leaders reporting workload "not acceptable" and outside their control fell from **57% (2022) to 49% (2023) to 43% (2024)**.
- *Limitation:* self-report; wave numbering should be verified against the GOV.UK release before citation.

**3.7 Worth, J. and J. Van den Brande (2020), *Teacher Autonomy: How Does It Relate to Job Satisfaction and Retention?*, NFER, Slough.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* large-scale quantitative analysis of national survey data — the first of its kind for England on autonomy. `[ASSOC]`; outcome is **intention** to stay
- *Sample and country:* England, teachers in state-funded schools.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* teachers report high perceived influence over classroom activities (teaching methods, lesson planning) and **low** influence over curriculum, assessment, and their own professional development goals. **Perceived influence over professional-development goal-setting is the autonomy dimension most strongly associated with job satisfaction and intention to stay**: around **half of those with the lowest autonomy** said they intended to stay in teaching in the short term, compared with **more than 85% of those with the highest autonomy**.
- *Limitation:* cross-sectional; intention outcome; autonomy is self-perceived and may be endogenous to satisfaction.

**3.8 Sharp, C., R. Smith, J. Worth et al. (2019), *Part-time Teaching and Flexible Working in Secondary Schools*, NFER, Slough.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* mixed-methods — national survey plus case studies of schools. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`/`[QUAL]`
- *Sample and country:* England, secondary schools.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **one in six secondary teachers would like to reduce their hours, and around one in twelve would like to reduce by more than a day a week.** Part-time and flexible working is strongly shaped by whether senior leaders take a proactive approach. Benefits reported: improved wellbeing, work–life balance and morale, retention of experienced teachers, greater staff diversity. Barriers reported by leaders: continuity for pupils and timetabling; the narrowness of flexible options beyond simple part-time; communication; additional cost.
- *Limitation:* no counterfactual; the retention claim is a reported perception, not an estimated effect.
- *Link:* NFER's *Teacher Labour Market in England* annual reports (2024, 2025, 2026) sustain the claim that **limited access to flexible working may be contributing to exit**, alongside the finding that leaving rates have not meaningfully improved since the pandemic while ITT recruitment remains poor.

**3.9 Class size, teaching assistants and pupil behaviour.**
- *Evidence:* Nguyen et al. (2020) find **no evidence that reducing class size reduces attrition** and no evidence that adding teacher aides does, in the odds-ratio models (§2.1). By contrast, **pupil behaviour is one of only two working-conditions factors robustly predicting observed exit** in Sims and Jerrim (2020) (§3.1), and OECD (2025) TALIS 2024 finds that stress from student intimidation/verbal abuse and from maintaining classroom discipline is **more closely associated with intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures** are.
- *Reading:* the physical resourcing lever (smaller classes, more adults) has no demonstrated retention return; the behavioural climate lever does. This is a substantively important and under-reported asymmetry.

## 4. Leadership, collegiality and school climate

**State of the evidence.** The most consistent finding in the whole field, replicated across the United States, England and international meta-analysis, and visible in both intention and behavioural outcomes. It is also the least well-identified: there is no credible experimental or quasi-experimental estimate of the effect of *changing* school leadership on teacher retention. The literature is effectively unanimous about the correlation and silent about the intervention.

---

**4.1 Boyd, D., P. Grossman, M. Ing, H. Lankford, S. Loeb and J. Wyckoff (2011), "The Influence of School Administrators on Teacher Retention Decisions", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 48/2, pp. 303–333.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* administrative panel data on teacher transitions linked to a large teacher survey, plus a separate survey of recent leavers. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* New York City public schools.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* among all measured school contextual factors, **teachers' perceptions of the school administration have by far the greatest influence on retention decisions**. The result is consistent for first-year teachers and for the full sample, and is corroborated by the independent survey of teachers who had recently left.
- *Limitation:* perceptions are self-reported and may be rationalisations of a decision already taken; no exogenous variation in leadership.

**4.2 Ladd, H. F. (2011), "Teachers' Perceptions of Their Working Conditions: How Predictive of Planned and Actual Teacher Movement?", *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, Vol. 33/2, pp. 235–261.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* statewide teacher survey linked to administrative records; both **planned** and **actual** one-year departure modelled. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* North Carolina; 2006 statewide survey of all teachers linked to state administrative data.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* working conditions are **highly predictive of intended movement**, independent of other school characteristics including racial composition of the student body. **School leadership, broadly defined, is the most salient dimension.** Perceptions are **less predictive of one-year *actual* departure than of intended departure**, but their predictive power for actual departure is still on a par with that of other school characteristics.
- *Limitation the paper states:* the gap between the predictive power for intentions and for behaviour is explicit in the design — this is one of the earliest clean demonstrations of the intention/behaviour wedge (see §10).

**4.3 Kraft, M. A., W. H. Marinell and D. Shen-Wei Yee (2016), "School Organizational Contexts, Teacher Turnover, and Student Achievement: Evidence From Panel Data", *American Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 53/5, pp. 1411–1449.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **school-level panel with school fixed effects**, using repeated annual survey waves to identify *changes* in climate within the same school over time. `[ASSOC]`, but with within-school identification stronger than cross-sectional work
- *Sample and country:* New York City middle schools; annual NYC School Survey linked to administrative turnover and achievement data.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **improvements in school leadership, academic expectations, teacher relationships and school safety are each independently associated with reductions in teacher turnover.** Improvements in school safety and academic expectations are additionally associated with student achievement gains.
- *Limitation:* survey measures are teacher-reported and could co-move with unobserved shocks; the design cannot rule out reverse causation from turnover to reported climate.

**4.4 Administrative support in meta-analysis.** Nguyen et al. (2020): stronger administrative support, **OR 0.80** across 13 studies (§2.1). Gundlach et al. (2024): higher principal effectiveness and regular supportive communication associated with lower attrition (§2.2). Sims and Jerrim (2020): the Leadership/Management factor is the strongest predictor of both observed turnover and observed attrition in England (§3.1). Boyd et al. (2011) and Ladd (2011) in the US. Note the **one dissenting null**: Nguyen et al. find directly measured "leadership" and "collaboration" items *not* significant — but on few studies and with imprecise estimates.

## 5. Pay and outside options

**State of the evidence.** This is the **best-identified** part of the literature and simultaneously the part whose policy implication is most contested. Multiple credible quasi-experiments establish that **targeted pay changes move retention**, with elasticities that are real but modest, and effects that often decay when the payment stops. The outside-option literature establishes that most leavers do **not** move to better-paid work, which is hard to reconcile with a simple wage story. And the discrete-choice literature (see §9) shows that in hypothetical choices pay dominates — the gap between what money does in choice experiments and what money does in the field is one of the field's central puzzles.

---

**5.1 Hanushek, E. A., J. F. Kain and S. G. Rivkin (2004), "Why Public Schools Lose Teachers", *Journal of Human Resources*, Vol. 39/2, pp. 326–354.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* large administrative panel; hazard/transition models for switching school and exiting public schooling. `[ASSOC]` with careful attention to compensating differentials
- *Sample and country:* Texas, full state administrative teacher records.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **teacher mobility is much more strongly related to characteristics of the students — particularly race and achievement — than to salary**, although salary exerts a **modest** effect once compensating differentials are taken into account. Schools in urban areas serving economically disadvantaged and minority students are particularly vulnerable.
- *Limitation:* student characteristics are correlated with unmeasured working conditions, so "students" here may be a proxy for behaviour, safety and leadership rather than a preference over pupils as such.

**5.2 Clotfelter, C., E. Glennie, H. Ladd and J. Vigdor (2008), "Would higher salaries keep teachers in high-poverty schools? Evidence from a policy intervention in North Carolina", *Journal of Public Economics*, Vol. 92/5–6, pp. 1352–1370.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* hazard models exploiting a three-year policy, comparing before/after, eligible/ineligible teacher categories, and eligible/barely-ineligible schools. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Sample and country:* North Carolina; **USD 1,800 annual bonus** for certified maths, science and special-education teachers in high-poverty or low-performing secondary schools, from 2001.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the bonus **lowered turnover rates for targeted teachers by about 17%**, with the strongest response among experienced teachers.
- *Limitation the paper states:* implementation was compromised by many eligible teachers not knowing they qualified, which if anything biases the estimate towards zero relative to a well-communicated scheme.

**5.3 Feng, L. and T. R. Sass (2018), "The Impact of Incentives to Recruit and Retain Teachers in 'Hard-to-Staff' Subjects", *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*, Vol. 37/1, pp. 112–135.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* difference-in-differences and **triple-difference** on state administrative data; Florida Critical Teacher Shortage Program. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **loan forgiveness cut mean attrition by 10.4% for middle- and high-school maths and science teachers and by 8.9%** respectively; **12.3% for special education when fully funded, and no significant effect once funding was substantially reduced**. A one-time bonus reduced the likelihood of exit by **as much as 32% in the short run**. Both components pass a back-of-envelope cost–benefit test.
- *Limitation:* the dose-dependence result — no effect when underfunded — is itself the key policy caveat.

**5.4 Falch, T. (2011), "Teacher Mobility Responses to Wage Changes: Evidence from a Quasi-natural Experiment", *American Economic Review*, Vol. 101/3, pp. 460–465.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* fixed-effects exploitation of a centrally set, school-status-contingent wage premium, with schools switching status over the period. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Sample and country:* Norway, 1993/94 to 2002/03; schools with a history of vacancies received a wage premium of about **10%**.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the wage premium **reduces the probability of a voluntary quit by 6 percentage points**, implying a **short-run labour supply elasticity of about 1.25**.
- *Limitation:* the premium is assigned on past vacancy history, so identification rests on the timing of status switches; the setting is a fully centralised wage system, which may not transfer to systems with local pay discretion.
- *Value:* the single best-identified non-US, non-Anglophone estimate of the pay–retention relationship.

**5.5 Sims, S. and A. Benhenda (2022), *The Effect of Financial Incentives on the Retention of Shortage-Subject Teachers*, CEPEO Working Paper 22-04, UCL.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* quasi-experiment on eligibility for early-career retention payments. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Finding:* payments of **GBP 2,000 per year after tax (about 8% of gross salary)** made eligible maths and physics teachers **23% less likely to leave** state-funded teaching in eligible years.

**5.6 Chingos, M. M. and M. R. West (2012), "Do More Effective Teachers Earn More Outside the Classroom?", *Education Finance and Policy*, Vol. 7/1, pp. 8–43.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* administrative teacher records linked to state **unemployment-insurance earnings records** for the same individuals after they leave. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* Florida; **more than 130,000 classroom teachers**, 2001–02 to 2006–07, of whom roughly **35,000 left the classroom**.
- *Findings, precisely stated:* **a majority of those leaving the classroom remained employed by public school districts** (i.e. within education). Among grades 4–8 teachers who left for **other industries**, a **1 standard deviation higher value-added in maths and reading is associated with 6–8% higher earnings outside teaching** — and the effectiveness–earnings gradient is **steeper outside teaching than inside it**, implying that flat salary schedules under-price effective teachers relative to their outside options.
- *Limitation:* earnings observed only for in-state, UI-covered employment; value-added is estimated with error; the estimate is a correlation between effectiveness and outside earnings, not the causal return to teaching skill.
- *Pairing:* Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner (2006) find that in Georgia "very few of those who leave teaching take jobs that pay more than their salary as teachers". The two results are compatible: the *average* leaver does not gain financially; the *effective* leaver who goes to another industry does.

**5.7 Corcoran, S. P., W. N. Evans and R. M. Schwab (2004), "Changing Labor-Market Opportunities for Women and the Quality of Teachers, 1957–2000", *American Economic Review*, Vol. 94/2, pp. 230–235.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* long-run repeated cross-sections of cohort test-score distributions matched to occupational destination. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`/`[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* United States, cohorts 1957–2000.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the share of **high-achieving women** among public school teachers **declined substantially during the 1970s** as outside professional options for women opened up. The relative academic quality of the teaching workforce is thus partly a function of the outside option facing its historically dominant recruitment pool, not of education policy.
- *Limitation:* descriptive decomposition; cannot attribute the shift to any specific policy or to teaching's own conditions.
- *Relevance:* the foundational statement of the **opportunity-cost** account, and a caution against reading changes in who teaches as evidence about teaching itself.

**5.8 See, Morris et al. (2020) on money — see §2.5.** The systematic review's causal-weighted verdict: money recruits, money does not durably retain, and the effect lasts only while the money does. This is the counterweight to 5.2–5.5 and should be cited alongside them.

**5.9 Kraft, M. A. and M. A. Lyon (2024), "The Rise and Fall of the Teaching Profession: Prestige, Interest, Preparation, and Satisfaction over the Last Half Century", NBER Working Paper 32386; published in *American Educational Research Journal* (2024).** **[NEW to the reference list, referenced in `evidencia_s2.md`]**
- *Design:* compilation and harmonisation of nationally representative time series across four constructs. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Sample and country:* United States, roughly 1970–2022.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* a **consistent dynamic pattern across every measure** — occupational prestige, student interest in teaching, numbers preparing to enter, and on-the-job satisfaction: rapid decline in the 1970s, swift rise through the 1980s into the mid-1990s, relative stability, then **sustained decline from around 2010**. The current state of the profession is **at or near its lowest level in 50 years** on these measures.
- *Limitation the paper states:* the series are descriptive; the authors explore but do not adjudicate between competing explanations (economic conditions, sociopolitical climate, education policy, school environments).
- *Use:* the best available evidence that the *attractiveness* problem is real and long-run even where the *attrition rate* is flat (see §12 for the tension with Aldeman and Yi 2025).

## 6. Pensions and deferred compensation

**State of the evidence.** Well-identified and almost entirely American. There is a strong structural-modelling literature showing that defined-benefit pension rules generate sharp, predictable spikes in exit at specific ages, and a valuation literature showing that teachers discount deferred compensation heavily. The valuation result is genuinely disputed (see 6.4). **There is no European equivalent of this literature** — no comparable study prices the effect of pension design on teacher exit timing in any European system, which is a first-order gap for an OECD-facing paper.

---

**6.1 Costrell, R. M. and M. Podgursky (2009), "Peaks, Cliffs, and Valleys: The Peculiar Incentives in Teacher Retirement Systems and Their Consequences for School Staffing", *Education Finance and Policy*, Vol. 4/2, pp. 175–211.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* actuarial simulation of pension wealth accrual under the rules of six state systems. `[DESCRIPTIVE]` of incentives
- *Finding:* pension wealth accrual is **highly non-linear**, spiking in the mid-to-late 50s and then falling sharply or turning negative — creating a **pull-then-push** structure that concentrates exit at ages that are young by professional standards. The complementary evidence in Harris and Adams (2007) is that early retirement is precisely what distinguishes teacher turnover from that of comparable licensed professions.

**6.2 Ni, S. and M. Podgursky (2016), "How Teachers Respond to Pension System Incentives: New Estimates and Policy Applications", *Journal of Labor Economics*, Vol. 34/4, pp. 1075–1104.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **structural estimation of a Stock–Wise option-value model** of retirement on administrative panel data, validated out of sample against the observed response to 1990s pension enhancements, then used to simulate alternative plan designs. `[CAUSAL]` in the structural sense
- *Sample and country:* Missouri, statewide administrative teacher panel.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* the option-value model **fits the observed retirement hazard well and predicts well out of sample** the effects of the 1990s pension enhancements — establishing that teachers respond to pension incentives in a forward-looking, financially rational way. Simulations show that moving to alternative (less back-loaded) plan designs materially changes the timing distribution of exits.
- *Limitation:* single-state; the structural model imposes strong assumptions about discounting and expectations.

**6.3 Fitzpatrick, M. D. (2015), "How Much Are Public School Teachers Willing to Pay for Their Retirement Benefits?", *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, Vol. 7/4, pp. 165–188.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* revealed-preference estimation exploiting an opportunity for Illinois public school employees to **purchase** additional pension benefits at a stated price. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Finding, precisely stated:* employees are willing to pay on average **20 cents for a dollar increase in the present value of expected retirement benefits**; more than a quarter of Illinois teachers declined to pay 19 cents for a dollar of present-value pension enhancement. The paper concludes there is substantial inefficiency in the compensation mix and casts doubt on deferred compensation as a tool for attracting employees.

**6.4 Ni, S., M. Podgursky and F. Wang (2022), "How Much Are Public School Teachers Willing to Pay for Their Retirement Benefits? Comment", *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, Vol. 14/3, pp. 478–493.** **[NEW — this is the disagreement]**
- *Design:* re-analysis of the same Illinois cohort, tracked **to actual retirement**, permitting exact measurement of service years and the annuity received. `[CAUSAL]`
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **the vast majority of teachers did purchase the upgrade**; among those who did not, **the net benefit given their realised retirement timing was negative**, so declining was rational rather than evidence of undervaluation. The authors conclude that the complex relationship between retirement timing and pension-wealth gain "makes drawing inferences about teachers' willingness to pay for this benefit difficult".
- *Implication for our paper:* the widely cited "teachers value pensions at 20 cents on the dollar" figure is **contested in the top-field literature**. It should be presented with the Comment attached, not as a settled parameter. Our existing text in `evidencia_s2.md` and `lit_return_incentives.md` currently states the 20-cent figure without the challenge — this should be corrected.

**6.5 Fuchsman, D., J. B. McGee and G. Zamarro (2023), "Teachers' willingness to pay for retirement benefits: A national stated preferences experiment", *Economics of Education Review*, Vol. 92, 102349.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **discrete-choice stated-preference experiment** embedded in a nationally representative teacher survey. `[CAUSAL]` within the hypothetical choice setting
- *Sample and country:* United States, nationally representative.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* teachers would be **indifferent between a traditional defined-benefit pension and alternative plan designs if the alternatives came with a 2–3% salary increase** — i.e. the perceived premium on the traditional pension is small. **Experience is a significant mediator:** more experienced teachers are willing to pay considerably more to keep the traditional plan; inexperienced teachers have weak preferences over plan type.
- *Limitation:* hypothetical choices; the design cannot capture how teachers would behave facing a real plan change.
- *Reconciliation:* 6.3, 6.4 and 6.5 together suggest that teachers modestly under-value deferred compensation relative to its cost, that the extreme "20 cents" estimate is unsafe, and that the discount is much larger for the young — which is exactly the group whose retention is at issue.

**6.6 Fleck et al. (2025)** *(already in `evidencia_s2.md`)*: pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by **4–10 percentage points**. `[CAUSAL]`

**6.7 GAP.** No study located in this review prices the effect of pension design on teacher exit timing in any **European** system, despite substantial cross-national variation in whether teachers are civil servants, in early-retirement rules, and in recent reform (Germany's *Beamtenversorgung*, the Netherlands' ABP, French and Spanish reform of retirement age). This is the single largest identified evidence gap for an OECD-facing paper on this theme.

## 7. Wellbeing, stress and burnout — prevalence versus exit

**State of the evidence.** Prevalence is well measured; the link to *actual* exit is not. The burnout literature is large, internally consistent and almost entirely built on cross-sectional self-report with **intention** as the outcome (§2.3, §2.4). Where researchers have measured wellbeing objectively or followed leavers, the picture changes sharply and in ways uncomfortable for the standard narrative: teachers do not have systematically worse mental or physical health than comparable professionals, and leaving teaching does not improve general mental health.

---

**7.1 OECD (2020), *TALIS 2018 Results (Volume II)*, and OECD (2025), *Results from TALIS 2024*, "Sustaining the teaching profession".** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* internationally comparative cross-sectional survey. `[ASSOC]`/`[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Finding:* **18% (2018) / 19% (2024)** of lower-secondary teachers experience "a lot" of stress at work, with wide cross-country variation; such teachers are **about twice as likely** to say they intend to stop teaching within five years. On average across TALIS systems, **about 25%** want to leave teaching within five years. In 2024, stress from **student intimidation/verbal abuse and from maintaining discipline is more closely associated with intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures**.

**7.2 Jerrim, J., S. Sims, H. Taylor and R. Allen (2020), "How does the mental health and wellbeing of teachers compare to other professions? Evidence from eleven survey datasets", *Review of Education*, Vol. 8/3, pp. 659–689.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* harmonised comparative analysis across **eleven** independent social surveys. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* United Kingdom.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **little evidence that teachers have worse mental health and wellbeing outcomes than other occupational groups.** The authors conclude that research should shift from asking whether teachers are disproportionately affected to strengthening the evidence on the drivers of mental ill-health within the profession.
- *Limitation:* occupational comparison groups differ in composition; some surveys have small teacher subsamples.

**7.3 Sims, S., J. Jerrim, H. Taylor and R. Allen (2022), "Is teaching bad for your health? New evidence from biomarker data", *Oxford Review of Education*, Vol. 48/1, pp. 78–97.** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* comparison of teachers with other occupations using **objective biomarker data**, in a representative cross-section and a longitudinal convenience sample. `[ASSOC]`, but with an objective outcome
- *Sample and country:* United Kingdom (Understanding Society; UK Biobank).
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **no statistically significant overall association between teaching and physical health in any model or dataset.**
- *Limitation the authors state:* biomarkers capture physical, not mental, health; the longitudinal sample is a convenience sample.

**7.4 Jerrim, J., S. Sims and H. Taylor (2021), "I quit! Is there an association between leaving teaching and improvements in mental health?", *British Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 47/5.** **[NEW — the key study for the wellbeing-to-exit link]**
- *Design:* longitudinal within-person comparison of individuals before and after leaving teaching. `[ASSOC]` with within-person identification
- *Sample and country:* United Kingdom; individuals aged 40–65 in **UK Biobank**.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* people who leave teaching are **somewhat happier in their work** afterwards, but **do not generally experience any improvement in general wellbeing or mental health.**
- *Limitation:* Biobank is not representative (healthy-volunteer bias); the age range excludes the early-career leavers who dominate attrition; leaving is not randomly assigned.
- *Why it matters:* the standard policy syllogism runs "teaching damages wellbeing → teachers leave to protect their wellbeing → improving wellbeing will retain them". 7.2, 7.3 and 7.4 together break the first and second links of that chain, at least for mid- and late-career teachers in the UK. Job-specific satisfaction improves; life does not.

**7.5 Madigan and Kim (2021) and Li and Yao (2022) — see §2.3 and §2.4.** Both establish burnout as the dominant predictor of quit **intention**; both explicitly or implicitly concede that **actual attrition is almost never measured** in this literature. Madigan and Kim state that their search "found very few studies that had measured actual attrition".

**7.6 Prevalence versus exit — the bottom line.** No study located in this review estimates the effect of a change in measured burnout on subsequent observed exit, with credible identification, at scale. The best available approximation is Sims and Jerrim (2020) (§3.1), where **job satisfaction** — a related but distinct construct — is associated with a **57% reduction in the odds of observed attrition** per standard deviation. Cite that rather than burnout–intention correlations when the claim concerns behaviour.

## 8. Life-course and demographic factors

**State of the evidence.** The historically dominant account — that teacher exit, especially female teacher exit, is driven by family formation and departure from the labour force — rests on one very good study of a 1972 US high-school cohort. Everything about the setting has since changed: female labour-force participation, parental leave, childcare, and the destinations of leavers. The modern evidence base has **not** replaced it. This is a genuine and consequential hole: an OECD paper on re-entry needs to know whether today's leavers are on a career break or have exited permanently, and the honest answer is that no recent, well-identified, cross-national study establishes this.

---

**8.1 Stinebrickner, T. R. (2002), "An Analysis of Occupational Change and Departure from the Labor Force: Evidence of the Reasons that Teachers Leave", *Journal of Human Resources*, Vol. 37/1, pp. 192–216.** *(In the base.)*
- *Design:* structural hazard model. `[CAUSAL]` in the structural sense
- *Sample:* certified female teachers in the US NLS-72 cohort, followed 1973–1986.
- *Finding:* **about 67% of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely** rather than switching occupations; **the arrival of a newborn child is the dominant exit factor**, ahead of marriage or pay; many leavers later return; exit behaviour resembles that of comparably educated non-teachers.
- *Limitation, as flagged in our own base:* a 1972 high-school cohort of women only. Treat the 67% as historical.

**8.2 Guarino, Santibañez and Daley (2006) — the U-shaped age profile.** High attrition in the first five years, a mid-career trough, a late-career rise (§1.2). Confirmed in structure by Nguyen et al. (2020): teachers over 28 have **30% lower odds** of leaving their school; new teachers have **54% higher odds** of attrition than veterans (§2.1).

**8.3 Gender — a reversal in the literature.** Borman and Dowling (2008) reported women as more likely to leave. **Nguyen et al. (2020), across 37 studies, find no gender difference**, and explicitly consider that "women were more likely to leave historically, but this may have changed in recent years" (§2.1, §12). UNESCO (2024) reports the opposite pattern in some low- and middle-income settings (e.g. India 2022: female primary attrition 2.0% vs male 1.4%), while globally male primary-teacher attrition in 2021 was 9.2% against 4.2% for women. Räsänen et al. (2020) find a strong **gendered difference in stated reasons** rather than in prevalence: Finnish men cite lack of commitment, women cite workload and interpersonal strain (§2.8).

**8.4 Career stage and role compatibility — the Australian distinction.** *"What really influences teacher attrition, migration, and retention?"* (2025), *The Australian Educational Researcher*, DOI 10.1007/s13384-025-00842-4. **[NEW]**
- *Design:* survey of **930 current and former K–12 teachers**, Victoria, Australia; explicit separation of migration, attrition and retention. `[ASSOC]`
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **migration** between schools is driven by career factors, the search for advancement, and dissatisfaction with school culture. **Attrition** from the profession is driven by **personal life stages and role compatibility**. **Retention** is supported by personal choice, job compatibility, and positive relationships with students and colleagues. The authors argue that failure to distinguish attrition from migration has hindered the identification of tailored retention strategies.
- *Limitation:* single state, self-selected respondents, retrospective reasons.

**8.5 Ageing and the bimodal workforce.** Ingersoll et al. (2021) document simultaneous "greying" and "greening" in the US (§1.6). OECD *Education at a Glance 2025* (§11.1) shows retirements accounting for more than half of teacher departures in Argentina (primary), France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel — while in Denmark, England and Estonia **over 80% of leavers resigned rather than retired**. The life-course composition of exit is therefore itself a cross-country variable, not a constant.

**8.6 GAP.** No recent large-scale, administrative-data study located here estimates the effect of **childbirth or care responsibilities** on teacher exit and return in a contemporary OECD system, in the way the child-penalty literature has done for the wider labour market. The closest is Stinebrickner (2002) on a 1970s cohort. For a paper whose subject is re-entry, this matters directly: career-break leavers and permanent leavers are different policy targets and we cannot currently size them.

## 9. Stated versus revealed preference

**State of the evidence.** Newly strong, and the most important recent methodological development in the field. Discrete-choice experiments now allow the trade-offs teachers make to be priced, and their results are strikingly different from what teachers say in open-response surveys: in choice experiments pay is powerful, whereas in open-response surveys money "rarely features highly" (See et al. 2020). Meanwhile the field-based revealed-preference evidence — what leavers actually earn afterwards — shows most of them taking a pay cut. Any policy inference must specify which of these three sources it is relying on.

---

**9.1 Sims, S., H. Lowes-Belk and C. Routledge (2026), "Why do people choose to enter and exit the teaching profession? An interdisciplinary quantitative synthesis", *British Educational Research Journal*, Vol. 52/3, pp. 1652–1676.** *(In the base; fully characterised here for the first time.)*
- *Design:* systematic synthesis of **job-choice survey experiments** with random assignment of job attributes, organised on an adapted Cassar–Meier framework (income / meaning / costs); eight inclusion criteria including random assignment and OECD-country setting. `[CAUSAL]` within the experimental settings
- *Sample and country:* **12 studies, 8 countries** (United States, Spain, Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland, Chile, Norway, Costa Rica); six studies of serving teachers, six of general adult populations; sample sizes 90 to 4,358, median 1,108.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - **Income:** a **10% increase in pay raises the probability of choosing a job by 5–12 percentage points**, consistently for teachers and non-teachers. Teachers value **current compensation roughly five times more than retirement benefits**.
  - **Meaning:** jobs offering choice over how tasks are executed are chosen **6 percentage points** more often; provision of training raises selection by **2–56 percentage points** across studies; a **collegial atmosphere raises the probability of choosing a job by 33 percentage points** — the single largest attribute effect in the synthesis.
  - **Costs:** a **20% reduction in hours raises the probability of choosing a job by 3–7 percentage points**; remote-work flexibility is consistently positive; **ten additional days of paid leave is worth about a 7% pay rise**.
- *Limitations the authors state:* the model may omit work intensity, job security and occupational prestige; hypothetical job-choice experiments may not predict real behaviour; mastery and relatedness were combined, preventing clean separation; there is little evidence outside survey settings on the real effects of flexible working and professional development on recruitment and retention.

**9.2 Johnston, A. C. (2024), "Preferences, Selection, and the Structure of Teacher Pay", *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* (NBER WP 33088; earlier SSRN versions 2021).** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **discrete-choice experiment** eliciting willingness-to-pay for compensation structure, working conditions and contract features, embedded in a real district consultation with weak union presence so respondents had a genuine incentive to reveal preferences. `[CAUSAL]` within the experimental setting
- *Sample and country:* Aldine Independent School District, Houston, Texas — a large urban district.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* teachers' willingness to pay is estimated for salary structure, retirement benefits, class size, performance pay and time-to-tenure. **Schools appear to underpay in salary and in performance pay while overpaying in retirement.** **High-performing teachers have stronger preferences for schools offering performance pay**, implying performance pay would generate positive selection. The paper concludes that restructuring compensation at constant cost could raise both teacher welfare and student achievement.
- *Limitation:* single district; stated choices; the selection result is inferred from preference heterogeneity, not observed sorting.

**9.3 Somers, M. A., T. Stolp, F. Burato, W. N. J. Groot, F. van Merode and M. Vooren (2024/2026), "Increasing the working hours of nurses and teachers: Evidence from a discrete choice experiment", ROA Research Memorandum 2024/5, Maastricht University; published in *PLOS ONE* (2026).** **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **discrete-choice experiment**, nine choice sets of two hypothetical jobs differing on seven attributes. `[CAUSAL]` within the experimental setting
- *Sample and country:* the Netherlands; nurses and teachers.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - **High work pressure is the most negatively valued attribute for both occupations.** Teachers require a **net hourly wage increase of EUR 4.06** to accept high work pressure (nurses EUR 3.26).
  - **Part-time teachers require a 23% increase in net hourly wages to accept a full-time position** (part-time nurses 21%).
  - Teachers place significant weight on **social support from colleagues and managers**; control over working hours is highly valued.
- *Limitation:* hypothetical; Dutch institutional setting with unusually high part-time prevalence.
- *Policy relevance:* directly prices the "just get part-timers to work full time" proposal — and finds it expensive.

**9.4 Revealed preference in the field.**
- **England:** Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015), NFER — leavers experience on average a **~10% fall in wages** relative to comparable stayers, buying shorter hours and higher job satisfaction; more than half of leavers (excluding retirees) take jobs in the education sector. *(In the base.)*
- **United States:** Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner (2006) — "very few of those who leave teaching take jobs that pay more than their salary as teachers" (Georgia administrative data). Chingos and West (2012) — a majority of Florida classroom-leavers stay employed by school districts; only the most effective, and only those leaving for other industries, earn more (§5.6).
- **Reading:** the median leaver is not being poached. The revealed-preference evidence says leavers are **buying conditions with money**, which is the mirror image of what the choice experiments say they would buy with money.

**9.5 The stated/revealed gap, stated plainly.** In open-response surveys of leavers, pay is rarely the top reason (See et al. 2020, reviewing this literature; Brandenburg et al. 2024 for Australia; Perryman and Calvert 2020 for England; Räsänen et al. 2020 for Finland). In **choice experiments**, pay is one of the largest single attributes (Sims et al. 2026; Johnston 2024). In **field quasi-experiments**, pay moves retention by real but bounded amounts (Falch 2011; Clotfelter et al. 2008; Feng and Sass 2018) and the effect fades when the money stops (See et al. 2020). The three are reconcilable if pay is a **strong marginal-choice attribute but a weak narrative reason** — people do not say "I left for money" but they do respond to money. Our paper should say this explicitly rather than choosing a side.

## 10. Intention versus behaviour

**State of the evidence.** Settled empirically, widely ignored in practice. There is now a direct, large-sample estimate of the wedge, and it is large. Any figure of the form "X% of teachers say they will leave" should be discounted by roughly a factor of three to four before being read as a forecast.

---

**10.1 Nguyen, T. D., E. Bettini, C. Redding and A. F. Gilmour (2025), "Comparing Teacher Turnover Intentions to Actual Turnover: Cautions and Lessons for the Field", *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, Vol. 47/2 (online 2024).** *(Cited in the base; fully quantified here for the first time.)*
- *Design:* descriptive and regression analysis linking survey-measured intentions to observed turnover one year later; three waves pooled. `[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* United States; SASS 2003–04, 2007–08 and 2011–12 linked to the Teacher Follow-up Survey; **analytic sample 102,970 public school teachers**, nationally representative with sampling weights.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - Teachers who said they intended to leave **as soon as possible** left at **32.5%**; those who did not, at **6.8%** — a **26 percentage point gap**, "a nearly 400% increase in the likelihood of actually leaving".
  - **But two-thirds of those who intended to leave were still teaching the following year**, and **7% of those who did not intend to leave left anyway** — a rate comparable to the overall annual leaving rate of 7.16%.
  - Unweighted: for every 100 teachers who said they would leave as soon as possible, **44 left and 56 were still teaching**.
  - The **correlation between intent-to-leave and leaving is only r = 0.15**; between thinking-about-transferring and moving schools, r = 0.17.
  - **Intent-to-stay** performs worse still: those intending to stay as long as possible left at 5.4% versus 8.8% for others — "small in magnitude".
  - On school moves: **4%** of those strongly disagreeing that they think about transferring actually moved; **11.1%** of those somewhat agreeing and **18.5%** of those strongly agreeing did. Even among the most emphatic, **only 19% moved**.
  - Regression-adjusted: intending to leave as soon as possible raises the probability of leaving by **26.9 percentage points**; strongly agreeing about transferring raises the probability of moving by **13.8 percentage points**.
  - *Conclusion:* turnover intent "measures a distinct construct that is moderately related to turnover"; using intent as a proxy for turnover "is likely inappropriate in most cases".
- *Limitations the authors state:* SASS measures intentions with **single items** rather than validated scales; only **one-year** follow-up, so dissatisfaction may manifest in later years; **voluntary and involuntary turnover cannot be separated**, which may bias the intention–turnover relationship downward.

**10.2 Corroborating evidence on the wedge.**
- Ladd (2011): perceptions predict **intended** movement much better than **actual** one-year movement (§4.2).
- Gundlach, Slemp and Hattie (2024): documents systematic differences in antecedent–outcome strength between real and intended turnover (§2.2).
- Sims (2016) versus Perryman et al. (2025) on inspection: **30% "considered leaving"**; **3.4 percentage points actually moved** (§3.4, §2.10).
- TALIS: **~25%** intend to leave within five years across OECD systems, against realised attrition of **5–11%** depending on system and definition (§7.1, §11.1).
- Räsänen et al. (2020): **half** of Finnish teachers report turnover intentions; Finnish realised attrition is far lower (§2.8).
- See et al. (2020) flag that several induction studies "use 'intention' to stay in the profession as an outcome rather than actual attrition figures" and treat this as a quality defect (§2.5).

**10.3 The rule of thumb for the paper.** Across the studies above, stated intention to leave exceeds realised one-year exit by a factor of roughly **three to four** where both are measured on the same population. Intention data are informative about the **distribution of dissatisfaction** across countries and school types; they are not a forecast of supply.

## 11. International variation

**State of the evidence.** Comparative measurement has improved markedly since 2024 but remains fragile. OECD *Education at a Glance* now publishes a harmonised attrition indicator for 19 systems; TALIS gives comparable stress and intention measures for 40-plus; Eurydice gives policy context. What is missing is comparative work on **drivers**: no study convincingly explains why attrition is 3% in France and above 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania. The one European cross-country study on turnover uses a retrospective ageing survey with all the recall problems that implies.

---

**11.1 OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025*, Indicator D8, "How severe are teacher shortages across countries?", OECD Publishing, Paris.** **[NEW as a directly quoted source for these figures]**
- *Design:* harmonised administrative statistics collected from national authorities. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Coverage:* 19 OECD countries and economies with available data, 2022/23; pre-primary to upper secondary; public institutions; full- and part-time.
- *Findings, precisely stated:*
  - **Average attrition — permanent departure of fully qualified teachers from the profession within a year, including both resignations and retirements — is 6.5%.**
  - Range: **below 3% in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel; over 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania.**
  - By level: pre-primary **7.3%**, primary **5.8%**, secondary **5.9%**.
  - Trend: **stable in most countries over the past decade**; Austria and Estonia rose by more than 2 percentage points between 2013/14 and 2022/23; **Denmark fell by a similar margin**.
  - Composition: **on average 51% of leavers resigned rather than retired**. In **Denmark, England (United Kingdom) and Estonia, over 80% resigned**; in Argentina (primary), France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel, **retirements account for more than half** of departures.
- *Limitations in the source notes:* reference years differ for some countries; coverage exclusions (upper secondary in Denmark and Israel; upper secondary vocational in the Netherlands; pre-primary in Greece and Ireland); some countries include non-fully-qualified teachers; some count teachers appointed to other positions in the education sector as leavers. **Cross-country comparisons of the level should therefore be made with explicit caveats, though within-country trends are sounder.**
- *Use:* this is the single best table for the paper's cross-country attrition claims and should replace looser figures.

**11.2 Federičová, M. (2021), "Teacher turnover: What can we learn from Europe?", *European Journal of Education*, Vol. 56/1, pp. 102–116.** **[NEW — the only located pan-European turnover study]**
- *Design:* secondary analysis of retrospective life-history data. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`/`[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* **Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), waves 1–7; 19 European countries**; occupational transitions of respondents who had ever taught.
- *Findings, precisely stated:* turnover rates follow broadly comparable trends across countries but with **substantial variation in employment arrangements and evaluation systems**. **Southern European countries show very low turnover, around 15% on average (lifetime measure), against about 37% in the northern countries in the sample (Sweden and Denmark).** Czech turnover is 31%, above other Central and Eastern European systems but below the Nordics. **Roughly 45% of beginning teachers in European countries leave the profession completely within five years** on this measure. The **decision to leave is strongly age-dependent, with young teachers leaving most often**. Eastern European patterns are distinctive because of post-1990 economic transformation.
- *Limitations:* SHARE is a survey of the **50-plus population**, so teacher careers are reconstructed retrospectively, with recall error and survivorship issues; the turnover measure is lifetime, not annual, and is **not comparable with the OECD annual attrition indicator in §11.1**; the article does not state formal limitations in the accessible text.
- *Caution:* the "45% within five years" figure is frequently recycled without its retrospective-cohort caveat. Do not present it alongside annual OECD rates without a definitional note.

**11.3 Eurydice / European Commission (2021), *Teachers in Europe: Careers, Development and Well-being*, Publications Office of the European Union, Luxembourg.** *(In the base as a reference; characterised here.)*
- *Design:* combined qualitative policy mapping (national legislation and policy across the EU/EEA) and quantitative analysis of TALIS. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`
- *Coverage:* lower-secondary (ISCED 2) teachers across Europe.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* a **general shortage** of teachers across countries, exacerbated in some by uneven distribution across subjects and geographical areas, an **ageing teacher population**, **low initial-teacher-education enrolment** and **high attrition**. Governments across Europe are responding by reshaping ITE, improving working conditions, reforming career structures and modernising CPD.
- *Limitation:* the quantitative base is TALIS, so the shortage and attrition claims are largely indirect; no harmonised attrition measurement of its own.

**11.4 TALIS-based comparative evidence.** OECD (2019, 2020, 2025) on entry motives, stress and intention (§7.1); Sims and Jerrim (2017) on 34 countries and 50,000+ teachers (§3.2); Jerrim and Sims (2022) on 40+ countries and 100,000+ teachers (§3.3). **What TALIS establishes:** intrinsic and social motives dominate entry in every system (~90%); high-stress prevalence varies enormously across systems around a 19% average; the stress–intention link is roughly a doubling and holds nearly everywhere; behaviour/discipline stress outranks workload stress as a correlate of intention to leave in the pooled 2024 data. **What TALIS cannot establish:** anything about actual exit, since it has no follow-up — except in England, where the DfE linked it to the School Workforce Census (§3.1).

**11.5 Nordic register evidence.** Falch, T. et al. (2022), "Teacher education and early teaching career", *Teachers and Teaching*, Vol. 28/8, pp. 943–963 (see also "Are Teachers Increasingly Leaving the Profession?", *Professions and Professionalism*). **[NEW]**
- *Design:* **administrative register panel** following teacher-education graduates for up to 12 years after graduation. `[DESCRIPTIVE]`/`[ASSOC]`
- *Sample and country:* Norway, full graduate cohorts.
- *Finding, precisely stated:* **gradual net attrition from teacher positions of about 1.3 percentage points per year**, beginning 1–3 years after graduation — a steady drip rather than an early-career cliff.
- *Value:* one of very few non-Anglophone administrative-panel measurements of the early-career attrition profile, and it does not look like the US "40–50% leave in five years" story.

**11.6 Netherlands.** Somers et al. (2024), *The hidden reserve of nurses and teachers in the Netherlands*, ROA — **~62,000 qualified teachers working outside education, about four times the secondary shortage of 13,600 FTE at end-2023**; inactive teachers rate several working conditions *more* favourably than active teachers. *(In the base.)* Complementary Dutch work: den Brok, P., T. Wubbels and J. van Tartwijk (2017), "Exploring beginning teachers' attrition in the Netherlands", *Teachers and Teaching*, Vol. 23/8, pp. 881–895 **[NEW]** — mixed-methods; identifies pre-service education quality, absence of mentorship, relationships with administrators and colleagues, excessive workload, professional status, low salary and alternative job opportunities as triggers, with attrition concentrated in the first three years; estimates that **13–16% of beginning secondary teachers leave within five years**, depending on definition.

**11.7 Germany.** No quantitative administrative study of teacher attrition comparable to the US, English, Norwegian or Dutch work was located. The German-language literature (see the *Teacher Shortages in Germany* chapters in the WATS/Brill volumes, 2023–2025) is dominated by policy description, Land-level comparison, and studies of **teacher-education dropout** (e.g. reported dropout of around 60% for Gymnasium-track students at Greifswald and Rostock after the standard study period) rather than in-service exit. The literature explicitly notes that research on attrition among *Seiteneinsteiger* (lateral entrants) is largely absent. **This is a substantial gap for the largest education system in the EU.**

**11.8 Global.** UNESCO / International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024), *Global Report on Teachers* — **primary-teacher attrition rose from 4.62% (2015) to 9.06% (2022) globally**; **44 million additional teachers needed by 2030**; **attrition accounts for 94% of teacher need in Europe and North America and 89% in Latin America and the Caribbean**; sub-Saharan Africa needs ~15 million, South Asia 7.8 million, Latin America and the Caribbean 3.2 million (2.8 million of them attrition-driven). *(In the base; the regional attrition-share figures are new here.)* `[DESCRIPTIVE]` — note that the underlying national reporting is of very uneven quality and the doubling should be treated as indicative.

## 12. Where the literature disagrees

1. **Is there a crisis at all?** Aldeman and Yi (2025) show US occupational leaving flat at ~7.6% across 40 years of CPS data with the pandemic spike fully reversed; NCES reports 84/8/8 stayer/mover/leaver shares statistically unchanged from 2012–13. Sutcher et al. (2019) and the LPI tradition treat 8% as a crisis-level rate that should be halved. OECD (2025) finds attrition "relatively stable in most countries" over a decade. Against all of this, Kraft and Lyon (2024) show every measure of the profession's *attractiveness* at or near a 50-year low, and UNESCO (2024) reports global primary attrition doubling. **The disagreement is partly definitional (stock versus flow, attrition versus attractiveness) and partly about the counterfactual: stable attrition against falling entry is a worsening position even with a constant exit rate.**

2. **Gender.** Borman and Dowling (2008): women more likely to leave. Nguyen et al. (2020), 37 studies: no difference. UNESCO (2024): direction varies by country. The most likely resolution is real historical change plus compositional differences across systems — but the older finding is still widely recited.

3. **Workload.** Every survey in every country puts workload at or near the top of stated reasons (Perryman and Calvert 2020; Cuervo and Vera-Toscano 2025; Brandenburg et al. 2024; Räsänen et al. 2020; Li and Yao 2022). Yet the one England study that links teacher-level survey data to *observed* exit finds workload's relationship with attrition "inconclusive" and its relationship with turnover and job satisfaction not robust — and in one specification hours worked is associated with *lower* attrition (Sims and Jerrim 2020). Jerrim and Sims's task-level work suggests the resolution is composition: specific tasks (marking, accountability documentation) drive stress, total hours do not. **Unresolved.**

4. **Teacher wellbeing.** The burnout literature treats teaching as an occupation in a wellbeing crisis (Madigan and Kim 2021; Li and Yao 2022; OECD TALIS). Jerrim et al. (2020) across eleven UK datasets find teachers no worse off than comparable occupations; Sims et al. (2022) find no biomarker health penalty; Jerrim et al. (2021) find that leaving teaching does not improve general mental health. **Directly contradictory, with the two sides using different data types (self-report scales versus population surveys and biomarkers).**

5. **Whether teachers undervalue pensions.** Fitzpatrick (2015): 20 cents on the dollar. Ni, Podgursky and Wang (2022): the same cohort tracked to retirement shows most teachers *did* buy the upgrade and those who did not were rational given their timing. Fuchsman et al. (2023): a 2–3% salary equivalence, i.e. a modest rather than extreme discount, with strong experience gradient. **Our base currently cites the 20-cent figure without the challenge and should be corrected.**

6. **Whether pay retains.** Falch (2011), Clotfelter et al. (2008), Feng and Sass (2018) and Sims and Benhenda (2022) all find real, quantified retention effects of targeted pay. See et al. (2020), quality-weighting the same class of studies, conclude that money recruits but does not durably retain and that effects last only while the payment does; Thompson-Lee et al. (2025) find the highest-quality recruitment study shows no bonus effect at all. **The disagreement is really about persistence and about what happens at the margin when the incentive is withdrawn.**

7. **Do students or salary drive mobility?** Hanushek, Kain and Rivkin (2004): student characteristics dominate salary. Nguyen et al. (2020): percent Black and percent Hispanic students are **not** significant once other factors are held constant, while student *achievement* is. The most plausible reconciliation is that "student characteristics" in the older work is a proxy for working conditions — behaviour, safety, leadership — that later studies measure directly.

8. **Class size and adult support.** Universally believed to matter; Nguyen et al. (2020) find no retention effect of class-size reduction or teaching aides in the odds-ratio models. Almost no rigorous test exists.

9. **Induction and mentoring.** Ingersoll and Strong (2011) find mostly supportive evidence; a 2022 meta-analysis reports mentored teachers with 2.85 times higher odds of remaining. Glazerman et al. (2010), the largest RCT (17 districts, 418 schools, 1,009 teachers), finds **no retention impact in any of three follow-up years**. See et al. (2020): "the stronger studies do not show consistent positive effects". **The correlational and experimental literatures point in opposite directions; the experimental one should govern.**

## 13. Where the literature is thin

**By motive.**
- **Collegiality as a distinct driver of exit.** It is the single largest attribute in the choice experiments (a collegial atmosphere worth 33 percentage points of choice probability, Sims et al. 2026), and it is the least well-evidenced in the behavioural literature: Nguyen et al. (2020) find collaboration non-significant on few studies; Sims and Jerrim (2020) find the Collaboration factor unrelated to any outcome. **We can price it hypothetically and cannot detect it in exit data.**
- **Autonomy.** One large England study, intention outcome only (Worth and Van den Brande 2020). No behavioural estimate anywhere.
- **Part-time and flexible working.** Strong evidence of unmet demand (one in six English secondary teachers want fewer hours; Dutch part-timers require a 23% wage rise to go full time) and near-zero evidence on whether making it available retains anyone. An EEF trial of offsite planning, preparation and assessment time is under way.
- **Care responsibilities and childbirth.** Nothing modern and well-identified (§8.6).
- **Status and prestige.** Documented as falling (Kraft and Lyon 2024) and cited constantly as a driver; never estimated as one.
- **Behaviour and safety.** Well-evidenced as a correlate of both intention (TALIS 2024) and observed exit (Sims and Jerrim 2020), but with **no evaluated intervention** anywhere linking a behaviour policy to retention.
- **Involuntary exit.** Nguyen et al. (2025) explicitly could not separate voluntary from involuntary turnover. Almost the whole literature treats exit as chosen.

**By region.**
- **Germany** — no quantitative in-service attrition study located; the largest EU system is effectively absent (§11.7).
- **Southern Europe** (Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece) — no dedicated attrition study located; represented only through EAG aggregates and Federičová's SHARE analysis.
- **Central and Eastern Europe** — one Czech study and Federičová's SHARE work; the two systems with the *highest* measured attrition in EAG 2025 (Estonia, Lithuania) have no study explaining it.
- **Japan, Korea and East Asia** — present in the burnout meta-analyses via Chinese samples but absent from the exit-behaviour literature.
- **Latin America** — represented in the choice-experiment synthesis (Chile, Costa Rica) and nowhere else.
- **Low- and middle-income countries generally** — UNESCO (2024) asserts that attrition accounts for 89–94% of teacher need in several regions, but the underlying national attrition measurement is weak and there is essentially no driver-level evidence.
- **The English-language bias is documented, not merely suspected:** Thompson-Lee et al. (2025) state that restricting to English "creates over-representation of studies from the USA".

**By method.**
- **No randomised evidence on any non-financial retention lever.** Thompson-Lee et al. (2025) identified no RCTs at all in the STEM literature; See et al. (2020) found that the interventions the correlational literature says matter most — accountability, workload, conditions, behaviour, leadership support — have almost no rigorous evaluations.
- **Almost no study links measured burnout to observed exit.** Madigan and Kim (2021) say so explicitly.
- **Very few systems link teacher survey data to administrative exit records.** England (via TALIS–SWC) is the exemplar; this design should be recommended in the paper's conclusions as the cheapest available improvement to the evidence base in any system that already runs both.
- **No causal evidence anywhere on re-entry incentives** — as our `lit_return_incentives.md` already notes, the causal literature prices *retention* and *entry*, never *return*.
