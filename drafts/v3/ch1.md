# 1. Introduction: a population and the count that measures it

Every education system has qualified more people to teach than it currently employs. The difference between the two is not an abstraction but a set of named individuals, held on registers that ministries already maintain, with a qualification date, a service history and, in most systems, a certificate status attached. As recruitment has grown more difficult across OECD countries, these registers have been read with new interest, and their headline totals circulate in public debate as estimates of a reserve awaiting recall. This paper opens with a practical question. What would it take to turn such a register into a count a ministry can act on?

The answer begins from the population rather than from the number. The people on those registers are a distinct policy object. They hold a licence the state issued, they accumulated their experience in the state's own classrooms, and the barriers that now separate them from a school were mostly written by the state itself, in pension law, certification rules and salary scales. Former teachers therefore merit analysis in their own right, for who they are and what they carry, and not chiefly for what they might contribute to closing staffing gaps; re-entry is a modest margin of any response to recruitment pressure, and this paper says so at the outset rather than discovering it at the end.

## 1.1. A population defined by what it carries

**Former teachers arrive already licensed or relicensable, immediately deployable without consuming an initial training place, and fluent in how schools actually run.**

That claim deserves careful statement, because it is easy to overclaim. Much of what former teachers would carry back overlaps with what systems already prize in their veteran staff, knowledge of the curriculum as it is actually taught and the standing to support colleagues newer than themselves. Yet what the record establishes is deployability and institutional fluency; the classroom value of accumulated craft remains an open question, since no study in this evidence base links a returner to the outcomes of the classes they teach or the colleagues they work beside. The paper treats that as a gap in what has been measured rather than as a verdict on what returners are worth, and it returns in the final chapter to the linkage that would settle it.

Return is meanwhile a routine channel wherever anyone counts it. Around a third of all new entrants to state schools in England in a given year are returners (Department for Education, 2023), and re-entrants have made up between a third and a half of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). Governments are already running a re-entry system; the question is whether they are running it deliberately.

In descriptive terms the population contains three groups: retirees, whose exit was largely actuarial; people who stepped out of paid work altogether, most often into care or family responsibilities; and job switchers who took their qualification into another sector. The linked records that exist suggest the third group is smaller than commonly assumed. In the United States, for example, more than half of leavers remain in education broadly defined, about a fifth exit the labour force entirely, and fewer than a quarter take jobs outside education (Brummet et al., 2024). Earlier cohort evidence points the same way, with most exiting women leaving the labour force rather than changing employer (Stinebrickner, 2002). Destination describes where former teachers went, and little more.

Two other distinctions carry the analysis, and both are visible in the registers. The first is whether a person ever served. Qualified people who never entered a classroom have no craft, no curriculum experience and no mentoring capacity to bring back, and under the premise of this paper they are not members of the population, whatever they represent for a supply model. The second is where a person stands relative to pensionable age, because that boundary decides which body of law prices their return. For those past it, pension and service-crediting rules set the offer; for those before it, salary scales and certification rules do.

## 1.2. What the headline figures measure

**Published reserve figures measure the rule of the register that produced them, and the deductions separating a register rule from a population all run in the same direction.**

The systems that have published their own internals show what each rule does (Table 1.1). In England, for example, nearly a third of the headline pool of qualified teachers outside state service has never been in service at all, a group the department already holds as a separate series (Worth, 2023, citing DfE), so a substantial share of the quoted number has nothing to return to. A register that links certificates to employment shows the same pattern from the inside. In Michigan, seven in ten of the certified non-teachers had not taught in a public school at any point in the previous five years, and close to half held expired certificates, against a small minority of serving teachers (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Neither figure is an error; each is an accurate count of whatever its register rule defines.

A single system can produce two reserves in the same year once two rules are applied. In the Netherlands, a count restricted to people qualified since the turn of the century put the reserve at just over 62 000 (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024b), while a series counting every qualification holder at least two years out of the sector, retirees excluded, put it near 98 000 (DUO, 2026). The gap between the two figures is the gap between the two rules, since the first truncates the qualification cohorts it counts and the second does not. Even a single rule, moreover, reads out shares of the qualified stock so different between primary and secondary education (Table 1.1) that no one percentage describes a national reserve, which is a further reason to publish the series by sector rather than as a headline.

In one system the ambiguity was even carried into the legislation designed to reverse it. The Dutch returner subsidy defined its target as a teacher who had interrupted, or had not begun, a career in primary education (Ministerie van OCW, 2017), so the instrument's own legal definition of a returner covered people with nothing to return from. A supply planner may reasonably object that the never served are convertible people and belong in a count of potential recruits. They are, and they do, for a supply model; for a paper about what former teachers carry back, they sit outside the population, and the two series should at the very least be published apart.

## 1.3. The count a ministry can publish

**A netted reserve series is a definitional and administrative product, and every register examined in this paper already holds the data to publish one within the year.**

The specification is short enough to state in full. A register that records service history can separate the served from the never served; one that records certification can distinguish valid from restorable from lapsed; last-taught dates separate recent leavers from those a decade or more away; and subject and phase can be set against vacancies, age against pensionable age, residence against region. Each of the six cuts moves the count downward and none moves it up, which is why the direction of the error in headline figures is known even where its magnitude is not. No system examined here publishes the resulting series, and any of them could.

A ministry that has been quoting a reserve is, in other words, a definition and a query away from quoting a measurement.

An upper bound is all the netted series would be, and the paper claims nothing more for it. Willingness, geography, subject fit and the price of return all bind after the register does, so even a netted count measures the population rather than forecasting a supply; the case for it is simply that a defensible upper bound beats an indefensible one. The OECD has already named the underlying gap, observing that many education systems lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees (OECD, 2024). The specification above is this paper's answer to that observation.

The same registers can also say how the population left, and that fact selects the instrument. Across OECD countries with data, about half (51%) of the fully qualified teachers who left the profession in the most recent year measured resigned rather than retired. Resignations exceed 80% of departures in some systems, notably Denmark, England and Estonia, while retirements account for most departures in others, including France, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025a). A system whose leavers resigned mid-career faces a working-age population priced by salary scales and working conditions; a system whose leavers retired faces a population priced by pension law. The exit split is therefore the first diagnostic to run, before any programme is designed.

## 1.4. What this paper does

**This paper treats re-entry policy as a standing administrative function of government, and reads it from the record governments themselves have produced.**

The strand is an established one. Former teachers have been treated as a potentially important source of recruits in OECD teacher policy for two decades (OECD, 2005), and supporting the re-entry of teachers who left the profession appears among the supply levers in the Organisation's current policy stocktake (OECD, 2024). What this paper adds is a compilation with an argument. Drawing on programme evaluations, audit findings, legislation and administrative statistics from some twenty education systems, it asks what governments have offered former teachers, what price their own rules attached to return, and what verifiably happened.

Chapter 2 asks what the people who left actually bought when they went, and what former teachers can be asked to do on their return. Chapter 3, the centre of the paper, examines the international experience with re-entry programmes and with the pension, licensure and re-employment rules that govern return. Chapter 4 converts the record into lessons and recommendations, ending with the two measurements on which better policy most depends, a reserve netted to a stated specification and a returner linked to the classes and colleagues they work with. Throughout, cases appear as evidence for propositions, and the argument is meant to be legible without any single one of them.
