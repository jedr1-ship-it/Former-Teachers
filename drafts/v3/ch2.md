# 2. What former teachers bought, and what they can be asked to do

Chapter 1 defined the population and the count that measures it. This chapter turns to the people themselves and asks what they actually got when they left, and at what price they got it. The question is a commercial one. Every re-entry offer competes with an alternative the former teacher has already chosen, so a ministry drafting such an offer is bidding against a purchase, and it needs to know what the purchase was.

The chapter makes two arguments. The first is that leavers, in the main, bought time, in the form of fewer hours and more control over them, at a cost in earnings they were willing to bear. That is why the offers that recruit former teachers in any numbers are offers of days rather than posts. The second is that a returner's contribution to a school is a designed role at a specified dose, and that the trial evidence on such roles reflects how tightly each role was drawn before it reflects anything about the people who filled it. Both arguments are read from behaviour rather than from testimony wherever the record allows it.

## 2.1. What leaving bought

**Former teachers bought fewer hours and more control over their time when they left, at a cost in earnings they were prepared to accept.**

The panel evidence that follows the same individuals across the exit shows the purchase directly. In Australia, where household data span two decades, those who left teaching report job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations higher than stayers one year after exit and 0.19 higher after three (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). Their weekly wages, meanwhile, run 6.2% and then 9.4% below the final year in post, and their hourly wages stay lower for a decade. Satisfaction that rises as earnings fall is the signature of a trade, not of a misfortune. On the evidence of their own subsequent behaviour, most leavers got what they paid for.

Where post-exit earnings can be observed elsewhere, the same terms recur. English leavers accept a wage cut of roughly a tenth against comparable stayers in exchange for shorter hours and higher job satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015), and very few American leavers move to jobs paying more than teaching did (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006). Linked administrative records in the United States find more than half of leavers still working in education broadly defined, with mean earnings below pre-exit levels years later (Brummet et al., 2024).

Stated preferences tell the same story from the other side. Dutch teachers outside the classroom rate several conditions of their current jobs more favourably than serving teachers rate theirs, and name lower work pressure, more control over working hours and more autonomy ahead of salary when asked what would bring them back (Somers et al., 2024a). In three quite different labour markets, then, the ledger of leaving shows time gained and money knowingly given up.

For part of the population the purchase was a stage of life rather than a better job. In the classic cohort evidence, around two thirds of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely, and the presence of a newborn child predicted exit more strongly than pay did (Stinebrickner, 2002). That cohort is historical, and parental leave and female labour supply have changed since, but recent panel data still place more than a quarter of leavers outside the labour force a year after exit, before retirements are counted (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025). The price of this group's return moves with childcare, the school calendar and the availability of part-time posts, well before any salary scale is consulted.

One qualification matters too much for a footnote. The panel estimates above are computed on a sample that excludes people who left permanently and never returned, so they are strongest as evidence about temporary exit, about leavers who kept a foot in the door. The limitation is also an explanation, because the segment for whom exit was explicitly temporary turns out to be the segment that converts best when an offer arrives, as the evidence below confirms.

The population a re-entry offer addresses is therefore mostly employed, mostly still close to education, and mostly no richer than it was. What it holds that it did not hold before is time.

## 2.2. What would reverse the trade?

**Behaviour observed after exit points to conditions as the price of return; the one large survey that asked former teachers directly points to salary.**

The two readings are reported here as a genuine split in the evidence, not a disagreement to be smoothed away. The survey arm has real weight. Among certificate-holders outside the classrooms of the one American state to survey them at scale, a third (33%) placed a higher salary among their three most important reasons for not teaching (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). A salary increase was also the incentive to return selected most often, ahead of pension, benefit and loan-forgiveness measures that were themselves financial. No revealed-preference study in this evidence base covers the population at comparable scale, so the arm cannot be discounted merely for pointing the other way.

Applying the same scepticism to both arms narrows the disagreement without dissolving it. The conditions-first reading rests on observed post-exit trajectories in three separate labour markets. The salary-first reading rests on a single survey with a response rate below one in three, which its own authors describe as not representative, answered from a menu of incentives dominated by financial items (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). Stated willingness is also cheap in every direction. In the one national registration survey that asks, over half of registered teachers not working in schools say they would consider returning under the right circumstances, while roughly one in twenty is actively looking (AITSL, 2025); a costless signal at unspecified terms is not an estimate of supply.

How people left also shapes what would bring them back, and the exit split introduced in Chapter 1 selects the relevant dial before any survey is fielded. Leavers whose exit was a life event return when circumstances change rather than when advertising improves, and many in the classic cohort eventually did return (Stinebrickner, 2002); mid-career resigners are priced by workload and control; retirees are priced by pension law, whatever their stated views on salary.

What the split settles is where a ministry should look first, not what it will find. Post-exit earnings trajectories are a register product, so which reading fits a given system is knowable from data the state already holds, and establishing it costs less than running a programme calibrated to a misread preference. A system whose leavers reveal a conditions-first exit buys little by offering money against that preference; a system whose former teachers state a price has at least been told which dial to test.

Chapter 3 returns to both dials with the programme record in hand.

## 2.3. Selling days rather than posts

**Every system that recruits former teachers at any scale sells them hours rather than posts, and the fit is causal, because an offer of days lets returners keep the trade they made.**

The standing regulatory routes converge on this product from very different starting points. In New Zealand, the statutory on-ramp is twenty half-days of relief teaching a year without a current practising certificate (New Zealand Legislation, 2020), and in Ontario a retired teacher may work fifty days a year before pension consequences begin (Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, 2026). In Ireland, the annual supply measure suspends the limit on substitute work by teachers already on career break (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). None of these rules was derived from a preference study. Each defines a small, divisible quantity of teaching, and that is the quantity the market clears.

The convergence has a seller-side logic as well as a buyer-side one. A day of relief teaching is a transaction a principal can complete alone, without an establishment post, a placement on the salary scale or a year-long commitment, and the thresholds in the rules above sit precisely where pension or certification consequences would otherwise begin. Each instrument, in effect, defines the largest quantity of teaching a former teacher can supply without re-engaging the machinery they left; what happens when that machinery is engaged anyway is the business of Chapter 3.

Absorption tells the same story as regulation, because where returners land in numbers, they land in part-time and casual work. In the one Japanese prefecture studied in depth, retirees make up roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024), and the returners recruited through direct outreach in New South Wales were placed into casual and temporary engagements (NSW Government, 2024). The Portuguese returner statute goes further and pro-rates its pay by teaching hours (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). The pattern predates the current generation of programmes. Returners already made up about a quarter of the full-time inflow into UK maintained schools at the turn of the century, but more than half of the part-time inflow (OECD, 2005).

Hours are the channel through which return has always run, not a concession the recent programmes invented.

The demand side matches. When registrants for England's returner pilot were segmented by motivation, the most return-committed group, the career breakers, made up 11% of registrants and returned at about double the rate of the other four types (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). What its members wanted was part-time work that schools rarely advertise. The scarce input in that market was an advertised part-time post, and interest was never the binding constraint.

Two systems drew the operational conclusion independently, which is some evidence that it is the right one. England's successor programme tied funded returner places to schools with identified vacancies, correcting a pilot that had prepared people for posts that did not exist (NCTL, 2017). New Zealand, for its part, waives its refresher requirement for returners who already hold an offer of work, so that the rule never delays a returner a school actually wants (Teaching Council of Aotearoa New Zealand, 2026). Both designs treat the identified vacancy, with the hours attached to it, as the unit in which re-entry policy deals.

The proposition has a boundary. No system in this record has offered a full-time post on genuinely changed conditions, with a protected timetable and a capped administrative load, so the revealed preference for small quantities is partly a preference expressed on the only menu available. The salary-first survey stands as contrary evidence throughout, and the obvious test, advertised part-time contracts with protected timetables, has been named since 2018 and tried nowhere (Worth and Sharp, 2018). There is also one true exception to the rule that scale comes in days. A single system filled establishment posts with former teachers at scale, and it did so by letting a full pension stack on a full salary, an institutional change whose price and consequences belong to Chapter 3 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

## 2.4. The designed role and the state of its measurement

**What former teachers can usefully be asked to do is a designed role at a specified dose, and the asymmetry in today's trial evidence is an asymmetry of measurement effort; the deployment that was specified is also the one that was measured.**

The channel that absorbs most returners asks least of what they carry. Relief and casual work buys presence in a classroom rather than continuity with a class, a hand in induction or a mentoring relationship, and the record that shows where former teachers are absorbed has never been asked what is gained or lost by absorbing them there. The comparison is unattempted rather than expensive; any system that linked returner records to the classes they teach and the colleagues they work beside could make it.

What the population carries also differs across systems more than the policy debate allows. Among the seven OECD countries with seniority data on resigning teachers, one in three resigners on average had less than five years of service (OECD, 2025a). The share ranges from about one in six in Denmark to around two thirds in Israel (Table 2.1). Leaver pools, in other words, differ radically in accumulated experience; a pool can be rich in accumulated craft or largely early-career. A designed role has to be designed against the pool a system actually has, which is a further reason the netted, subdivided count of Chapter 1 comes first.

At the specified end of the spectrum, the role was scripted, dosed and tested at scale, and the workforce that delivered it is the nearest kin to former teachers anywhere in the trial literature. A structured mathematics protocol for at-risk first-graders, taught in small groups for forty minutes, three times a week, over seventeen weeks, was delivered by more than eighty tutors recruited from local pools of retired and substitute teachers. Across nearly a thousand first-graders in dozens of schools in four urban districts, it reproduced an effect first obtained with graduate students, at +0.34 standard deviations on a standardised early-mathematics test (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012; Fuchs et al., 2005). The design purchased fidelity to a protocol rather than professional judgement, and it measured what it purchased.

At the discretionary end, the single trial is small and the dose was largely left to the mentoring pair. Recently retired master educators, screened by interview for a record of excellence and deliberately given no role in evaluating the teachers they mentored, averaged more than two decades in their district's high-need schools. The 77 probationary teachers in 11 schools of one district were assigned at random to receive one for two years (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The study meets What Works Clearinghouse standards without reservations, and under those standards its effects are indeterminate in all three reviewed domains, reading, mathematics and teacher retention (What Works Clearinghouse, 2019). Its first-year mathematics result did not survive the report's own multiple-comparison adjustment, and its retention point estimates ran the wrong way. Its one encouraging dose finding, a doubling of the odds that a mentee stayed with each additional ten hours of mentoring, is exploratory and non-causal by the report's own account.

That is one trial of an unspecified dose set against one trial of a specified one. An indeterminate rating under trial standards is a statement about evidential power, not a negative result, and a small trial that set no dose is no verdict on twenty years of practice.

The attribution problems run in the other direction as well, and they deserve stating once, plainly. The tutors in the positive trial were retired and substitute teachers, with no published split between the two. The tutoring meta-analysis finds the largest effects for teacher and paraprofessional tutors but does not isolate retired educators as a class (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024). The longest-running deployment of older adults as school reading tutors, for its part, is evidence about older volunteers, since being a former teacher is not among its eligibility conditions (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009). That programme still earns its place here, because its randomised volunteer arm found health gains for the tutors themselves, in physical activity across the trial and in brain volume within a neuroimaging subsample (Fried et al., 2004; Carlson et al., 2015). A result of that kind invites a health ministry's budget into a conversation education ministries mostly conduct alone.

In the meantime, the case for former teachers rests on what the evidence does establish. A former teacher arrives with a valid or restorable licence and is deployable without consuming an initial teacher education place. They also combine fluency in how schools run with a position outside the school's evaluation hierarchy, a trust advantage the mentoring trial's own report identifies and that neither a new graduate nor a serving head of department can offer (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The mechanism such roles would harness is itself documented, since teachers improve measurably when the quality of their colleagues rises, with the gains largest for the least experienced (Jackson and Bruegmann, 2009). But that mechanism works through sustained proximity, and dose is not a detail. The largest randomised trial of comprehensive teacher induction found no effects after one year of support, and a positive achievement effect only in the third year, among teachers who had received two full years of it (Glazerman et al., 2010).

Availability does not bind either design. The doses that matter in this literature run to a few dozen hours or sessions a year, with student gains concentrated among pupils who received at least that much (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009), while the caps that constrain retiree work run to hundreds of hours. A system that engages former teachers for token hours should therefore expect no measured effect, and one that buys a serious dose is buying well inside what its own rules already permit.

What is missing is the linkage that would settle the craft question. No study in this evidence base connects a returner to the outcomes of a class or of a colleague. Even the authors of the largest documented return episode, several thousand experienced retirees back in full-time posts, state that they could not attempt the estimate for want of vacancy data and a counterfactual (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). The exposure runs both ways. Linked data could show returners outperforming novices in comparable posts, which would strengthen a premise this paper has declined to assert, or underperforming them, which would narrow the rationale to the licence, the deployability and the fluency. The craft rationale therefore stands as an open question with a price attached, a linkage study and a trial of a designed role at a specified dose, and Chapter 4 converts both into requests.

What this chapter hands forward is a specification of the product former teachers have shown they will buy. It is denominated in hours, attached wherever possible to an identified vacancy, and, for the designed roles, delivered at a dose someone has chosen on purpose. Chapter 3 reads two decades of programmes and regulation against that specification, asking of each system what it offered, what price its own rules attached to return, and who set that price.
