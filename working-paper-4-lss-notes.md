# Working Paper 4 — Learning Support Staff (LSS)

*Research notes and revision plan*

---

## 1. The core problem

- **There is almost no research on Learning Support Staff (LSS).** This is the gap the paper aims to fill.
- LSS support teaching through **direct interaction with students**, covering both **instructional and non-instructional tasks**.

### The driving question — and the question behind it

The headline question is: **What drives the expansion of LSS?**

But that question presupposes something we haven't yet established. Before answering it, we need to confirm the premise:

- Is LSS *actually* expanding at a higher rate than in the past?
- Can we credibly claim it is expanding *widely*?

Only once expansion is established does the "what drives it?" question make sense.

1. **First — establish that an expansion is happening.**
2. **Then — argue what is driving that expansion.** This need not be a country-by-country account. Instead, identify **common factors** and support them with **illustrative examples**.

## 5. What do learning support staff do?

- **Enormous variation** — the role is **very, very difficult to characterize.**
- There are **too many levels of heterogeneity**. Keep this firmly in mind for the chapter.

### 4.2 — Is the role understood the way it is defined?

Essentially: **nobody has a clear, shared definition.** LSS is **not a shared category among policymakers.**

Open questions this raises:

- Do we even **need this much categorization**?
- Is the message, then, a **call to build common categories across countries**?
- **Does that make sense** given how heterogeneous each education system is?

## 6. Data gaps: job satisfaction and attrition

- **No international survey data on LSS job satisfaction or attrition** currently exist.

### An idea for working around this

No survey specifically tracks LSS — partly *because* there is no shared definition. However:

- Most countries run **longitudinal workforce surveys** that follow a representative sample of the population and track their **employment evolution over time**.
- Each job is tagged with an **occupation code**. In Europe, for example, **ESCO** characterizes each occupation and even the **skills** associated with it.
- **The workaround:** if someone maps specific occupation labels — e.g. *special education teacher*, or *teaching assistant at the K–12 level* — and declares that **these labels *are* LSS**, then that longitudinal data could be used to **follow LSS careers over time**.

This offers a possible empirical route into a population that is otherwise statistically invisible.

- A key unresolved question: **is there genuine attrition, or is it just rotation, temporary employment — or even informal (non-formal) employment?**
