# arg_tension.md — The case against re-entry policy, and the tensions we must not smooth

*Prepared 10 August 2026. Internal critique memo for the argument architecture. Every claim below is traceable to the verified research base; the fix files, `ch5_synthesis.md` and `TEAM_INPUT.md` govern where sources conflict. Nothing here is drawn from memory or the web.*

*Purpose: to state, as strongly as our own evidence permits, the case AGAINST investing in re-entry policy, and then to catalogue the tensions inside our material without resolving them cheaply. Where our team premise is strained, this memo says so. The point is not to sink the paper. It is to establish which of its claims can bear weight — because a policy paper that overclaims on this evidence base will be dismantled by the first reader who has actually read Buchanan et al. (2018).*

---

## PART I — THE CASE AGAINST

### Proposition 1. On the only cost-per-returner estimate that exists anywhere, re-entry programming costs more per teacher than training a new one — and the funnel shape that produced that result is a general property, not an English accident

**The claim.** England's Return to Teaching pilot cost an estimated GBP 37,400 per *additional* returner placed in a permanent shortage-subject secondary post — "similar to or higher than the average cost of training a new teacher" (GBP 33,300–46,900 per trainee employed after one year), leading NFER to conclude the pilot was "unlikely to represent good value for money" (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 56–58, 91). This is the only such estimate in the corpus, and the paper should treat it as the price of the activity, not as one country's misfortune.

**The mechanism.** The number is produced by a funnel that sheds an order of magnitude at every stage, and by deadweight at the end of it. Registrations of interest were 5,729 against a target of roughly 3,000; 354 people were actually supported; 27 were in permanent EBacc posts in state-funded secondary schools by November 2016 — eight per cent of those supported — with 62 in any state-funded post at all (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 11–12, 30–32). The GBP 17,800 cost per supported returner employed becomes GBP 37,400 once one assumes, as NFER does, that half would have returned without the programme. That assumption is the whole ballgame, and it is not conservative: around 4% of *non-supported* registrants returned over the same window, and the evaluators warn even that comparison flatters the programme because supported returners were more certain of returning to begin with.

The reason this generalises is that self-selection into a returner programme is not random with respect to return probability. NFER's own typology found "career breakers" — 11% of participants — returned at roughly double the rate of the other four types (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 10–12; Worth and Sharp, 2018). A programme that recruits by open registration therefore fills with people who were already going to return and people who were never going to be placed, and pays for both.

**What strains against it.** Two things, and neither rescues the programme model.

First, England's standing adviser service is an order of magnitude cheaper: GBP 361,344.69 in 2019/20 and GBP 325,562.73 in 2020/21 against roughly 128 recorded returns in maths, physics and MFL over those two years — an implied GBP 5,400 per recorded returner (UK Parliament, 2022; own calculation). But the same parliamentary record shows the conversion problem intact rather than solved: 23 physics returners in two years from more than 111 physics leads assigned an adviser, and more than 600 former teachers advised across the three subjects (Walker, 2022). No independent evaluation of the service has ever been published. The cheap instrument is cheap because it barely intervenes; its additionality is unknown and, on the physics numbers, unlikely to be high.

Second — and this is the more important qualification — the cost problem attaches to *programmes*, not to *rules*. North Carolina's 1999–2009 return-to-work window generated roughly 3,500 returners with no programme apparatus at all, and Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024) argue correctly that the pension annuity should not be counted as a policy cost, since it is paid whether or not the retiree teaches. But the rule was not free: returners drew full salary *plus* pension, up to about 50% more total income than a regular full-time teacher. The marginal fiscal cost per *post filled* was therefore not trivial, even though the cost per *returner* was near zero. The distinction the paper needs is between cheap-per-head and cheap-per-vacancy-closed, and North Carolina is only the former.

**Policy implication.** The cost-effectiveness case for re-entry rests almost entirely on regulatory instruments — removing abatement, eligibility screens, waiting periods and re-licensure friction — and almost not at all on funded programmes. Where a programme is funded, additionality must be designed in from the start. England had 5,729 registrants and could support only 354: a lottery among registrants was available, would have cost nothing, and would have produced the causal estimate the field still lacks. It was not used. The paper should say that.

### Proposition 2. Wherever a target was set, the yield fell short of it — and the shortfall is largest where the offer asks the returner to change the most

**The claim.** Re-entry instruments underperform their own stated ambitions with a consistency that rules out coincidence, and the pattern in the shortfalls is informative.

**The evidence, arranged as a mechanism rather than a list.** Portugal set a quota of 200 returner places under Article 5 of Decree-Law 51/2024, received 79–80 applications and accepted 63 (`cases_retiree_return` §3; `fix_austria_portugal` D1). The Netherlands budgeted EUR 1.25 million for 500 subsidised returners, subsidised just over 100 in 2018 and nearly 200 cumulatively by late 2019, then *lowered* its own annual ceilings to 120 and 100 grants explicitly because the budget "was not exhausted" (Stcrt. 2017, 62002; Stcrt. 2019, 28510; Kamerstukken II 35 300 VIII nr. 15). England overshot its registration target by 91% and missed its placement target by 96%. Georgia's HB 385 produced 635 full-time rehires cumulatively across FY2023–FY2025, about 350 in any given year, against roughly 6,000 vacant or not-fully-certified positions and a workforce of about 125,000; the state's own auditors called the effect on the shortage "minimal" (Georgia DOAA, 2025, Report 24-20). New Mexico's flagship no-caps route drew 154 users in its first year from a pool of roughly 48,000 potentially eligible educational retirees (NMERB, 2023; NM HED, 2022). Austria's Court of Audit counted 1,062 returners across six school years against a workforce of about 123,000 — while Austria alone reported 4,778 unfilled teaching posts in a single year, 4.6% of positions and equal to 107% of the previous year's teacher-training graduates (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24–25; OECD, 2025, *EAG 2025*, p. 491). Germany's thirteen recruiting Länder, one of which wrote to some 7,000 retirees in a single year, had just over 5,000 pensioners teaching nationwide, under 1% of an 800,000-strong workforce, against a shortage estimated at around 25,000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).

The common structure: outreach is cheap and interest is abundant; placement is scarce. NSW contacted more than 1,500 former teachers, drew more than 260 expressions of interest and placed 145 — roughly one placement per ten contacts (NSW Government, 2024a). Australia's registration data give the cleanest statement of the gap: over half of non-deployed registered teachers say they would consider returning "under the right circumstances", and 5% are actively looking (AITSL, 2025). The distance between 50% and 5% is the entire policy problem expressed in one line.

**The mechanism.** The binding constraint is neither information nor eligibility. It is the value of the job on offer relative to the alternative the person has already chosen — and that alternative is better than policy assumes. Australian leavers report job satisfaction 0.13 SD higher one year after exit and 0.19 SD higher three years later, while their weekly wages fall 6.2% and 9.4% respectively and their hourly wages remain lower throughout the following decade (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, pp. 9–11, Table 4, Figure 2). English leavers accept roughly 10% lower wages in exchange for shorter hours and higher satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). These people did not fail to optimise. They paid for what they wanted. A returner offer that reproduces the conditions they left is an invitation to unwind a trade they made deliberately, and its low take-up is the correct response, not a failure of communication.

**What strains against it.** North Carolina. Roughly 3,500 returners, over half of all schools hiring at least one, up to 2% of the full-time workforce at peak, and just over 35% of previously retirement-eligible teachers returning to full-time work by 2009 — with returns collapsing to zero when the window expired in 2010 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). That is a large yield and a clean dose-response. Yields are therefore not universally small; they are small when the offer is small. But the uncomfortable corollary is that the only design in our corpus with a large yield paid roughly 150% of a teacher's compensation to fill one post, and even then produced 2% of the workforce. The paper should state both halves: re-entry responds to price, and the price at which it responds is one no finance ministry will pay at scale.

**Policy implication.** Stop setting volume targets for re-entry instruments. Every target in the corpus was missed, and the act of setting one converts a marginal instrument into a visible failure, which is how instruments get cancelled before their regulatory core (the barrier removal) has been separated from their programmatic shell.

### Proposition 3. There is no causal evidence anywhere on re-entry incentives, and retention elasticities cannot stand in for it, because the two populations are selected on opposite sides of the same decision

**The claim.** No quasi-experimental study in the corpus prices re-entry incentives. The causal base — Fleck et al. (2025), in which 72 of roughly 86 included studies are American, and Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026) — concerns retention of incumbents and entry of novices (`lit_return_incentives` §C1, carried verbatim by `ch5_synthesis` §C6.2). Everything the paper says about what moves returners is inference from adjacent populations, administrative take-up under differing rules, or stated preference.

**Why the borrowing is not innocent.** Leavers are, by construction, the subpopulation whose reservation value for teaching under prevailing conditions was exceeded. If teachers differ in their taste for the job, leavers are drawn from the unfavourable tail of that distribution, so a given incentive should move them *less* than it moves incumbents — not equally. Sims and Benhenda's 23% reduction in leaving among eligible early-career maths and physics teachers, Feng and Sass's 10.4% and 8.9% attrition reductions, and Clotfelter et al.'s 17% turnover reduction are upper bounds for re-entry, and probably loose ones.

Fitzpatrick (2015) sharpens the point in a way the paper should use. Illinois teachers offered the chance to buy additional pension benefits were willing to pay only about 20 cents per dollar of present-value pension wealth — they value current cash roughly five to one over deferred compensation. Now note what currency re-entry offers are actually denominated in: abatement thresholds, service-crediting rules, waiting periods, salary-scale placement, pension-suspension arithmetic. These are the most deferred and most complex instruments in the toolkit, aimed at the least motivated population. Austria makes the point starkly: formerly civil-servant teachers who return are excluded by law from prior-service crediting for any period already generating a pension, so they draw their pension plus the teacher *starting* salary, while former contract teachers recover their pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Question 19, p. 18). Two returners doing the same job on different pay because of a rule about crediting is not an incentive; it is an accident of statute.

**What would settle it.** The natural experiment is sitting in plain sight and nobody has run it. United States state return-to-work law changed in staggered fashion and in documented directions: Georgia's HB 385 (2022, sunset June 2026, extended to 2030 by SB 150 in 2026); New Mexico's HB 73 (2022, 36 months, no salary or hours caps, extended to 60 months and a USD 25,000 threshold by SB 133 in 2025); Michigan's PA 184 of 2022 (nine-month wait, no earnings limit) then PA 147 of 2023 (six months, with an explicit sunset on 10 October 2028); Virginia's cut from a twelve-month to a six-month break in 2023 with a sunset of 1 July 2028; Louisiana's Act 394 of 2024 extending the no-suspension critical-shortage route to all retirees regardless of retirement date, consolidated by Act 344 of 2026. Every one of these systems administers pension re-employment and therefore counts it. A difference-in-differences on retiree re-employment rates across states and reform dates is feasible with existing administrative data. Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024) did the single-state descriptive version; the panel has not been built.

Portugal is the closest thing to a within-system comparison anywhere — same decree, same month, same profession, two margins — but it is not identified either, because the eligible populations differ (retired five years or less, versus at retirement age and still serving). The paper must not present it as a price experiment; `fix_austria_portugal` D1 is explicit that returners were never offered the EUR 750.

**Policy implication.** The paper should state the evidence gap as a finding in its own right and name the design that would close it, rather than hedging every claim into mush. "We do not know what re-entry incentives buy, and here is the study that would tell us" is a stronger sentence than any number in the corpus.

### Proposition 4. Portugal ran both margins at once, and the retention margin outperformed the re-entry margin by roughly thirty-five to one

**The claim.** Decree-Law 51/2024 created two instruments in the same text. Article 5 let teachers retired for five years or less return on fixed-term contracts keeping their full pension plus compensation indexed to the first step of the salary scale, pro-rated by teaching hours: 63 accepted returnees against a 200-place target. Article 6 paid EUR 750 a month to teachers who had reached retirement conditions but stayed on: roughly 2,200 users in 2025/26 (`cases_retiree_return` §3; `fix_austria_portugal` D1.1). This is the single most consequential comparison in our corpus and it argues against our own subject.

**The mechanism — three distinct forces, which the paper should name rather than merely report the ratio.**

*Switching costs.* Staying requires no re-entry decision: no re-accreditation, no new school, no re-establishing classroom routines, no renegotiation with a household that has already reorganised itself around retirement. Returning requires all of them. Re-entry policy pays to undo a transition that has already been consumed. This is the mechanism the paper should elevate above the arithmetic, because it explains the entire pattern in Proposition 2 as well.

*Instrument complexity.* EUR 750 a month is simple, current and salient. Pension plus first-step-indexed pay pro-rated by hours is none of those. Fitzpatrick's five-to-one preference for current over deferred compensation predicts exactly this ordering: the more motivated population got the simpler instrument.

*Pool composition.* The Article 6 pool is everyone reaching pensionable age while still serving; the Article 5 pool is those retired five years or less and willing. Pool size differs, but not by thirty-five, in a system with Portugal's ageing profile — so composition attenuates the comparison without dissolving it.

**What strains against it, and must not be smoothed.** Three things.

First, the retention margin has its own deadweight, and the Portuguese government conceded it. In July 2026 the EUR 750 supplement was narrowed for 2026/27 to shortage areas only — eight pedagogical zones covering 235 school units — with the minister saying the blanket version "worked" but "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026; CNN Portugal, 2026). Some unknown share of the 2,200 would have stayed regardless. The asymmetry is real; the 35× is an upper bound on it.

Second, the retention margin has a hard ceiling that the re-entry margin does not. A retirement can be postponed once, and the person retained is by definition from the oldest cohort. Article 6 buys one year at a time at the end of a career. Its yield is high and its durability is short. This is a genuine reason not to read 63-versus-2,200 as a straight verdict on which margin deserves investment.

Third, causal analogues from outside Portugal point the same way but are also about retention: pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4 to 10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). We have no matching estimate on the re-entry side.

**Policy implication.** Where a system faces a retirement wave and can act on both margins with the same money, the retention window should be exhausted before the re-entry channel is funded — and the paper should say so plainly, even though it is a sentence that demotes its own subject. What re-entry offers that retention cannot is a channel for people already gone, and a lever on *where* they go (see Proposition 8).

### Proposition 5. Where returners and new entrants compete for the same posts, re-employment rules have already been rolled back on equity grounds — and this is the only documented case in our corpus of a re-entry policy reversed on the merits

**The claim.** Ontario moved its pension re-employment cap from 20 to 95 days in 1990 amid a severe shortage, cut it to a flat 50 days from September 2012, restored 95 days for the pandemic school years 2020/21 to 2022/23, and returned to 50 days from July 2023, with the Ontario Teachers' Federation refusing a fourth extension in March 2024 (`cases_other_legislation` §2.3; OTPP, 2026; OTF, 2023; Alphonso, 2024).

**The mechanism, and why the 2012 cut matters more than the increases.** The reduction followed a *Globe and Mail* investigation showing Ontario's ten largest boards spending CAD 108.3 million a year on retired supply teachers; retirees earning CAD 432.69 a day against CAD 227.36 for new teachers; roughly 12,774 newly certified teachers chasing about 6,500 annual openings; and one board giving retirees 2,861 supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). Note the price inversion: retirees cost nearly twice as much per day and were still preferred. The preference was not for cheapness but for a known quantity — which tells us displacement operates through principals' risk aversion in decentralised casual hiring, not through wage undercutting.

Displacement bites hardest where the post is casual or supply rather than permanent, where the returner arrives with a pension income and can therefore accept intermittent work that a new graduate cannot afford to wait for, and where hiring is devolved to schools. Japan shows the equilibrium this produces: in the prefecture studied in depth, 52.7% of part-time adjunct hires were in their sixties and 7.1% in their seventies, so retirees made up roughly 60% of all adjuncts (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, p. 203). The Bahamas is formalising the same dependence — the union president observes that "many retired educators now work full-time in supply roles" because vacancies remain unfilled, and the Teacher Reservist Program runs on the existing Supply Teacher platform (Munnings, 2026; ZNS Bahamas, 2026a). Chinese coverage of the Silver Age plan carries a warning that some localities use retiree recruits to cut formal posts or delay hiring new full-time teachers (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025 — secondary source, and the paper must flag it as such).

The Ontario Teachers' Federation's stated reason for refusing the 2024 extension is the sharpest formulation of the principle anywhere in the corpus: "The Pension Plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers; it is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024; OCEOTA, 2024).

**What strains against it.** North Carolina's returners flowed *toward* disadvantage, not away from it: 30% taught in the top quartile of economically disadvantaged schools against 21% in the bottom quartile, in schools with 3.71 percentage points more economically disadvantaged and 4.86 percentage points more Black students than average, and 54% returned to the school they had taught in before retirement (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). No mandate produced this sorting.

More awkwardly, Ontario's own numbers undercut the displacement alarm at the same time as they document it: when the cap was raised in the pandemic, only about 60 of 142,000 retired plan members had expressed interest between September 2021 and January 2022, and the average retiree age was 72 (Global News, 2022). Displacement and yield are two readings of the same number. **The paper cannot simultaneously claim that re-entry is a meaningful supply lever and that it poses no risk to new entrants.** Whichever it asserts, it owes the reader the other side.

**Policy implication.** Re-employment caps are two-way dials, and the equity guardrail belongs in the design, not in the review. Where a system relaxes caps, it should publish new-graduate hiring rates alongside retiree re-employment counts, and Ontario's two shocks — the 2012 cut and the 2020 restoration — are the cleanest available test of whether the concern is real.

### Proposition 6. A returner who left for conditions returns to those conditions, and England's own data show returners retain worse than novices

**The claim.** Of the 2011 cohort of returners to English state schools, 32% were out of service four years later with a further 2% retired, against 29% of the 2011 newly qualified cohort; separate DfE analysis found the odds of a returner leaving after their first year back are 60% higher than average (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 57). This is the most under-used figure in our corpus and it is corrosive to the reserve framing: the reserve is not a stock to be drawn down once. It is a revolving door.

**The mechanism.** The conditions that caused the exit are, at the moment of return, unchanged. TALIS 2024 reports 19% of teachers experiencing high stress, and — the finding the paper should carry — stress from student intimidation, verbal abuse and maintaining classroom discipline is more closely associated with intending to leave within five years than workload, accountability or reform pressure (OECD, 2025, *Results from TALIS 2024*, Ch. 7). Meanwhile the counterfactual improves with time: the Australian panel shows the leaver's satisfaction advantage *growing* from +0.13 SD at one year to +0.19 SD at three (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, p. 11). The gap a returner must close therefore widens the longer they have been away — which is precisely the population the large reserve counts are made of.

**The arithmetic consequence the paper should draw.** GBP 37,400 is a cost per additional *returner*. The policy-relevant denominator is additional *teacher-years*. If returners carry a hazard rate 60% above average in their first year back, the cost per teacher-year is meaningfully higher than the headline, and England's already-unfavourable comparison with initial training gets worse. No system in the corpus reports cost per returner teacher-year. England is the one system that could, because it already computes returner-versus-NQT retention.

**What strains against it.** North Carolina's returners were a different animal: an average of 31 years' experience, 54% returning to their own former school, most of them within a year or two of retiring. Resch's Austrian interviews point the same way — the finding, abstract-verified only, is that continuing professional contact with the former school is what converts availability into return (Resch, 2025; `fix_austria_portugal` A4.3–A4.4). The retention penalty is therefore probably concentrated among long-absent, weakly attached returners. That does not rescue the reserve framing; it indicts it, because long-absent and weakly attached is exactly what a 366,000-person out-of-service stock consists of.

**Policy implication.** Re-entry without a change in the job recruits people into a hazard rate the policy has not reduced. Systems should either pair re-entry offers with a genuinely different job — part-time, protected timetable, reduced administrative load, mentoring or tutoring rather than a full class — or accept that they are buying short-duration cover and price it accordingly.

### Proposition 7. The reserve is a stock of qualifications, not a supply of willingness, and every register country that has interrogated it has found the effective pool an order of magnitude smaller

**The claim.** The headline reserve numbers are sound as administrative counts and misleading as supply estimates, and the paper's own sources say so.

**The evidence.** Michigan is the clearest demonstration because the report does the demolition itself. Of 141,810 certified teachers, 61,252 were not teaching in a Michigan public school in 2017/18. But 70% had not taught in a public school at any point over the previous five years; 44–49% held expired certificates against 6% of active teachers; roughly 16% may live twenty or more miles outside the state; 22% of respondents had become administrators or instructional leaders; and the survey layer rests on a 30% response rate with an analytic sample the authors themselves describe as "not representative of Michigan certified teachers who do not teach", whose unweighted percentages "should be interpreted with caution" (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, pp. 4–5, Table 1, Box 2, notes 2 and 5). Apply those filters in sequence and the 61,000 becomes a number the paper should not put in an abstract.

The Netherlands supplies the disclaimer. DUO's register-based estimate, on a stricter definition — at least two years out of the sector, excluding agency workers, retirees and the incapacitated — gives 34,000 in primary and 64,000 in secondary and vocational education, and states in terms that the reserve "does not, however, solve the teacher shortage" (DUO, 2026). It also shows the reserve *falling* by 17% and 18% respectively between October 2015 and October 2024, which means the shortage has already been drawing it down and the marginal returner is drawn from a residual that has repeatedly declined the invitation. England's equivalent verdict is NFER's: alongside an out-of-service and never-in-service stock near 366,000, "there is not such a ready supply of other sources of teachers" (Worth, 2023, p. 9).

The longitudinal literature closes the argument. Roughly one third of United States leavers ever return (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016); Moyer (2022), replicating with the NLSY97, finds only about one fifth of those who exited between 2000 and 2019 ever returned — substantially below the earlier cohorts studied by Grissom and Reininger (2012). The propensity is not merely modest. It is declining.

**The anomaly that should be in the paper and is currently nowhere.** The Dutch RAP evaluation reports that several regions received "more side-entrant and returner applications than places" available (De Jong et al., 2022, p. 37), while tailored support for returners was among the least-realised activities of the whole scheme (p. 60). In the country with the best-measured reserve, in the same programme, applicant supply outran school absorption capacity. If schools cannot place the returners who already apply, a policy to generate more applicants is aimed at the wrong constraint. Both constraints — too few willing returners, too few places for willing returners — cannot bind in the same system at the same time. The paper must say which binds, where, and stop treating the reserve as uniformly available.

**Policy implication, and an inversion worth taking seriously.** The most defensible use of the reserve statistic is not as a supply forecast but as a *diagnostic of retention failure*. Forty-three per cent of Michigan's viable certified stock outside the classroom; 43% of qualified Dutch secondary and vocational teachers outside the sector; 366,000 in England; roughly 19% of Australia's 553,300 registrants not deployed in schools — read as symptoms, these numbers measure how badly systems keep the people they trained. Read that way, the reserve arithmetic is an argument for retention, not for re-entry. The paper should make that move explicitly rather than letting the reader make it and conclude the paper missed it.

## PART II — THE TENSIONS, AND WHAT WOULD RESOLVE THEM

Each of the following is genuinely unresolved on our evidence. For each: what the tension is, what would settle it, and what the paper should honestly say while it stands.

### Tension A. The reserve is large and the yield is small

*The tension.* Somers, Groot and van Merode's quotable result — if 22% of the Dutch hidden reserve worked full-time in education again the shortage would be completely solved — coexists with a Dutch returner subsidy that could not spend EUR 1.25 million on 500 returners and was wound down for lack of takers. Both are true, from the same country, in overlapping years.

*What would resolve it.* A register-linked panel that follows identified members of a measured reserve through a policy change and observes return. DUO already tracks the Dutch reserve annually from 2015; linking that panel to the herintreders subsidy window (2017–2020) would produce the first credible estimate of how much of a measured reserve any instrument can activate. Nobody has done it, and the Dutch data are the only place it is currently possible.

*What the paper should say meanwhile.* Report the reserve as a diagnostic of churn and a boundary condition on ambition, never as a supply forecast; quote DUO's own disclaimer in preference to the 22% headline; and state, as `TEAM_INPUT` §3 requires, that the arithmetic is context and not promise. Add the observation that a shrinking reserve is evidence the easy returns have already happened.

### Tension B. Conditions drive exit; financial instruments are what governments deploy

*The tension.* The conditions evidence is broad and consistent: Australian panel data (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025), the 256-leaver Australian survey in which school leadership at 17.71% and workload at 16.57% lead and pay barely features (Brandenburg et al., 2024), Dutch inactive teachers naming lower work pressure, more control over hours and more autonomy ahead of salary (Somers et al., 2024), and English leavers accepting a 10% wage cut for better conditions (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). Yet Michigan's inactive certificate-holders put higher salary first, both as a reason for not teaching (33% among their top three) and as the leading incentive to return (Lindsay et al., 2021, Figure 1), and the American causal literature is dominated by money because money is what governments deploy.

*The resolution the paper should NOT reach for.* "It depends on the country" is forbidden by `TEAM_INPUT` §2, and it is also the wrong diagnosis. The deeper explanation is that **the evidence base is shaped by what can be evaluated, and financial instruments are what can be evaluated.** Fleck et al. (2025) find the working-conditions evidence "thinner but directional" — a curriculum reform that raised workload increased turnover by about 4.3%, reduced testing had little effect overall — and Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge (2026) find only one quasi-experimental study on professional development as a recruitment or retention lever. Governments deploying money are not being lazy; they are deploying the only instrument with both an evidence base and a budget line. A paper that scolds them without saying this is being unfair, and will be read as naïve.

*What would resolve it.* A causally identified evaluation of a *conditions* instrument aimed at returners: a guaranteed part-time contract, a protected timetable, a capped administrative load, with random or discontinuous assignment. NFER identified the scarcity of advertised part-time posts as the binding constraint for the most return-committed segment in 2018 (Worth and Sharp, 2018; Buchanan et al., 2018), and in the eight years since, nobody has tested the fix.

*What the paper should say meanwhile.* Financial instruments should be judged as *compensation for friction*, not as payment for preference reversal. Every design in the corpus with real take-up pays to remove a cost of returning rather than to outbid a preference: North Carolina removing an abatement penalty, New Mexico removing caps, New Zealand waiving the Teacher Education Refresh for returners with a relief-teaching offer, China's CNY 20,000 covering the cost of serving in poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority and border counties. Michigan's respondents naming salary may be naming the wrong instrument for the right reason — and the paper should note that this is the corpus's weakest survey (30% response, self-declared non-representative) carrying its heaviest "pay first" conclusion.

### Tension C. Retirees are the group policy can most easily move and the group with the shortest remaining horizon

*The tension.* Almost every instrument in the corpus targets retirees — Austria, Germany, Japan, China, Portugal's Article 5, all six United States state laws, Ontario, France's *cumul emploi-retraite*, the Bahamian reservist pool, and NSW, where roughly 60% of the 145 placements came from retirement. They are targeted because their barriers are legal and financial rather than motivational, and because in systems without registers — Germany, Austria, France — retirees are the only pool policy can see (`lit_reserve` Claims 7–8). But Ontario's retired members average 72 years old (Global News, 2022); Portugal's returners averaged about 68; Japan's willingness is visibly eroding, with only about 35% of age-based elementary retirees and 55% of junior-high retirees now seeking full-time re-employment and ageing adjuncts increasingly declining the most demanding cover (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203); and Austria's Court of Audit records intakes peaking in 2021/22 and 2022/23, notes the trend "did not continue", and stresses the measure's temporary character (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24–25).

*The measurement trap this creates, which the paper should name.* A policy whose most responsive population has a three-to-eight-year horizon must be re-run continuously, and each re-run draws from a pool depleted by the last. First-cohort take-up skims the stock of near-retirees who were already ambivalent; steady-state yield is necessarily lower. **Every take-up figure in our corpus except Austria's and New Mexico's is a first- or second-year number**, which means the literature systematically overstates what these instruments deliver in equilibrium. Austria's declining series is what exhaustion looks like when someone bothers to measure for six years.

*What would resolve it.* Multi-year cohort take-up series. Only the Austrian Court of Audit and the New Mexico Educational Retirement Board publish them.

*What the paper should say meanwhile.* Never quote a first-year take-up figure without saying it is a first-year figure, and make multi-year uptake reporting a design condition of any re-entry instrument. And state the structural point: a retiree-based reserve depletes on a schedule policy does not control.

### Tension D. The craft argument is strong in rhetoric and thin in trial evidence

*The tension.* `TEAM_INPUT` §3 makes the paper's premise that former teachers matter per se for what they bring back — classroom craft, curriculum knowledge, mentoring capacity. Here is what the evidence establishes.

The one randomised trial of retired teachers mentoring new teachers meets What Works Clearinghouse standards without reservations and produces no statistically significant finding in any reviewed domain: reading, mathematics or teacher retention are all rated indeterminate (WWC, 2019, Study #88756). The underlying REL report's year-one mathematics effect (ES 0.064, p = .045) does not survive the report's own Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment at a threshold of .025, and retention point estimates ran the wrong way — 11 programme leavers against 5 controls (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The strongest positive result, Number Rockets, shows 86 tutors recruited from local pools of retired *and substitute* teachers reproducing, at scale with 994 first graders in 76 schools, an effect (+0.34 SD on TEMA-3) first obtained with graduate students (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). Read carefully, that is evidence retirees are adequate deliverers of a scripted intervention, not evidence that accumulated craft adds anything a trained non-teacher would not. Experience Corps is weaker still as craft evidence, because being a former teacher is not an eligibility criterion and the share of retired educators among its 50-plus volunteers is undocumented (`cases_mentoring` §4); it is a study of older volunteers, not of former teachers. The best causal footing for skill transfer — Jackson and Bruegmann's finding that a 1 SD improvement in peer quality raises a teacher's students' growth by 0.02 SD in reading and 0.04 SD in mathematics, most strongly for the inexperienced — is about incumbent colleagues, not returners.

Against the premise, the corpus's only direct testimony on returners' currency is anecdotal and unfavourable: generational gaps and retirees being less current on new curriculum, instruction and technology (Rice, WestEd, personal communication — unpublished, and `cases_mentoring` §5 recommends the surrounding entry be dropped). And the OECD's own position points the same way: *Teachers Matter* recommends extending induction to returning teachers, not only beginners (OECD, 2005, p. 121). The canonical view is that returners need re-skilling, not that they arrive skilled.

*The uncomfortable conclusion.* Our premise is a reasonable prior with no direct causal support, and in the one place it has been tested head-on it failed. Note also that where former teachers *have* shown measurable value — Number Rockets, Experience Corps — it was in tutoring, not in classroom return. That cuts against the shortage framing entirely, since tutoring roles do not fill vacancies.

*What would resolve it.* A design comparing returner-staffed with novice-staffed classrooms on student outcomes in a system with matched teacher–student administrative data. Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024) explicitly could not do this: no vacancy data, no counterfactual, no achievement outcomes. North Carolina's NCERDC and England's School Workforce Census linked to the National Pupil Database could.

*What the paper should say meanwhile.* Hold the premise, but restate it so it does not depend on an effectiveness claim. Former teachers merit their own analysis because they are a distinct policy population with distinct, *legally created* barriers — lapsed licensure, pension abatement, service-crediting exclusions, re-accreditation — and because they are the only entrant group whose reasons for leaving are on the record. That is fully defensible on our evidence. The claim that they bring back superior craft is a hypothesis with a named test, and should be labelled as one. The paper should also resist borrowing the veteran-teacher evidence — Jackson and Bruegmann, and TALIS's mentoring gap in which only 22% of novices have an assigned mentor and 38% of teachers have participated in any induction activity (OECD, 2019) — to support claims about returners. That elision is exactly the move the lead author's diagnosis forbids.

### Tension E. Systems that legislate get measurable results; systems that run programmes get announcements

*The tension.* On one side: North Carolina's 3,500 returners and 35% take-up with a collapse to zero on expiry; Georgia's 635 rehires, 350 a year, USD 11.1 million in contributions and an audit the statute itself mandated; New Mexico's tiered annual participation reporting (154, 390, 686 and 1,716 across four routes, 2,792 in total); Austria's audited 1,062; Ontario's documented cap history with costs; Louisiana's actuarial report mandated before 2034. On the other: NSW's funnel published once in May 2024 and absent from the following Annual Report, with the programme no longer named on the teach.NSW returner page; no uptake counts for Michigan's PA 184 or PA 147, Virginia's critical-shortage rehires, New Zealand's TER waiver, France's *cumul emploi-retraite* for teachers, or China's progress toward 120,000; the Dutch subsidy lapsing on 1 January 2022 without an evaluation; the Bahamas at announcement stage.

*Why the paper must not resolve this cheaply.* It is not obvious that this is a difference in *effectiveness* rather than a difference in *observability*. Pension systems must count re-employed retirees because pension eligibility depends on the count; programmes need not count anything. So the correlation between legislating and measuring may be an artefact of administrative machinery. Two facts support the artefact reading: Georgia's audit exists because the statute ordered it, not because legislation is inherently accountable; and the single best-evaluated instrument anywhere is a *programme* — England's pilot — evaluated because the department commissioned an evaluation. Accountability is a design choice, not a property of instrument type.

Two facts support the substantive reading: a rule acts on the entire eligible population continuously and costs nothing per head, whereas a programme acts on a self-selected registrant flow at high unit cost; and North Carolina's return to zero on expiry is a dose-response no programme has produced.

*What the paper should honestly say.* Claim the substantive advantage only for the specific mechanism — removing a legal penalty reaches everyone and requires no take-up effort — and attribute the evidence asymmetry to accountability design, quoting the OECD's own diagnosis: "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically" (OECD, 2024, *Education Policy Outlook 2024*, p. 51). And acknowledge that our comparison is between instruments we can see and instruments we cannot, which is not a fair fight.

### Tension F. The premise says former teachers matter per se; most of the verified evidence is about supply

*The tension.* Count the corpus honestly. Material about former teachers as *people* amounts to: nine episodic interviews in Austria, abstract-verified only because the article is paywalled (Resch, 2025; `fix_austria_portugal` A4); a self-selected survey of 256 Australian leavers (Brandenburg et al., 2024); one panel study (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); one survey with a 30% response rate that its own authors call non-representative (Lindsay et al., 2021); one willingness item (AITSL, 2025); and one returner typology (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). Everything else in the corpus — reserve counts, take-up figures, pension rules, audits, funnels — is supply accounting. The premise's evidentiary base is thin enough to characterise the population and too thin to establish its distinctive value.

*What would resolve it.* Nothing in the corpus, and nothing currently in the field. The Australian and Dutch register infrastructures could support a proper study of what returners do in classrooms; neither has been used that way.

*What the paper should say meanwhile.* Make the per-se claim definitional and normative rather than empirical, as set out under Tension D, and be explicit that the paper is a policy analysis of a population defined by its barriers, not an effectiveness study. Also acknowledge that the framing is *not* new: *Teachers Matter* called former teachers "a potentially important source of recruits" twenty years ago, recommended maintaining contact with them, targeted retraining, part-time opportunities and flexible re-entry pathways, and noted that returners were about 25% of the full-time inflow and more than half the part-time inflow into United Kingdom maintained schools in 2000/01 (OECD, 2005, pp. 68–70, 88–89, 121, 165). Two decades later the corpus contains one evaluated programme. The paper's contribution is compilation and diagnosis, not discovery, and saying so is a strength.

### Tension G. The instruments are aimed at the wrong margin in the systems that need them most

*The tension, which the paper should treat as a finding rather than a caveat.* Across 19 OECD systems, on average 51% of departing fully qualified teachers resigned rather than retired; over 80% resigned in Denmark, England and Estonia, while retirements exceed half of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025, *EAG 2025*, Ch. D8, pp. 484, 493–495, Table D8.4). Now line the instruments up against that distribution. Almost every re-entry instrument in the corpus targets retirees. But the systems with the largest resignation problem are exactly where a retiree-targeted instrument addresses the smallest share of the leaver pool — and the systems where retirement dominates departures are the *low-attrition* systems, with France, Greece, Ireland and Israel all below 3% total attrition against Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania above 10%.

*The mechanism.* Retirees are the only pool most systems can see. Register countries can measure a working-age reserve; Germany, Austria and France cannot, so their debate defaults to the visible pool (`lit_reserve` Claims 7–8, and `ch5_synthesis` Lesson 1). The instrument set is therefore shaped by measurement capacity rather than by the shape of the problem. That is a mechanism-level explanation of a policy mismatch, and it is the strongest argument in the corpus for the "measure before legislating" recommendation.

*The counter-strain.* England, the most resignation-heavy system in the OECD table, is precisely the one that built *returner*-targeted rather than retiree-targeted instruments — the 2015–16 pilot, the Returners Engagement Programme, the adviser service — and produced the worst measured yield of all. Visibility is therefore not the whole story: the working-age resigner margin may simply be harder to move, because the alternative those people hold is a job they preferred, not retirement.

*What the paper should say.* Diagnose the exit margin before choosing the instrument, and say openly that the two margins are not equally tractable — the retiree margin is legible and legally movable but short-horizon; the resigner margin is larger in the systems with the worst shortages and has never been moved at measurable scale anywhere in our evidence.

### Tension H. Programmes are additional to a flow they did not create and cannot distinguish themselves from

*The tension.* Re-entry is already large and mostly unprogrammed. Roughly 17,000 teachers return to English state schools each year, about a third of all new entrants (DfE, 2023); returners rose in 2024/25 while other entrant categories fell; and in July 2026 the Education Secretary reported that more teachers are returning to state schools than at any point in the past decade — with no returner-specific scheme in the post-2024 package (UK Parliament, 2026). In the United States, re-entrants are 37–49% of new hires and "one-third to one-half of each year's supply" (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). Every programme in the corpus operates on top of this flow, and none can separate itself from it. That is what makes additionality unmeasurable in practice and what makes GBP 37,400 the right number rather than GBP 17,800.

*Reframing that follows.* The policy question is not "how do we create re-entry", because re-entry happens anyway at scale. It is "at the margin, can policy change the *volume* or the *destination* of a flow it did not cause?" On volume, the corpus says: not measurably, except at prices like North Carolina's. On destination, the corpus is more encouraging, and this is where the paper should place its constructive weight. North Carolina's returners went disproportionately to high-need schools with no mandate. China conditions its CNY 20,000 allowance on service in poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority, old revolutionary base and border counties (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023). Florida conditioned incentives on shortage subjects (Feng and Sass, 2018). Portugal narrowed its supplement in July 2026 to eight shortage zones after conceding the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026). Sorting is cheap, measurable and defensible; volume is none of the three.

*What the paper should say.* Judge re-entry instruments on where they send people, not on how many they produce — and say that this is a deliberate retreat from a volume claim the evidence will not support.

### Tension I. Temporary instruments for durable decisions

*The tension.* New Zealand's Teacher Education Refresh waiver was repeatedly extended and then effectively mainstreamed into the Teaching Council's registration policy (Ministry of Education, 2026a; Teaching Council, 2026). Ireland's suspension of career-break substitution restrictions has been renewed annually for, in the instrument's own words, "the past number of school years" (TC/IN 0005/2025). Michigan's loosening sunsets on 10 October 2028; Virginia's provisions on 1 July 2028; Georgia's HB 385 sunset in June 2026 and was extended to 2030. A returner weighing a multi-year decision faces an instrument with a one-to-five-year life.

*Why it cannot be resolved on our evidence.* If re-entry is a structural channel, its rules should be permanent, and annual waivers returners cannot plan around are self-defeating. If it is an emergency valve, permanence is the mistake, and the Ontario Teachers' Federation is right that a pension plan is not a labour-market tool. Our corpus contains both framings and no basis for choosing between them. Resch's title poses the question exactly — untapped reserve or emergency response? — and the corpus does not answer it.

*What the paper should say.* Force the choice on the policymaker rather than dodging it: an instrument justified as barrier removal should be permanent and evaluated as regulation; an instrument justified as crisis cover should be explicitly sunset, counted, and reviewed at arm's length before renewal. What no system should do is run a crisis instrument on an annual renewal cycle for five years while describing it as structural reform.

## WHERE OUR OWN PREMISE IS STRAINED — stated plainly for the lead author

1. **"Former teachers matter per se for what they bring back."** No causal evidence supports the craft claim; the single direct test is a WWC null; the two positive randomised trials concern tutoring roles, and one of them is not about former teachers at all. Keep the premise as a claim about a distinct policy population defined by distinct legal barriers. Do not let it carry an effectiveness claim.

2. **"Re-entry is a modest margin of the shortage response."** Our evidence supports something more uncomfortable than modest. In volume terms, the measured yield of re-entry *policy* — as distinct from re-entry itself, which happens at scale unprogrammed — is within the noise of the unprogrammed flow almost everywhere it has been counted, and is unknown almost everywhere else. The paper will be stronger, not weaker, for saying that the defensible value of re-entry policy lies in barrier removal and in sorting, and that its volume contribution is unproven.

3. **"Cases are evidence, never the organising unit."** Compatible with this analysis, but note the exposure: four of the propositions above rest on a single case each — England on cost, Portugal on the retention-versus-re-entry asymmetry, Ontario on displacement, North Carolina on price response — and in each instance that case is the only observation of its kind in the world. The paper should be candid that its comparative architecture is built on a handful of observations rather than implying a literature.

4. **"This is a policy paper for policymakers."** The strongest recommendation this angle supports is partly a research recommendation: build the returner register, mandate the uptake count, run the difference-in-differences on the staggered United States return-to-work reforms, and randomise support among registrants next time there are 5,729 of them and money for 354. The paper should own that openly rather than dress it as a policy action. The OECD has already supplied the sentence that licenses it (OECD, 2024, p. 51).

5. **One framing correction the corpus supports and the draft has not used.** The paper should stop treating attrition as a crisis needing a rescue and treat it as a flow needing management. Teacher turnover is comparable to nursing and accountancy and has been broadly stable for decades (Harris and Adams, 2007; Aldeman and Yi, 2025); only 17% of the United States 2007-08 beginning cohort had left teaching by year five, against the 40–50% figure that circulates (Gray and Taie, 2015); and the OECD notes that "in most countries there are pathways for teachers to return to the profession if they wish" (OECD, 2025, *EAG 2025*, p. 495). A paper that motivates re-entry with crisis language inherits every objection to the crisis framing. A paper that treats movement in and out of teaching as a normal feature of a modern professional labour market — which is also *EPO 2024*'s framing, that "not all types of departures should be viewed as problematic" (p. 50) — can argue for re-entry infrastructure on its own terms and survive the scrutiny.

## CANDIDATE PROPOSITIONS FOR THE PAPER

1. **Re-entry policy earns its place through barrier removal, not through programmes:** the only cost-per-additional-returner estimate in existence, GBP 37,400 in England, exceeds the cost of training a new teacher, while the instruments with real take-up — North Carolina's abatement window, New Mexico's uncapped route, New Zealand's re-certification waiver — are rule changes that cost nothing per head and reach the whole eligible population without a funnel.

2. **At equal money, preventing an exit beats reversing one, because re-entry pays to undo a transition that has already been consumed:** Portugal ran both margins under one decree and drew 63 returners against 200 places while some 2,200 teachers took the stay-on supplement — an asymmetry driven by switching costs and instrument complexity, and bounded by the fact that a retirement can only be postponed once.

3. **The reserve measures how badly systems keep the teachers they trained, not how many they can get back:** Michigan's 61,252 certified non-teachers dissolve under the report's own filters, DUO states that the Dutch reserve "does not solve the teacher shortage" and is shrinking, and the share of leavers who ever return has fallen from about a third to about a fifth across United States cohorts.

4. **Re-entry without a change in the job recruits people back into the hazard rate that produced them:** English returners leave at a rate 60% above average after their first year back and retain worse than newly qualified teachers, while leavers' satisfaction advantage over stayers grows with time out — so the policy-relevant price is per additional teacher-*year*, which no system reports.

5. **Because the yield is small, the displacement risk is small — and the paper cannot claim otherwise on both counts:** Ontario cut its re-employment cap in 2012 after documenting CAD 108.3 million a year spent on retired supply teachers while new graduates went short, then found only about 60 of 142,000 retired members interested when it raised the cap again, which is the same number read twice.

6. **The instrument set is shaped by measurement capacity rather than by the shape of the problem:** retirees absorb almost every re-entry instrument in the corpus because they are the only pool systems without registers can see, yet resignations account for over 80% of departures in the systems with the worst shortages — which is why building the returner register precedes, rather than follows, legislating for one.
