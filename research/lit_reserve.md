# Literature review — The potential reserve of qualified former teachers (feeds Chapters 2–3)

Prepared 4 August 2026. All statistics below were verified against the cited source during this review unless marked [UNVERIFIED]. OECD author-year conventions; full bibliography in section (d).

## (a) Key claims, with supporting citations and exact statistics

### Claim 1. In countries with register data, the pool of qualified people outside education far exceeds the teacher shortage — the Netherlands is the benchmark case

- **The 2024 paper the team's Excel refers to is Somers, M., L. Fleck, W. Groot and F. van Merode (2024), *The Hidden Reserve of Nurses and Teachers in the Netherlands*, ROA Research Memorandum 004E, Maastricht University (DOI: 10.26481/umaror.2024004E), published 30 October 2024.** A companion Dutch-language article by the same team appeared earlier: Somers, Groot and van Merode (2024), "Stille reserve voldoende om personeelstekort onderwijs op te lossen", *ESB*, 28 February 2024.
- Using CBS register data on everyone who obtained a teaching qualification **between 2000 and 2021**, the ESB article puts the hidden reserve ("stille reserve") at **just over 62,000 people** holding a teaching qualification but working in another sector, unemployed/on benefits, or in further study (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024).
- Against this stands a shortage of **9,800 FTE in primary education (9.7% of the workforce) and 3,800 FTE in secondary education (5.8%)** — 13,600 FTE combined at end-2023 (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024; DutchNews, 2024). The reserve is thus **roughly 4.5 times the combined shortage** in headcount terms.
- Quotable core result: **"If 22 per cent of this hidden reserve decided to work full-time in education again, the shortage would be completely solved"** (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024, ESB).
- Composition (share of post-2000 qualification holders not working in education): **19.8% of primary-qualified, 22.7% of secondary-qualified** work in other sectors; a further **7.4% (primary) and 10.2% (secondary)** are unemployed or on benefits (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024, ESB).
- The survey component (ROA memorandum) finds inactive teachers **rate several working conditions more favourably than active teachers do**, and that what would bring them back is **lower work pressure, more control over working hours, autonomy — more than salary** (Somers et al., 2024).
- Lead researcher Melline Somers stresses the estimate is a **lower bound**: "because of a data set limit we could only count the people who qualified after 2000" (DutchNews, 2024).

### Claim 2. The Dutch statistical agency's own register series confirms a large reserve but shows it is *shrinking* — and warns it does not mechanically solve the shortage

- DUO (the Dutch education executive agency) published its own register-based estimate on **28 May 2026**: a stille reserve (defined as qualified people **not working in the sector for at least 2 years**, excluding agency workers, retirees and the incapacitated) of **34,000 people in primary education and 64,000 in secondary/vocational (vo/mbo)** on 1 October 2024 — equal to **22% of all qualified primary teachers and 43% of all qualified secondary/vocational teachers** (DUO, 2026).
- The reserve **fell by 17% (primary) and 18% (secondary/vocational) between 1 October 2015 and 1 October 2024** — i.e. shortages have already been pulling people back in, or fewer qualified people are parked outside (DUO, 2026).
- DUO's explicit caveat: the reserve **"does not, however, solve the teacher shortage"** (shortage: 7,900 FTE primary, 3,800 FTE secondary at the October 2025 reference) (DUO, 2026).

### Claim 3. Michigan: the certified-but-not-teaching stock is enormous relative to measured vacancies — but the survey layer rests on a 30% response rate

- **The reports the Excel refers to are by Jim Lindsay, Natalya Gnedko-Berry and Carol Wan (REL Midwest at American Institutes for Research): *Michigan Teachers Who Are Not Teaching: Who Are They, and What Would Motivate Them to Teach?*, REL 2021-076, April 2021**, with an accompanying Study Snapshot (ED611792) and infographic. The survey itself was administered by the Michigan Department of Education between **5 December 2019 and 10 January 2020**. Note: I could not identify a *separately published* "2020 preliminary findings" document; the 2020 date most plausibly refers to preliminary results circulated before the April 2021 report (see caveats).
- Stock: of 141,810 certified teachers in the analysis sample, **61,252 (about 61,000) were not teaching in a Michigan public school in 2017/18**, versus 80,558 who were — i.e. **43% of the viable certified stock was outside the public school classroom** (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, p. 4–5).
- Of the non-teaching, **70% had not taught in a public school at any point 2013/14–2017/18**; **93% were younger than 60** (retirement age); 76% were women; 72% were 35 or older; 57% were certified for elementary grades; 11% were from racial/ethnic minority groups. Among those not teaching, **44–49% held expired certificates** (vs 6% of active teachers) (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, Table 1).
- Scale vs need: the largest endorsement-area shortages in 2018/19 were career and technical education (966 vacancies), special education (677), elementary (539), science (192), art/music (174) — i.e. **the certified non-teaching stock is more than 20 times the sum of the listed vacancy counts**, though certification mix, expired certificates and location blunt the comparison (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, p. 1).
- Survey findings (motivations): **33% selected wanting a higher salary** among their three most important reasons for not teaching; 22% had become administrators/instructional leaders; ~10% could not obtain a full-time position (53% among those who had *never* taught). An increase in salary was the most frequently selected incentive to return; allowing **retirees to keep retirement benefits**, better benefits, and student-loan forgiveness also ranked highly (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, Figure 1).
- **The 30% response-rate caveat the team flagged is real and stated in the report**: 59,433 surveyed, **17,551 responses (a 30 per cent response rate)**, analytic sample 9,842 after screening; the report says the sample **"is not representative of Michigan certified teachers who do not teach"** (it under-represents minority teachers and over-represents older, earlier-certified respondents) and that unweighted percentages **"should be interpreted with caution"** (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, Box 2 and note 5). A further 16% of the 61,252 may live 20+ miles outside Michigan (note 2).

### Claim 4. England: ~366,000 people with qualified teacher status were not teaching in state schools (2018), and returners are a routine, sizeable supply flow — yet targeted returner programmes converted very few

- Stock: **"According to the DfE, in 2018 there were 260,000 qualified teachers of working age who were out of service but had previously been in service and a further 106,000 teachers with qualified teacher status who have never been in service"** (Worth, 2023, footnote 2, citing DfE). DfE publishes the underlying tables ("Qualified teachers out of service"; "Qualified teachers never in service") from the Database of Qualified Teachers and pension records in the School Workforce in England series. For scale, this ≈366,000 pool compares with a qualified state-school workforce of roughly 450,000 FTE.
- Flows (natural return happens continuously): in 2024/25, **41,700 FTE teachers entered the state-funded sector (9.2% of all qualified teachers), of whom 17,000 (41%) were newly qualified; "returners have increased"** while other entrant categories fell; **40,800 FTE left in 2023/24 (9.0%)** (DfE, 2025). In the year to November 2023, entrants were 44,002 FTE (17,462 newly qualified — lowest since 2010), leavers 43,522 FTE, with a **record-high 8.8% leaving for reasons other than retirement and a record-low 0.7% retirement rate** (Schools Week, 2024, reporting DfE data). [UNVERIFIED: a precise FTE count of the "returners" category for 2024/25 (~17,000 has been reported) could not be confirmed in the published headline text; the exact figure is in DfE's "qualified entrants by type" underlying table.]
- Policy conversion is hard: the DfE's **Return to Teaching pilot (2015–16)** attracted **5,729 registrations of interest**, supported **354** returners, and by November 2016 only **27 (8%)** were identified in permanent posts teaching EBacc subjects in state-funded secondary schools, at an estimated **£37,400 per additional returner**; NFER judged it **"unlikely to represent good value for money"** (Buchanan et al., 2018). NFER's typology found "career breakers" returned at about twice the rate of other returner types.
- NFER's broader verdict on the reserve: schools also hire movers and returners, but survey evidence suggests **"there is not such a ready supply of other sources of teachers"** waiting to fill gaps left by under-recruitment into training (Worth, 2023, p. 9).

### Claim 5. Australia: about one in five registered teachers is not working in a school — and over half of them say they would consider returning

- In 2023 Australia had about **553,300 registered teachers**; roughly **81% were deployed in schools** (AITSL, 2025).
- Among non-deployed survey respondents not on leave, **over 50% had left the profession three or more years earlier but remained registered**; **over half of non-teaching registered teachers said they would consider returning "under the right circumstances", and 5% were actively looking to return** (AITSL, 2025).
- Complementary evidence on who leaves and where they go: a 2024 survey of 256 former teachers across all states/sectors (left 2016–2024) finds many kept ties to education (team's Excel "2024 Paper"); and 20 years of HILDA panel data show leavers gain job satisfaction, leisure and flexibility **without meaningful wage gains**, pointing to workload/conditions, not pay, as the binding constraint (team's Excel "2025 Paper"; both retained here as the team's sources — full citations to be pinned down by the attrition-chapter reviewer).

### Claim 6. United States: re-entrants are a structurally important supply channel, but the share of leavers who ever come back is modest — and appears to be falling

- **Re-entrants made up 37–49% of new hires** in recent years, and "one-third to one-half of each year's supply", but **"estimates suggest only around a third of teachers who exit the profession ever return"** (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016, pp. 3, and re-entrants section).
- Grissom and Reininger (2012), using the NLSY79, established the reentry benchmark — earlier cohort studies put eventual return at **one-quarter to one-half of leavers** — and found returners are disproportionately **younger, more experienced, better paid pre-exit, and female (but less likely to return with young children at home)**.
- Moyer (2022), replicating with the NLSY97, finds **only about one-fifth of teachers who exited between 2000 and 2019 ever returned** — substantially below earlier cohorts — and no longer finds gender/child-rearing gradients.
- Stinebrickner (2002), the team's "2002 Paper" (NLS-72 cohort of certified female teachers): **about 67% of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely** rather than switching employer; a newborn child is the single strongest exit determinant — i.e. much of the reserve was never "poached", it stepped out of work.
- Brummet et al. (2024), linking district records to IRS/Census data (2003/04–2014/15): **more than half of leavers remain in education (broadly defined), about a fifth exit the labour force entirely, and fewer than a quarter take jobs outside education**; mean earnings remain slightly **below** pre-exit earnings even 8 years later.
- Stated willingness: in the last national Teacher Follow-up Survey with this item, **about half of those who left teaching said they would consider returning** to preK-12 teaching (NCES 2013, as cited in Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, p. 3).
- Retirees respond to rules: under North Carolina's 1999–2009 return-to-work policy (full salary + pension), take-up rose until **just over 35% of previously retirement-eligible teachers returned to full-time work** in 2009; the 2,694 returnees (81% female, disproportionately Black, 31 years' average experience) were **disproportionately hired by high-need schools**, and returns **dropped to zero when the policy expired in 2010** (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024).

### Claim 7. Germany and Austria: no register-based reserve statistics — policy leans on retirees, whose numbers are large but whose activation is small

- Germany has **no published register count of qualified teachers working outside schools** (data gap). What exists: **457,000 retired/pensioned teachers (2021)**; **13 of 16 Länder actively recruit retired teachers**, yet only **just over 5,000 pensioners were teaching nationwide — under 1% of the ~800,000-strong workforce** — against a KMK-estimated shortage of ~25,000 teachers by 2025. North Rhine-Westphalia employed 1,304 teachers past pension age (2022); Hesse wrote to ~7,000 retirees in one year; Schleswig-Holstein counted 390 "senior teachers" by end-2022/23 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).
- Austria: Resch (2025), the team's "2025 Paper", asks in its title whether retired returners are **"an untapped reserve or emergency response"**; based on qualitative interviews (n = 14, retirees and experts), it finds post-retirement return decisions are complex (individual, family, school-level and system factors) and that **keeping professional contact with the former school is what converts availability into return**; pension and service-crediting rules are flagged as a federal-level barrier.

### Claim 8. The macro backdrop: shortages have widened across the OECD, which is what makes the reserve question policy-relevant

- Across the OECD, **the share of students whose principals reported teacher shortages rose from 29% in 2015 to 46.7% in 2022** (OECD, 2024).
- France illustrates the data gap outside register countries: the cumul emploi-retraite framework lets retired teachers return (team's Excel, legislation sheet), and DEPP tracks exits and transitions (Feuillet and Prouteau, 2020), but **no published estimate of the qualified-but-inactive pool exists** [no source found — treat as a genuine gap, not an omission].

## (b) Cross-country contrasts (the paper's premise: answers depend on context)

| Dimension | Netherlands | England | Michigan (US) | Australia | Germany/Austria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reserve measured how | CBS/DUO registers (economy-wide employment) | DQT + pension records (out of service / never in service) | Certification files + employment records | Registration data + workforce survey | Not measured (only retiree stocks) |
| Size | ~62,000 (post-2000 qualifiers only); DUO 2026: 34,000 po + 64,000 vo/mbo | ~260,000 out of service + ~106,000 never in service (2018, working age) | ~61,000 certified not teaching (2017/18) | ~19% of 553,300 registered not in schools | 457,000 retirees (DE, 2021) |
| Relative to shortage | ~4.5× the 13,600 FTE shortage; 22% activation closes gap | Vastly exceeds any vacancy measure; but NFER: "no ready supply" | >20× listed endorsement-area vacancies | n/a (no single national vacancy count in ATWD) | Retiree activation <1% of workforce vs ~25,000 gap |
| Reserve trend | Shrinking: −17%/−18% over 2015–2024 (DUO) | Out-of-service inflow at record high (8.8% non-retirement leaving rate, 2023) | n/a (single snapshot) | n/a | n/a |
| What would bring them back | Workload, control over hours, autonomy > pay (ROA survey) | Flexibility/part-time; conversion costly (£37,400/returner in pilot) | Salary first (33%), then admin roles, flexibility; retiree pension rules | "Right circumstances": >50% would consider; 5% actively looking | Retirees: pension/earning rules, contact with former school |
| Natural return rate | n/a (not estimated) | Returners a routine, large entrant category (≈2nd largest after NQTs); increased in 2024/25 | US-wide: ~⅓ ever return (older cohorts ¼–½; 2000–2019 cohort ~⅕) | n/a | n/a |

Three contrasts worth stating explicitly in the chapter:

1. **Measurement drives the story.** Register countries (NL, England, Michigan, Australia) can *see* a reserve several times the shortage; Germany, Austria and France cannot, so their policy debate defaults to the only visible pool — retirees. The Dutch reserve is 4.5× the shortage, but under DUO's stricter definition (≥2 years out, retirees excluded) the *secondary* reserve equals 43% of all qualified secondary teachers while the *primary* reserve is only 22% — same country, same year, different denominators.
2. **Optimists vs sceptics.** Dutch economists frame the reserve as sufficient to close the shortage (22% activation); English evidence is the antidote: an out-of-service stock near 366,000 coexisted with a costly pilot that converted 27 people into permanent shortage-subject posts, and NFER concludes there is "not a ready supply". The US longitudinal literature sits in between: reentry is a major hiring channel (37–49% of hires) precisely because the stock is huge, even though only ~⅓ (falling to ~⅕) of individual leavers ever return.
3. **What the reserve says it wants differs by system.** Michigan respondents lead with salary (33%); Dutch inactive teachers lead with workload, hours-control and autonomy and already rate conditions outside education more favourably; Australian non-deployed registrants signal broad conditional willingness (>50%). Any re-entry policy transplant needs to respect this heterogeneity.

## (c) Caveats and limitations

1. **Michigan (team flag confirmed).** The 30% survey response rate (17,551/59,433) is stated in the report itself; the analytic sample (9,842) is explicitly non-representative and percentages are unweighted. The administrative stock (61,252) is solid, but as a *viable* reserve it is overstated: 44–49% hold expired certificates, ~16% may live out of state, 22% of respondents are now administrators, and certification mix does not match vacancy subjects. The team's Excel comment that the study covers "pre-K12" is correct (preK-12 public schools only; private-school teachers count as "not teaching").
2. **Michigan "2020 Preliminary Findings" (Excel source label).** No separately published 2020 preliminary-findings document could be located; the survey ran Dec 2019–Jan 2020 and the published outputs are the April 2021 report (REL 2021-076), a Study Snapshot (ED611792) and an infographic. Suggest the Excel either cite the snapshot or drop the 2020 item. [Flagged as likely Excel inaccuracy.]
3. **Netherlands.** The 62,000 covers only people qualified 2000–2021 (undercount of the true reserve; Somers calls figures "likely higher"). The ROA survey component is descriptive; the team's own quality note (Pepe: "administrative data good, analysis surprisingly poor") should temper how much analytical weight the paper places on the survey regressions, as opposed to the register counts. Also note the ROA "hidden reserve" concept in places includes part-timers who could extend hours — not identical to DUO's definition.
4. **England.** The 260,000/106,000 figures are DfE 2018 stock estimates from pension/qualification databases; they include people far from the classroom for decades and are not published on a consistent annual basis in the current release. Entrants/leavers are FTE, not headcount, and exclude working-pattern changes. The exact returner FTE for 2024/25 was not verifiable in the headline release text (underlying table required).
5. **Return-rate estimates are cohort- and design-specific.** Stinebrickner (2002) uses a 1970s–80s cohort (team flag correct: maternity/parental-leave changes since then limit external validity); Grissom-Reininger (NLSY79) vs Moyer (NLSY97) differ in window length as well as cohort. "Ever returns" also depends on observation horizon.
6. **Reserve ≠ supply.** Both DUO (2026) and Worth (2023) warn explicitly against reading the stock as available capacity: subject mix, geography, expired credentials, current earnings, and preferences all shrink the effective pool; the England RTT pilot and Portugal's 2024 retiree scheme (80 applicants for ~200 openings; Excel programs sheet) show conversion is the binding constraint.
7. **Excel data-quality notes (as requested).**
   - Research row 9 is truncated: country reads "International: USA," with empty short/long fields, and the comment ends mid-sentence ("...however.."). The intended 2025 international causal-evidence review could not be identified from the fragment — needs the team to restore the full row.
   - Michigan research row: sources list "2020 Preliminary Findings" (see caveat 2).
   - Programs sheet: "NSW Goverment Announcement" (typo); several long descriptions read as edited summaries rather than source quotes — fine, but they should not be cited verbatim.
   - Netherlands research row is accurate on substance (register data; conditions-over-pay finding) and matches Somers et al. (2024).
   - The `quality_jose`/`comments_jose` columns are empty throughout.
