# Adversarial critique of `arg_mechanism.md`

*Hostile reading of "The mechanics of return: where the pathway actually breaks". Every counter-claim below is traceable to the same verified corpus the memo uses; where I compute, I flag it as own arithmetic. Nothing here is drawn from memory or the web. The memo is a serious piece of work — its §0 warning about incommensurable denominators and its §9 "what would settle it" are the best thinking anyone on this project has produced. The problem is that the memo violates its own §0 warning in four of its five candidate propositions, and that its two boldest mechanisms (the seniority trap and the relational transaction) are contradicted by evidence sitting inside the files it cites.*

---

## Front 1. Claims that outrun their evidence

### 1.1 The order-of-magnitude comparison in Proposition 2 is arithmetically wrong, and the memo's own §2 contains the correction

The memo writes:

> "NSW's cold list … produced 145 placements from 1,500 contacts — 9.7% of everyone contacted … England's self-selected volunteers produced 62 state-school posts from 5,729 registrations — 1.1% … A cold call beat a national advertising campaign by roughly nine to one per head of the pool."

The 62 did not come from the 5,729. It came from the 354 who were supported (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 11–12; `cases_england_nz` §1.4). The other ~5,375 registrants are not a zero: the evaluation records that "around 4% of *non-supported* registrants returned over the same period" — which the memo itself invokes one paragraph earlier to argue that "the untouched pool produced several times more returners than the funded one," putting the figure "on the order of 210." Add them back and England's registration pool produced roughly 272 returns against 5,729 registrations, or about 4.7% (own arithmetic from Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 11–12). Against NSW's 9.7%, the ratio is about two to one, not nine to one.

Two to one is also flattered by the outcome definitions. NSW's 145 are placements in **casual and temporary** work (NSW Government, 2024a; `cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.2); England's 62 are **state-funded teaching posts**, of which 27 are permanent EBacc secondary posts. If you compare like with like — England's permanent shortage-subject posts against NSW's casual days — the English number is 27 and the New South Wales number is unstated. The memo concedes this in its "what strains against" paragraph ("the comparison rewards NSW partly for having asked less") and then carries the uncorrected nine-to-one into Proposition 2 anyway. A well-informed policymaker with the NFER report open will find this in ten minutes, and it is the kind of error that discredits the surrounding argument rather than just the sentence.

There is a third composition problem the memo does not raise. England's registration list was open to people "who qualified but never taught," primary-trained teachers, independent- and FE-sector teachers, overseas-trained QTS holders and supply teachers without a contract (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 95; `cases_england_nz` §1.2). A large share of the 5,729 were never candidates for a permanent EBacc secondary post. NSW's list was the Department's own payroll record of people who had held exactly the jobs on offer. The difference between the two funnels is therefore substantially a difference in **who was allowed into the denominator**, not in outreach technique — which is a better argument than the one the memo makes, and one that survives.

### 1.2 "Thirty-five times more effective" (Portugal) compares a capped instrument with an uncapped one, across two disjoint populations

The memo calls Portugal "the corpus's best natural experiment," specifying "one decree, one year, one government, **one population of pension-eligible teachers**, two margins," and concludes that "an offer that required no transition was thirty-five times more effective than one that required a full re-entry."

Three defects, all fatal to the ratio:

- **The populations are disjoint by law.** Article 5 is open only to those *already retired*, and retired for five years or less (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5(2); `fix_austria_portugal` D1.1). Article 6 pays teachers who have *reached retirement conditions but have not retired* (Art. 6). No individual could choose between them. This is not two margins offered to one population; it is two instruments offered to two populations, one of which has already revealed a preference for exit.
- **Article 5 was capped at 200 places** by government quota (DN, 2024; Renascença, 2024b). Even a stampede could not have produced more than 200 returners. The maximum observable ratio was therefore 11:1, not 35:1, and the ratio the memo reports is partly a report of the government's own quota-setting.
- **The offers differ on at least four dimensions at once**, not one. Article 6: stay in your own school, keep your own timetable, EUR 750 flat per month. Article 5: be ranked in a central DGAE pool by your professional graduation mark at retirement, be placed *where the shortage is*, on a fixed-term contract, at pay indexed to the **first step** of the career scale and pro-rated by hours (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Money, geography, tenure and status all move together. The memo names one of them — "the friction being priced is the transition itself, not the classroom" — and treats the other three as if they had been held constant.

The defensible version of the Portugal finding is the one already agreed in the corpus: "at equal money, preventing an exit beats reversing one" (`ch5_synthesis`, Lesson 3), stated as a *direction* with the caveat that the two arms were not offered to the same people and that the returner arm was quota-capped. The memo should also note, since it wants the natural-experiment framing, that the application window ran 30 September–11 October 2024 — twelve days — for classes beginning in January (Renascença, 2024a; 2024b). A twelve-day window is an administrative-access fact, not a preference revelation.

### 1.3 "Interest exceeds conversion at roughly ten to one" is contradicted by the memo's own funnels

> "In Australia, more than half of registered teachers not working in schools said they would consider returning 'under the right circumstances', while 5% were actively looking (AITSL, 2025). That ten-to-one ratio between conditional willingness and revealed search is roughly the ratio the programme funnels then reproduce."

The funnels do not reproduce it. England: 5,729 registrations to 62 state-school posts is 92:1 (own arithmetic). New South Wales: 260 expressions of interest to 145 placements is 1.8:1 (NSW Government, 2024a). Georgia's audit reports the opposite problem — 65% of surveyed systems planned to use the law and 45% said its provisions had blocked a hire they wanted to make (Georgia DOAA, 2025), which is unmet employer demand, not unmet worker interest. The 10:1 figure is a real and useful fact about Australian stated preference; presenting it as the ratio the funnels "then reproduce" converts a coincidence into a law and invites a reviewer to check.

### 1.4 "Every system that has solved the school's cost problem has done so by making the returner poorer" — and North Carolina, the memo's own exemplar, did not

The memo's §5 claim is that a returner priced on a seniority scale is unaffordable to the school, so systems either impoverish the returner or fail. It then says of North Carolina: "The school got a teacher at schedule cost; the teacher got a premium; the pension fund financed the gap."

But schedule cost for a North Carolina returner *is* the expensive hire the memo says schools avoid. The returners averaged 31 years' experience (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024, via `lit_reserve` Claim 6) — they sat at the top of the state schedule, and 52% of North Carolina schools (over 1,500) hired at least one anyway. The pension annuity did not reduce what the school paid; it raised what the *teacher* received. So North Carolina is not a case of a third party paying the seniority premium. It is a case of schools cheerfully paying full veteran salaries, in volume, for a decade — which is a direct counter-example to the mechanism the memo built the section around.

The same section says Georgia's audit "puts a number on it: rehired retirees earned an average of USD 78,487 in FY2023-24." The audit reports no novice comparator, so the figure sizes nothing; and the audit's own account of what deterred hiring names something else entirely: "the 30-year and one-year-break rules and the high-need-subject methodology were the main barriers" (Georgia DOAA, 2025; `cases_us_legislation` §2). The single piece of **employer-side** evidence in the whole corpus was asked why it did not hire returners, and it did not say salary.

### 1.5 "No system in the corpus has been shown to" extend induction to returners — one did, by law

The memo's §7 policy implication: "Induction must be extended to returners, as *Teachers Matter* recommended in 2005 … and as no system in the corpus has been shown to do."

The Dutch *Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs* was precisely that: Article 3 makes the subsidy "a contribution to the board's costs of **induction, guidance and support** of the returner — not a signing bonus to the teacher" (Staatscourant 2017, No. 62002; `fix_netherlands_more_cases` §1.1). New Zealand's returners agree a "Return to Practice Plan," now the "Kia Maia | Future-Ready Teaching Plan," with their principal (Teaching Council, 2025; 2026). Both facts are in files the memo cites for other purposes.

This is worse than a slip, because the Dutch instrument is the memo's own evidence for a *different* proposition. Section 1 reads the low take-up of the herintreders subsidy as proof that "the loss sits at the employer's door" and that "EUR 2,500 does not buy what schools actually lack." If EUR 2,500 for returner induction went unclaimed, then the memo's headline §7 recommendation — fund returner induction — has already been tried in the corpus and had a take-up problem. The memo cannot use the same fact as evidence of employer failure in §1 and forget it in §7.

### 1.6 "Returns dropped to zero when the window closed" is a definitional zero, not a price response

> "Note too what happened when the window closed in 2010: returns dropped to zero. This was a price response, not a change of heart."

When the North Carolina return-to-work policy expired in autumn 2009 the *category* ceased to exist: retirees reverted to the baseline rule, under which part-time return was permitted with earnings capped at half the previous full-time salary, and full-time return suspended pension and retiree health benefits (`cases_us_legislation` §1). Returns "under the policy" necessarily went to zero because there was no longer a policy to return under. What the corpus does not report is how many retirees returned under the reverted baseline rules. Until that number exists, "returns dropped to zero" measures the abolition of a reporting category. The memo is entitled to say the window's closure removed a large financial inducement; it is not entitled to read the zero as an elasticity.

### 1.7 The Australian satisfaction result is estimated on a sample that excludes the reserve

Section 8's central empirical support is that leavers "are, on their own accounts, better off": "job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations higher one year after exit and 0.19 higher after three years, while weekly wages fall 6.2%" (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025).

The verified methods note says the multivariate analysis runs on "895 teachers (8,048 observations), **excluding 92 who left permanently and never returned**" (`fix_hilda_fleck` §A2). The estimation sample is therefore composed overwhelmingly of people who left and came back. The finding is a real and important one about *temporary* exit — it may even be the single best piece of evidence in the corpus for the career-breaker segment — but it cannot carry the weight the memo puts on it, which is a claim about the standing reserve of people who did not come back. The memo should either re-scope the claim to leavers-who-return (where it is strong, and where it explains why NFER's career breakers convert at double the rate) or find other support for the reserve-is-content claim. Brummet et al. (2024) — "mean earnings still slightly below pre-exit levels eight years later" — is closer to what it needs and does not depend on this sample.

### 1.8 Smaller overreaches, listed for the editorial pass

- **"+0.34 on a mathematics assessment" attributed to retirees.** Number Rockets' 86 tutors were "recruited from local pools of **retired and substitute** teachers" (Gersten et al., 2015; `ch5_synthesis` Lesson 9). The corpus nowhere reports the split, and there is no arm using non-retiree tutors. The trial evidences the *programme*, not a retiree premium; `lit_retiree_mentoring` §(c)2 says as much: "no meta-analysis isolates 'retired educator' as a tutor class."
- **Experience Corps as evidence about former teachers.** Its volunteers are aged 50+, "many former educators" (`lit_retiree_mentoring` §A4) — not a former-teacher sample.
- **Aurora presented as a flat null.** True of the WWC 2019 characterisation, which the memo cites correctly. But `lit_retiree_mentoring` §(c) and `cases_mentoring` §2 both instruct that the underlying RCT's significant year-1 mathematics effect and the WWC's indeterminate rating "should appear together." Reporting only the null in a paper that wants to recommend retiree roles is selective in the direction that hurts the paper.
- **"Registrations of interest ran at 5,729 against a target of about 3,000, and the evaluators list 'demand vastly exceeded expectations' among the positive findings."** Accurate. But the same page reports that "national advertising played a small role relative to online search" (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 10–12) — which the memo uses in §2 to argue campaigns are ineffective, while using the 5,729 in §1 to argue awareness was not the constraint. Both cannot be free.

## Front 2. Explanations that are really redescriptions

### 2.1 "Identity" (§8) names the residual; it does not explain it

The memo's most ambitious mechanism: "Interest is an expression of professional identity and costs nothing; appointment is a labour-market transaction … These people are not deciding whether to accept a wage; they are deciding whether to resume being a particular kind of person."

Strip the prose and the argument is: many say yes, few act; therefore saying yes must be about something other than acting; call that something identity. Nothing in the corpus measures identity independently of the gap it is invoked to explain. The two supports offered are (i) NFER's segment *label* "idealists, 35%" — a name given to a cluster in a survey, not a measurement — and (ii) Resch's characterisation of return as "a biographical event," which rests on nine episodic interviews, six of them with returners (`fix_austria_portugal` §A4.3). A construct that is inferred from the outcome and corroborated by a label and nine interviews is a redescription.

The plainer alternative is standard and the corpus supports it directly: hypothetical questions elicit hypothetical answers. AITSL's item is explicitly conditional — "would consider returning **under the right circumstances**" (AITSL, 2025). The Michigan survey drew 17,551 responses from 59,433 (30%) and the report itself states the analytic sample "is not representative" and that unweighted percentages "should be interpreted with caution" (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, Box 2). No identity theory is required to explain why a costless survey tick does not predict a job application. And the memo already knows this: its own §8 test — "measure interest with a costly signal (an application to a named post with a named start date)" — is a stated-versus-revealed-preference test, not an identity test. The section would be stronger and shorter if it dropped the identity vocabulary and kept the measurement critique.

### 2.2 "The design chooses which stage bears the loss" (§1) renames where each system starts counting

Proposition 1 asserts a clean attribution: "England lost its returners at vacancy match, NSW at willingness, Portugal at application, the Netherlands at the school's absorptive capacity, Georgia at statutory eligibility."

Test it against the memo's own text and it dissolves:

- **England.** Section 1 puts the loss at vacancy match and hiring. Section 2 puts it at "who is admitted to the pool" — the entrance. Section 6 puts it at the hiring calendar. Section 7 adds retention. Four stages, one system.
- **Portugal.** Section 1: "the loss is at willingness … Applications were fewer than places." Section 6: the route is "late, impersonal and geographically indifferent, which is close to a design specification for deterring a 68-year-old" — i.e. the loss is at the terms of the offer, which is the same stage the memo assigns to the Netherlands.
- **Netherlands.** "The loss sits at the employer's door" and "EUR 2,500 does not buy what schools actually lack." But the EUR 2,500 was never meant to buy a place: it reimbursed a school board for the costs of inducting someone it had *already* appointed for at least six months (Staatscourant 2017, No. 62002, Arts. 3, 4, 9). Non-claiming of a small retrospective reimbursement by employers is evidence about administrative behaviour, not about absorptive capacity.
- **New South Wales.** "The loss is at the front" — but 17% interest from a cold, unsolicited approach to people who had left is not a loss at all; it is what an unsolicited approach yields. Calling the non-response of people who were not asking for anything a "stage failure" is a category slip.

Deeper: the stage a system appears to fail at is the stage at which it began counting. England began counting at self-selected registration, so its willingness loss is invisible — England had roughly 260,000 out-of-service working-age qualified teachers plus 106,000 never-in-service in 2018 (Worth, 2023, citing DfE), against which 5,729 registrations is 1.6% (own arithmetic). Measured on the same denominator NSW used — the population of former teachers, not the population of volunteers — England's dominant loss is at willingness too. The memo's §0 states this principle exactly ("the published funnels have incommensurable denominators") and then Proposition 1 spends the insight in the wrong direction: instead of concluding that stage attribution is an artefact of measurement, it concludes that stage attribution is a finding.

### 2.3 "Re-entry is a relational transaction, not a market one" (§3) restates a conditional distribution

The load-bearing datum is that "54% of them returned to the school they had taught in before retiring" (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). This is a distribution *conditional on having returned*. It tells us where returners went; it does not tell us that the tie caused the return, and the 46% who went elsewhere returned without one. Nothing in the corpus reports the base rate — what share of any teaching appointment goes to someone who previously worked at that school — so 54% has no comparator.

"Relational, not market" also does no work in the North Carolina case, where the memo's own §5 explains the volume by money: full salary plus pension, "up to roughly 50% more income than a regular full-time teacher." A returner who takes a 50% pay premium and, given the premium, takes it at the school five minutes from home is behaving like a market participant with a location preference. The memo cannot use North Carolina as the proof of the price mechanism in §5 and as the proof that price is not the mechanism in §3.

### 2.4 "Outreach versus attachment" is a distinction without an instrument

Section 3 rescues itself from the German counter-evidence — 13 of 16 Länder recruiting retirees, Hesse writing to ~7,000, just over 5,000 pensioners teaching nationwide (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023) — by declaring: "A mailing is not a relationship. The distinction the paper needs is between *outreach*, which is a ministry broadcasting to a list, and *attachment*, which is a school holding a tie to a named person — and only the second has evidence behind it."

But every case in the corpus with a measured yield is outreach by a system-level body. NSW is the Department writing from departmental staffing records. Vorarlberg's is a Bildungsdirektion service point. Vienna's 300 letters, Salzburg's contacting of every retiree to 67, Hesse's 7,000 — all ministries or directorates. There is **no case in the corpus of a school holding and using its own former-teacher register.** The memo's central recommendation — "the school records the reason, the subject, the preferred future hours and consent to be contacted, and the school — not the ministry — holds and uses that record. Nothing in the corpus costs less or has better odds" — is a claim about odds for an instrument that has no odds, because it has never been observed. That sentence must not survive into a policy paper in that form.

## Front 3. Alternative explanations the analyst did not consider

### 3.1 Outside options by subject — the corpus's own explanation for England, and the memo never uses it

*Teachers Matter* states, at the page the memo cites for something else: "relative salaries influence the decision to return to teaching after a career interruption, as **returning rates are generally higher among those teaching subjects that provide the fewest opportunities for employment elsewhere**" (OECD, 2005, p. 70; `fix_oecd_primary`, E row 1).

This predicts, without any funnel theory, exactly what happened. England's pilot targeted EBacc secondary subjects and its successor narrowed to maths, physics and MFL (NCTL, 2017; `cases_england_nz` §1.6, §2.1) — the subjects with the strongest non-teaching labour markets. The advisory service's worst arm was physics: 23 returners in two years from over 111 physics leads (Walker, 2022). NSW, by contrast, offered casual work across all subjects and levels. Portugal's returners came back in Portuguese (8), History (6) and special education (6) — not in the subjects with the best outside options (Renascença, 2024b).

This is a serious rival to Proposition 1's vacancy-match story and to Proposition 2's entrance story, and it is uncomfortable: it implies that re-entry policy is structurally worst at exactly the shortages governments most want to fix, and that a system can improve its conversion rate simply by targeting subjects where re-entry was going to be easy. Any funnel comparison that does not condition on subject is comparing different labour markets.

### 3.2 New South Wales changed the offer, not the funnel

The memo attributes NSW's conversion entirely to design: recency of the list, casual roles, one-stop re-accreditation. The NSW Government's own release attributes it to something else. The pull factors cited are "system-wide: the October 2023 pay deal (described as the largest teacher pay increase in decades, with removal of the public sector wage cap) and measures to reduce administrative workload," and "the teach.NSW returner page still leads with the pay rise as the incentive" (`cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.2; NSW Government, 2024a; NSW Department of Education, 2026). Over the same window, vacancies fell 20% and then to a three-year low, which the government attributes to the whole package, not to Teachers Re-Engage (`cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.3).

So the case the memo uses to prove that conversion is decided by pool selection and post availability is a case in which the system simultaneously raised pay by the largest margin in decades and cut workload — the two things the leaver literature says drove exit. That is at minimum a confound; read hardest, it is the opposite finding: NSW converted because it changed the job, and the outreach merely harvested a change that had already happened.

There is a second anomaly in NSW the memo smooths. Its stated target group is teachers who "had resigned from the NSW public system in the previous five years," yet **~60% of returners came from retirement** (NSW Government, 2024a; `cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.3). The memo's §4 builds a "flow, not stock" proposition partly on NSW's recency screen, while the majority of NSW's converters were retirees — the pool the memo says depletes.

### 3.3 Ontario 2022: permissiveness with no takers, in the memo's own case file

Section 5 asserts that price is what excludes returners and that the pension system is the decisive institution. Ontario is used for the cost half of the argument (CAD 432.69/day versus CAD 227.36; CAD 108.3 million a year) — data from a 2010 Globe and Mail investigation of 2008-09 spending.

The memo does not report what happened when Ontario removed the constraint. The cap was raised from 50 to 95 days for 2020-21 through 2022-23; the OTF "cautioned it did not expect that many retirees are interested — only **about 60 of 142,000 retired members** had expressed interest since September 2021 — and noted the average age of retirees was 72" (Global News, 2022; `cases_other_legislation` §2.4). That is 0.04% (own arithmetic). Ontario is thus simultaneously the memo's proof that returners are too expensive for schools and a demonstration that when the pension penalty is lifted, essentially nobody comes. New Mexico says the same in a different register: 154 users of the new no-caps route in year one against roughly 48,000 potentially eligible retirees, or 0.3% (NMERB, 2023; NM HED, 2022 — own arithmetic). The memo mentions New Mexico under "what strains against"; it does not mention Ontario 2022 at all, and Ontario is the case it relies on most.

The alternative explanation is age and life stage. Austria's returners were mostly retirees; Portugal's had an average age of about 68 (Renascença, 2024b); Ontario's retired members average 72; Japan reports that only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools now seek full-time re-employment, and that ageing adjuncts increasingly decline the most demanding replacement work (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203). A pool whose median member is in their late sixties or seventies has a low ceiling on full-time labour supply at any price. That is a mechanism, it is sized in the corpus, and the memo does not consider it.

### 3.4 Austria's tapering returns: emergency, not depletion

Section 4 reads the Austrian pattern — 1,062 returns 2018/19–2023/24, peaking in 2021/22 and 2022/23 then falling (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24–25) — as stock depletion, "even though the shortage did not go away." The peak years are the pandemic years. The Court of Audit's own gloss is that the measure was temporary in character. A withdrawal of ad-hoc Länder outreach after the emergency, or the end of pandemic absence cover, explains the same curve without any depletion mechanism. Austria's structural shortage did indeed persist — its unfilled positions equalled 4.6% of the workforce, the second highest of fourteen systems (OECD, 2025, EAG Chapter D8, p. 491) — which cuts against depletion rather than for it: if the reserve had been exhausted while demand stayed high, we would expect price or terms to have moved, and the corpus records no such move.

### 3.5 What "the funnel" leaves out: leaver-side counterfactual employment

The memo models re-entry as a pipeline with seven institutional owners. It never models the alternative the person is being asked to give up. Brummet et al. (2024) find more than half of US leavers remain in education broadly defined and about a fifth leave the labour force; Cuervo and Vera-Toscano (2025, Table 2) find 22.1% of Australian leavers in non-teaching school-sector roles at one year and a further 21.8% in wider education, health and social assistance; Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015) find more than half of English leavers stay in the education sector. A large share of the "reserve" is *already working in education*, often in schools. Re-entry policy in these systems is not recruiting from outside the sector; it is asking people to move between roles inside it, against employers who also want to keep them. That is a competition mechanism, and it is entirely absent from a memo whose stated purpose is mechanisms.

### 3.6 Employer demand, not employer cost, in Georgia

The memo reads Georgia as an eligibility-screen case, which is right, and then folds it into the cost story, which is not. The audit's finding that "45% of surveyed school systems said the law's provisions had blocked a hire they would otherwise have made" and that 65% planned to start or continue using it (Georgia DOAA, 2025) is evidence of **unsatisfied employer demand for expensive veteran returners** — schools trying to hire people at USD 78,487 and being stopped by the statute. That is the cleanest employer-side observation in the corpus and it points the opposite way from Proposition 4.

## Front 4. Propositions that are true but too weak, safe or obvious — with sharper formulations the evidence still supports

### 4.1 Proposition 1 → invert it

As written ("each system's design decides which stage bears the loss … no government should fund an instrument without first naming the stage it believes binds") the proposition is both unfalsifiable and, in its recommendation, a truism: *know what you are fixing*. Section 2.2 above shows the attribution does not hold.

**Sharper, and still supported:** *Every published re-entry funnel begins counting after the largest loss has already occurred, so conversion rates measure where a system chose to start counting, not how well its programme worked.* England counted from 5,729 self-selected registrations, which is 1.6% of its own 366,000-strong out-of-service pool; NSW counted from a 1,500-person payroll extract; Portugal counted from a 200-place quota; the Netherlands counted grants claimed by boards; Georgia counted rehires permitted by statute. The corollary is a reporting standard with a **common denominator** — the register of qualified people not teaching — and the six-number sequence the memo already proposes in §9 (contacted, eligible, applied, offered, appointed, in post at one and three years). This has an OECD endorsement to hang on: "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically" (OECD, 2024, p. 51). It is the memo's best idea, currently buried in §9 and mis-stated in Proposition 1.

### 4.2 Proposition 3 → make it a quantity, not a sentiment

"Re-entry is a relational transaction, not a market one … the operational unit is the school and its exit protocol" is, stripped of its evidence problems (§2.3, §2.4), the familiar advice to keep in touch with alumni — advice the memo itself notes the OECD gave in 2005.

**Sharper, and the most robust regularity in the whole corpus:** *Wherever a system has counted, re-recruitment of former teachers converts at well under 1% of the eligible stock per year, and it does so irrespective of instrument, money or legal permissiveness.* Germany: just over 5,000 pensioners teaching against 457,000 retired teachers, ~1% (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Austria: 1,062 over six school years against a 123,000 workforce, 0.86% (Rechnungshof, 2025). Georgia: ~350 a year against 125,000, under 1% (Georgia DOAA, 2025). New Mexico's flagship no-caps route: 154 against ~48,000 eligible, 0.3% (NMERB, 2023). Ontario after the cap rose: ~60 expressions of interest from 142,000 members, 0.04% (Global News, 2022). Netherlands: an instrument budgeted for 500 against a primary-sector reserve of at least 31,000, 1.6%, and even that ceiling was cut twice for want of claims (Staatscourant 2017, No. 62002; Staatscourant 2019, No. 28510). (All shares own arithmetic from the cited counts.)

Two systems break the band, and both break it by changing the institution rather than the message: North Carolina, which let retirees draw salary and pension concurrently and moved just over 35% of the previously retirement-eligible cohort in 2009 (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024), and Japan, where re-employment after mandatory retirement is the default staffing route and retirees are about 60% of adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, p. 203). This formulation does three things the memo's version cannot: it delivers the lead author's binding "modest margin" line with numbers instead of adjectives; it is a genuine cross-system generalisation rather than a case reading; and it identifies the only two known ways out of the band.

### 4.3 Proposition 4 → move the claim from salary to jurisdiction, and add the ceiling

Proposition 4 as written ("the seniority salary schedule, not licensure, is the decisive barrier") is contradicted by Georgia's audit (§1.4) and by North Carolina's schools (§1.4), and its Ontario support omits Ontario 2022 (§3.3).

**Sharper, and defensible:** *Every measured change in re-entry volume in the corpus followed a change in pension or re-employment rules; none followed a change in training, brokerage or marketing — but permissiveness alone buys almost nothing, so the operative variable is the size of the money stack, not the removal of a penalty.* Rules moved returns: North Carolina's window opening and closing; Ontario's cap at 20 → 95 → 50 → 95 → 50; Georgia's 30-year and one-year screens; Austria's statutory exclusion of pensioned service from prior-service crediting, which leaves former civil servants on pension plus the **starting** salary while former contract teachers recover pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Question 19, p. 18); France's six-month délai on returning to one's last employer. Money did not move returns when it merely removed a penalty (Ontario, New Mexico) or when it was small and indirect (the Dutch EUR 2,500 to boards). It moved them once, when it stacked a full pension on a full salary (North Carolina). The policy sentence writes itself and is uncomfortable in a useful way: **the education ministry does not own the only lever that has ever worked, and the finance ministry will not like the one that did.** Fitzpatrick's (2015) five-to-one preference for current cash over pension wealth explains why the cheap versions fail, and the Ontario Teachers' Federation's refusal — "The Pension Plan is designed to provide an income to retired teachers; it is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024; OCEOTA, 2024) — is the political price attached.

### 4.4 Proposition 5 → keep the direction, drop the ratio, add the outside-option mechanism

Proposition 5 is the memo's best, and it collapses only where it reaches for the 35:1 and 10:1 numbers (§1.2, §1.3) and for the identity construct (§2.1).

**Sharper:** *Re-entry offers compete against a job the leaver already prefers, so an offer that changes only the pay is bidding on the one dimension the leaver has already conceded.* The concession is measurable: English leavers accept roughly a 10% wage cut for shorter hours and higher satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015); Australian leavers' hourly wages remain lower for a decade while job satisfaction rises (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, pp. 9–11, Table 4 — with the sample caveat at §1.7 above); Dutch inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs and name work pressure, control over hours and autonomy ahead of salary (Somers et al., 2024); Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner (2006) find "very few of those who leave teaching take jobs that pay more than their salary as teachers." And the return gradient runs the way the outside-option account predicts: *Teachers Matter* (OECD, 2005, p. 70) reports return rates highest in the subjects with the fewest alternative opportunities — which is why re-entry policy performs worst precisely in the shortage subjects it is built for.

### 4.5 Proposition 2 → what is left after the arithmetic

Once the nine-to-one falls (§1.1) and the two English arms are seen to be non-comparable — different subject scopes, different outcome definitions, different measurement windows, and an adviser-service cost that excludes the Get Into Teaching infrastructure it rides on while the pilot's cost includes a national marketing campaign — Proposition 2 has one durable component: **funded places should be tied to identified vacancies before recruitment opens**, which is not the memo's inference but England's own correction (NCTL, 2017), and which New Zealand independently arrived at by making the refresh requirement contingent on *not* having a job offer (Teaching Council, 2026). That is a real, cheap, transferable design rule. It does not need the cost comparison to stand up, and it is safer without it.

### 4.6 A proposition the memo makes and then loses its nerve on

Section 6's finding — that returners are absorbed by relief, casual and substitute markets and rejected by permanent appointment processes — is the most consequential thing in the memo for the paper's premise, and it is stronger than the memo lets on. *Teachers Matter* recorded the same pattern a generation ago: UK returners were about 25% of the full-time inflow but **more than 50% of the part-time inflow** in 2000/01 (OECD, 2005, p. 68). Japan, the Bahamas, New Zealand, Ireland, Ontario and NSW all run the returner channel through relief work. This is not a design accident of the 2020s; it is the structural position of the returner in a teacher labour market, observed across twenty-five years and six systems.

Its implication is genuinely uncomfortable for the project, and the paper is better off saying it than having a reviewer say it: **the route that reliably absorbs former teachers is the one that destroys the value the paper says they bring** — no class continuity, no induction, no mentoring function, no development role. The memo states this and then retreats into recommending tutoring, where the evidence is about programmes rather than about former teachers (§1.8). The honest version keeps the tension open and names what would resolve it, which brings us to the memo's own §9: *no study anywhere in the corpus links returners to student outcomes* (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024, state they could not, having no vacancy data and no counterfactual). The paper's per-se premise currently rests on an OECD assertion — returners bring "diverse career experiences, which can enhance teaching quality and drive innovation" (OECD, 2024, p. 50) — and on nothing measured. That should be said in the paper's own voice, once, early, and then the paper should argue the case on craft and continuity rather than pretend it has been tested.

## Which propositions survive a hostile reading, and which collapse

**Collapses: Proposition 2** (conversion decided at the entrance; cold list beat the campaign nine to one). The nine-to-one is wrong on the memo's own figures — the correct comparison is roughly two to one and compares casual placements with state-school posts (§1.1). The two English arms are not comparable on subject scope, outcome definition, measurement window or cost boundary (§4.5). A policymaker who has read the NFER evaluation will reach for a pen at the first sentence. Salvageable residue: tie funded places to identified vacancies.

**Collapses: Proposition 4** (the seniority schedule is the decisive barrier; only a third-party payer escapes it). The one employer-side dataset in the corpus was asked what blocked hires and named statutory screens, not salary (Georgia DOAA, 2025). North Carolina's schools hired 3,500 top-of-scale veterans across 52% of schools, which is the opposite of a cost deterrent (§1.4). Ontario 2022 shows that lifting the penalty produced about 60 interested people from 142,000 (§3.3). The proposition survives only if rewritten as a jurisdictional claim about pension rules with an explicit ceiling on what permissiveness buys (§4.3).

**Survives, wounded: Proposition 1.** The stage-attribution table does not hold — the memo assigns England four different binding stages across four sections, and Portugal two (§2.2). What survives, and is stronger than what it replaces, is the denominator finding the memo states in §0 and then abandons: published funnels start counting after the biggest loss, so conversion rates are artefacts of measurement, and the recommendation is a common denominator plus a six-number reporting standard (§4.1). Rewrite, do not delete.

**Survives, wounded: Proposition 3.** The 54% is conditional on returning and has no base rate; the outreach/attachment distinction has no instrument behind it; and the flagship recommendation — school-held exit registers — is nowhere observed in the corpus, so "nothing costs less or has better odds" is unsupportable (§2.3, §2.4). What survives is much better: the near-invariant sub-1% conversion band across every system that has counted, and the two exceptions that break it by changing the institution rather than the message (§4.2).

**Survives: Proposition 5**, and it is the one to keep. Its direction — that the reserve is composed of people who left, are not obviously worse off, and are being asked to reverse a transaction they have already priced — is supported by four independent bodies of evidence in three countries (English wage-for-conditions trade, Dutch stated conditions, Australian post-exit trajectories, US destination and earnings data), and by the Portugal asymmetry read as a direction rather than a ratio. It needs three repairs: drop the 35:1 (disjoint populations, quota-capped arm — §1.2), drop the 10:1-reproduces-the-funnels claim (falsified by the memo's own funnels — §1.3), and re-scope or re-source the Australian satisfaction result, whose estimation sample excludes permanent leavers (§1.7). Repaired, and joined to the outside-option gradient from *Teachers Matter* p. 70, it becomes the spine the lead author has been asking for: not "it depends," but a stated position about what re-entry policy is actually bidding against, and why it is structurally weakest in the shortage subjects governments most want to fill.

**One structural warning that applies above the level of individual propositions.** All five propositions treat former teachers as a staffing input and re-entry as a shortage instrument. `TEAM_INPUT.md` §3 forbids exactly that framing and requires that former teachers be treated as mattering per se, for what they bring back. Section 6 is the only part of the memo that engages the per-se question, and it concludes that the empirically available route strips the value. Adopted wholesale, this memo would reinstate the rejected premise by the back door. Whatever is carried forward needs a sixth proposition — or a rewritten first chapter — that argues the per-se case and states plainly that no study in the corpus links returners to student outcomes.
