## (a) Key claims, statistics and citations

### 1. Scale and trend of attrition

**Claim 1.1 — US teacher turnover was 15.1% in 2021‑22: 8.0% moved schools, 7.1% left teaching.**
- Source: Tan, Wei, Carver‑Thomas and García (2026), Learning Policy Institute (LPI), analysing the federal NTPS 2020‑21 / Teacher Follow‑up Survey (TFS) 2021‑22. Exact figures confirmed on the LPI product page: total turnover 15.1% of public school teachers, movers 8.0%, leavers 7.1%.
- Cross‑check: NCES's own First Look (NCES 2024‑039) reports rounded figures — public school teachers between 2020‑21 and 2021‑22: **84% stayers, 8% movers, 8% leavers**, "not statistically different" from 2012‑13 (also 84/8/8). Private schools: 82% stayers, 6% movers, **12% leavers** (NCES press release, 13 December 2023). **[EXCEL ISSUE — minor]** The Excel's 7.1% "leavers" is the LPI estimate; NCES's rounded official figure is 8%. Cite LPI for the decimal split and NCES for the official First Look.
- The Excel's "2026 Report" is identified: **Tan, T. S., W. Wei, D. Carver‑Thomas and E. García (2026), "Teacher Turnover in the United States: Who Moves, Who Leaves, and Why", Learning Policy Institute.**

**Claim 1.2 — Turnover concentrates in specific segments, not uniformly.** (Tan et al., 2026)
- Uncertified teachers: 20.1% turnover vs 14.7% certified.
- Special education and ESL teachers: 16.4–19.0%.
- City and charter schools: 17.7–17.8%.
- Schools with the highest concentrations of low‑income students and students of colour: 35–37% higher turnover.
- Black teachers: 18.1% vs 14.7% for White teachers.

**Claim 1.3 — Long‑run US turnover is flat and the pandemic spike was temporary.**
- Aldeman and Yi (2025, Education Next), pooling Current Population Survey (CPS) data: **7.6% of US teachers leave the occupation annually (2015–2024 pooled)**; leaving rose from **6.5% (2019) to 9.8% (2020)** and returned to **7.7% by 2023‑24**; turnover "remained relatively stable" across 40+ years of data; **older teachers are slightly *less* likely to leave than in the 1990s**. Policy takeaway (their words): the goal "shouldn't be to eliminate all turnover, but to focus on retaining the most effective educators and those who work in the hardest‑to‑staff schools and roles."
- This is the Excel's "2025 Article": **Aldeman, C. and S. Yi (2025), "Are Teachers Abandoning Teaching? Data Show Teacher Turnover Remains Low", Education Next, published online 18 December 2025.** (The article page displayed a print attribution of Vol. 25, No. 4; treat volume/issue as **[UNVERIFIED]** and cite by date/URL.)

**Claim 1.4 — Globally, attrition is rising: primary‑teacher attrition nearly doubled from 4.62% (2015) to 9.06% (2022).**
- UNESCO and the International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024), *Global Report on Teachers: Addressing teacher shortages and transforming the profession*. Same report: **44 million additional primary/secondary teachers needed by 2030**, ~15 million of them in Sub‑Saharan Africa; shortages also affect "high‑income regions like Europe and North America"; many leavers exit within their first five years.

**Claim 1.5 — Australia: annual leaving rate 11.6% in 2022 (HILDA), peak 16.3% in 2012.**
- Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano (2025), Asia Pacific Journal of Education, using ~20 years of HILDA panel data. The authors frame the Australian problem as both an attraction *and* a retention problem.

**Claim 1.6 — England: roughly 10% of teachers leave each year, stable across most years.**
- Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015), NFER, *Should I Stay or Should I Go?* — "around 10 per cent leave each year… the same in most years", contrasting with surveys reporting far larger shares *considering* leaving.

### 2. Determinants of attrition

**Claim 2.1 — Workload, stress and work‑life balance — not salary — are the strongest push factors in Australia (panel evidence).**
- Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano (2025): teachers leave mainly due to "high stress levels, heavy workloads, and inadequate work‑life balance"; attrition is "closely associated with workload pressure and temporal strain". The Excel row for this study is accurate on this point.

**Claim 2.2 — Former teachers most often blame school leadership and workload.**
- Brandenburg, Larsen, Simpson, Sallis and Trần (2024), survey of **256 former Australian teachers who left between 2016 and 2022**: most frequent reasons — **school leadership issues 17.71%, workload 16.57%, workplace environment 9.7%, student behaviour 7.43%, lack of respect 6.86%, administrative overload 5.14%, lack of personal satisfaction 5.71%**. Reasons were "multifaceted" and leaving was "often protracted".
- **[EXCEL ISSUE]** The Excel says respondents "left the profession between 2016 and 2024"; the published study covers **2016–2022** (most left in 2021–22, n=110).

**Claim 2.3 — Stress roughly doubles stated intention to leave (OECD‑wide).**
- OECD (2020), *TALIS 2018 Results (Volume II)*: on average across the OECD, **18% of lower‑secondary teachers experience "a lot" of stress at work**; those teachers are **about twice as likely** to report they will stop working as teachers within five years; on average across TALIS systems, **~25% of teachers want to leave teaching within the next five years**. In half of countries with data, stress predicts intention to leave even after controlling for job satisfaction.
- Update: OECD (2025), *Results from TALIS 2024* ("Sustaining the teaching profession"): **19% of teachers report high stress** (wide cross‑country variation); teachers stressed "quite a bit"/"a lot" by student intimidation/verbal abuse or by maintaining classroom discipline are **twice as likely** to wish to leave within five years — and discipline/behaviour stress is **more closely associated with intention to leave than workload, accountability or reform pressures**.

**Claim 2.4 — In the US, salary, leadership and satisfaction are the strongest (correlational) predictors.**
- Tan et al. (2026): each **$1,000** (cost‑of‑living‑adjusted) salary increase is associated with **‑0.34 percentage points** turnover; predicted turnover falls ~**40%** moving from a $50,000 to a $70,000 salary; low→high perceived leadership effectiveness: turnover **18.7% → 9.0%** (‑52%); low→high job satisfaction: **22.0% → 8.0%** (‑64%). *Correlational — linear probability model, no identification strategy* (the team's own Excel comment is correct and should be kept in the text).

**Claim 2.5 — Family/life‑course events, not better‑paying jobs, drove most exits of US female teachers (classic hazard evidence).**
- Stinebrickner (2002), Journal of Human Resources 37(1), 192–216, hazard model on certified female teachers in the **NLS‑72** cohort (National Longitudinal Study of the High School Class of 1972; follow‑ups 1973–1986): **~67% of exiting female teachers left the labour force entirely** rather than switching occupations; **the presence of a newborn child is the dominant exit factor**, ahead of marriage or pay; many leavers later return to teaching; exit behaviour resembles that of comparably educated non‑teachers.
- This is the Excel's "2002 Paper": **Stinebrickner, T. R. (2002), "An Analysis of Occupational Change and Departure from the Labor Force: Evidence of the Reasons that Teachers Leave".** The Excel's description is accurate; its caveat (1970s–80s cohort; parental‑leave policy changes since) is well taken.

**Claim 2.6 — Workload in England: hours and perceived acceptability.**
- DfE *Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders* survey series (as reported by SecEd, 2025): teachers averaged **48.1 hours/week in 2024** (down from 49.4 in 2023; full‑time teachers still >50); **43%** of teachers/leaders felt workload was "not acceptable" and lacked control over it (down from 49% the previous year and **57% in 2022**). (Wave numbering/report number **[UNVERIFIED]** — cite DfE survey series via GOV.UK.)

**Claim 2.7 — Systematic evidence: determinants cluster into personal, school and policy factors.**
- Nguyen, Pham, Crouch and Springer (2020), *Educational Research Review* 31, 100355 — meta‑analysis of **120 US studies**; correlates grouped as personal characteristics, school factors (leadership, working conditions), and external/policy factors (accountability, pay structures). Use as the framing citation for the determinants section.

### 3. Intrinsic vs extrinsic motives — the cross‑country contrast

**Claim 3.1 — Intrinsic/social motives dominate entry everywhere; extrinsic motives vary more across countries.**
- OECD (2019), *TALIS 2018 Results (Volume I)*: **~90%** of teachers across 48 systems cite influencing children's development / contributing to society as a major motivation; only **61%** cite teaching's "steady career path"; teaching was the **first‑choice career for 2 in 3** teachers (59% of men vs 70% of women).
- Watt, Richardson, Klusmann, Kunter, Beyer, Trautwein and Baumert (2012), *Teaching and Teacher Education* 28(6), 791–805 (FIT‑Choice scale in **Australia, the US, Germany, Norway**): motivations were "more similar than different" across countries — social utility and intrinsic value ranked highest everywhere — while **perceptions of the profession (salary, status, demand) reflected country differences**.
- Country deviation example: analysis of TALIS 2018 by FFT Education Datalab (2019) found **England an outlier in the weight teachers give to job security, reliable income and a steady career path** relative to other systems.

**Claim 3.2 — Where the *exit* margin sits differs by system: US evidence is more salary‑responsive; Australian/Dutch evidence points to conditions, not pay.**
- US: salary is a first‑order predictor of turnover (Tan et al., 2026, above); in Michigan, certified non‑teachers most often named **higher salary as both the top reason for not teaching and the top incentive to return** (REL Midwest, 2021).
- Australia: HILDA panel evidence puts workload/stress/work‑life balance ahead of pay (Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano, 2025); the 256‑leaver survey barely features pay among top reasons (Brandenburg et al., 2024).
- Netherlands: ROA/Maastricht (Somers et al., 2024) — what would bring the "hidden reserve" back is "less about pay than about lower workload, more autonomy, and more control over hours".
- England: leavers accept **~10% lower wages** in exchange for fewer hours and higher satisfaction (NFER, see 4.3) — revealed‑preference evidence that the binding constraint is conditions, not pay.
- This contrast is the paper's premise: identical re‑entry incentives will not travel across systems whose leavers left for different reasons.

### 4. Destinations after exit

**Claim 4.1 — US (older cohort): most exiting female teachers left employment altogether.**
- Stinebrickner (2002): **~67% exited the labour force** (see 2.5).
- Complementary Georgia administrative evidence: Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner (2006), *B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy* 6(1): "very few of those who leave teaching take jobs that pay more than their salary as teachers" — against the "poached by better‑paying employers" narrative.

**Claim 4.2 — Australia: leavers stay close to education and report better lives, not better pay.**
- Brandenburg et al. (2024): **51.8%** of respondents work in education‑related fields (coaching, counselling, social work); **36%** in education sectors (policy, publishing, higher education, museums); **20%** moved to higher education; **62%** use their teaching skills "a lot".
- Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano (2025): **~20% of leavers remain employed in the education sector**; leavers report **modest job‑satisfaction gains within 1 and 3 years** of exit and **sustained gains in leisure‑time satisfaction**. **[EXCEL ISSUE — partial]** The Excel additionally claims leavers "move into related caring fields like healthcare" and that "wages don't meaningfully improve"; these specific claims could not be verified from the accessible abstract/coverage (full text was paywalled during this session) — **[UNVERIFIED]**, plausible but check full text before quoting.

**Claim 4.3 — England: over half of leavers stay in education; they take a ~10% wage cut.**
- Worth, Bamford and Durbin (2015), NFER: **more than half of leavers (excluding retirees) take jobs in the education sector**; leavers experienced on average a **~10% fall in wages** relative to comparable stayers, gaining shorter hours and higher job satisfaction.

**Claim 4.4 — The "reserve pool" of qualified non‑teachers dwarfs measured shortages.**
- Netherlands: ROA (Somers et al., 2024) — **~62,000 qualified teachers work outside education, ~4× the secondary shortage** (shortage: **13,600 FTE at end‑2023**); a conservative estimate (only post‑2000 qualifiers counted). Inactive teachers rate several working conditions *more* favourably than active teachers.
- Michigan, US: REL Midwest (2021, REL 2021‑076) — **~61,000 Michigan‑certified teachers were not teaching in the state's public schools in 2017/18**; non‑teaching certificate‑holders skew young (30%+ under 35 vs 20.4% of active teachers) and minority.

### 5. How teacher attrition compares with other professions

**Claim 5.1 — Teacher turnover is not exceptional among comparably educated, licensed professions.**
- Harris and Adams (2007), *Economics of Education Review* 26(3), 325–337, pooling **CPS 1992–2001** (the team's Excel notes 18,786 teachers): annual probability of leaving the profession **7.73% for teachers vs 6.09% (nurses), 14.94% (social workers), 8.01% (accountants)**; differences shrink or disappear when only *public school* teachers are compared (private schools churn more). Early‑career teachers behave like early‑career workers in the comparison professions.
- Aldeman and Yi (2025) replicate the pattern with 2015–2024 CPS data: teachers ≈ nurses ≈ accountants; social workers consistently higher.
- This confirms the Excel's "2007 Paper" and its description: **Harris, D. N. and S. J. Adams (2007), "Understanding the level and causes of teacher turnover: A comparison with other professions".**

**Claim 5.2 — What *is* distinctive is early, pension‑driven retirement.**
- Harris and Adams (2007): "teacher turnover is relatively high among older teachers reflecting the fact that they **retire considerably earlier than other professionals**", consistent with pension design (high replacement rates once vested).
- Mechanism: Costrell and Podgursky (2009), *Education Finance and Policy* 4(2), 175–211 — pension wealth accrual in six state systems is "highly nonlinear", spiking in the mid‑to‑late 50s then turning to "dramatic decline or even negative returns", creating strong pull‑then‑push retirement incentives at ages that are young for professionals. This is the key link between Chapter 2 (why they leave) and the paper's re‑entry theme: a sizeable share of "leavers" are pension‑incentivised early retirees — precisely the pool re‑entry and mentoring programmes target.

**Claim 5.3 — UK cross‑profession comparison: teachers work more hours for lower hourly pay than police, similar to nurses.**
- Hillary, Andrade and Worth (2018), NFER Research Update 4 (Understanding Society data): term‑time hours **teachers 50/week vs police 44, nurses 39**; real average hourly pay **£17.70 (2015‑16)**, ≈ nurses, below police (£18.80); teachers' real hourly pay **fell 15% since 2009/10 vs ‑4% (nurses) and ‑11% (police)**; only **47%** of teachers satisfied with leisure time — lowest of the three.

## (b) Cross‑country contrasts (explicit, for the paper's framing)

1. **Pay vs conditions as the exit margin.** US federal survey data show salary among the strongest predictors of turnover (Tan et al., 2026), and US non‑teaching certificate‑holders name salary as the top return incentive (REL Midwest, 2021). By contrast, Australian panel data (Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano, 2025), the Australian leaver survey (Brandenburg et al., 2024), Dutch register‑plus‑survey evidence (Somers et al., 2024) and English leaver wage trajectories (Worth et al., 2015) all point to workload, leadership and autonomy — leavers there accept *lower* pay for better conditions. Re‑entry policy that only pays (e.g. Portugal's €750/month top‑up, ~63 returners — programmes sheet) fails exactly where conditions drove exit; unions there attributed low uptake to working conditions, matching the pattern.
2. **Destination of leavers.** In the older US cohort most exiting women left employment entirely (Stinebrickner, 2002); in contemporary Australia and England most leavers keep working, often inside the wider education sector (Brandenburg et al., 2024; Worth et al., 2015; Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano, 2025). Implication: the modern "former teacher" is typically employed and reachable — a genuine reserve — rather than out of the labour force.
3. **Whether there is a "crisis" at all.** US long‑run turnover is flat at ~7–8%/year and pandemic effects reversed (Aldeman and Yi, 2025; NCES, 2023), whereas the *global* attrition rate doubled 2015–2022 (UNESCO, 2024) and Australia's leaving rate hit double digits (Cuervo and Vera‑Toscano, 2025). "Is attrition a problem?" is country‑ and segment‑specific: in the US it is a distribution problem (hard‑to‑staff schools/subjects), elsewhere a level problem.
4. **Entry motives are similar; profession perceptions differ.** Intrinsic/social motives dominate everywhere (OECD, 2019; Watt et al., 2012); extrinsic security motives are unusually salient in England (FFT Datalab, 2019). Systems differ more in what teaching *offers* (pay, status, security) than in what teachers *want*.
5. **Stressors differ by system.** TALIS 2024: high‑stress shares range widely across countries (19% average; 42% in Alberta, Canada), and behaviour/discipline stress predicts exit intentions more strongly than workload in the pooled data (OECD, 2025) — while in Australia and England workload dominates the national evidence.

## (c) Caveats and limitations

- **Intentions ≠ exits.** TALIS "want to leave within five years" (~25%) vastly exceeds realised attrition (5–11%). Nguyen, Bettini, Redding and Gilmour (2025, EEPA) explicitly caution against equating turnover intentions with actual turnover. Use intention data only for cross‑country patterning of *dissatisfaction*.
- **Causality.** Only a minority of the evidence is causal/quasi‑experimental (Stinebrickner's structural hazard; pension‑incentive studies). The LPI predictors are correlational (the team's Excel comment is correct). Brandenburg et al. (2024) is a self‑selected survey; the Michigan REL survey had ~30% response (per the team's Excel comment).
- **Vintage.** Stinebrickner (2002) uses a 1972 high‑school cohort of *female* teachers; parental leave, female labour supply and teacher labour markets have changed materially — treat the 67% out‑of‑labour‑force figure as historical, contrasted with modern destination data (NFER 2015; Brandenburg et al., 2024).
- **Measurement.** CPS-based occupation-switch measures (Harris and Adams, 2007; Aldeman and Yi, 2025) capture different objects than school-administrative leaver rates (England SWC, NSW), and both differ from TFS survey-based movers/leavers; rates are not directly comparable across sources.
