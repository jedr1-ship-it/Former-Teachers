# Explaining cross-country variation in re-entry policy

*Analytical memo for the argument architecture. Angle: why outcomes differ across systems. Every claim traceable to the verified research files; no facts from memory or the web. Where the evidence cannot settle a question, the memo says what would settle it.*

---

## What actually needs explaining

The rejected draft lined countries up by how many former teachers came back and let the reader infer that permissive rules produce returners and restrictive rules do not. That ordering is not wrong so much as empty: it explains the two cases that fit and is silent on the four that do not.

The cases that fit are the ones everybody cites. North Carolina suspended the earnings cap on retirees between 1999 and 2009, letting them draw a full salary alongside a full pension — up to about 50% more income than a regular full-time teacher — and around 3,500 teachers returned, with just over 35% of the previously retirement-eligible returning to full-time work by 2009 and returns falling to zero when the window closed (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Georgia stacked three eligibility screens on an otherwise comparable offer and got roughly 350 full-time rehires a year, under 1% of its workforce, with 45% of surveyed school systems reporting that the statute had blocked a hire they would otherwise have made (Georgia DOAA, 2025).

The cases that do not fit are more instructive. Ontario raised its re-employment cap from 50 days to 95 for the pandemic years, and the Ontario Teachers' Federation reported that roughly 60 of 142,000 retired members had expressed interest since September 2021 (Global News, 2022). The Netherlands offered school boards EUR 2,500 for every returner they inducted, budgeted for 500, subsidised barely 200 in three years, and cut the annual ceilings in 2019 explicitly because the money had not been spent (Stcrt. 2019, 28510). Portugal set a quota of 200 returner places and received 79 or 80 applications, so the quota never bound (DN, 2024; SÁBADO, 2024). France maintains an enabling framework with no programme and publishes no figures at all, while the one documented outreach effort — the Toulouse rectorat writing to recently retired teachers in December 2021 — was conceded by the rector himself to have "not had the desired impact" (France Bleu Occitanie, 2022, snippet only).

Three of those four are *liberalisations that produced nothing*. A theory in which restrictiveness is the master variable cannot accommodate them. The rest of this memo proposes one that can.

## 1. Rules bind matches, not motivation

**Claim.** Re-entry rules govern whether a willing former teacher and a willing school can complete a transaction that both already want. They do very little to change how many such pairs exist. Cross-country variation in take-up is therefore variation in two nearly independent quantities — the number of latent matches, and the share of them the rules permit — and the literature has been reading the second where it should have been reading the first.

**Mechanism.** A pension penalty, an earnings cap or a service threshold is a tax levied at the moment of hiring. It is decisive for someone standing at the door with a school that wants them, because it converts a positive-surplus match into no match. It is close to irrelevant for someone who is not at the door, because the tax is not what kept them away: a retiree who has not thought about teaching since 2014 does not consult the earnings cap. Loosening a rule therefore harvests the stock of blocked matches quickly and then stops.

**Evidence for.** The two clean identifications in the corpus both concern blocked matches, not motivation. Georgia's audit is the strongest evidence anywhere on this point, because it does not infer a counterfactual — it asks employers for one: 45% of surveyed systems said statutory provisions had stopped a hire they wanted to make, and named the 30-year service rule, the one-year break and the high-need-subject methodology as the culprits (Georgia DOAA, 2025). North Carolina supplies the mirror image at the other end: when the window expired in autumn 2009 the flow of returners collapsed, which is the strongest available proof that the rule and not preferences had been doing the work (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; lit_reserve Claim 6). Note that the North Carolina story is about *conversion*, not recruitment: 54% of returners went back to the school they had taught in before retirement. These were pre-existing relationships waiting for a rule to permit them.

**Evidence against — or rather, evidence for the other half of the claim.** Where the stock of blocked matches was thin, liberalisation did nothing. Ontario's cap went up and about 60 of 142,000 retirees stirred. New Mexico's most permissive tier — 36 months with no salary or hours limits — attracted 154 users in its first year out of some 48,000 eligible retirees (NMERB, 2023). The Netherlands could not give away money for returners it had already estimated at 31,000 in primary education alone. Portugal's quota did not bind. In each case the constraint sat somewhere other than the rule.

**Policy implication.** Removing a pension penalty is worth doing and is cheap, but it should be costed as a one-off harvest of an existing backlog, not as a recurring supply channel. Systems should expect a spike and a decay, and should not read the decay as programme failure. It also follows that the *order* of reforms matters: there is no point paying for outreach into a rule set that will disqualify the people the outreach finds, which is exactly Georgia's configuration — an active statute, an audited process, and 45% of employers turned away at the counter.

## 2. "Restrictive" is four different things, and only some of them bite

**Claim.** Lumping waiting periods, service thresholds, subject tests, earnings caps and sunset clauses into a single "restrictiveness" dial destroys the information in the comparison. They act through different mechanisms, on different populations, with different reversibility, and at least one of them is not a restriction on re-entry at all but an instrument aimed at something else entirely.

**The pension arithmetic.** This is the only channel that is genuinely a price. It is also the one where the North Carolina–Georgia contrast, told properly, refutes the received reading. *Both* states let retirees draw a full pension alongside a full salary, and Georgia's rehires were paid well — an average of USD 78,487, because they were placed high on the salary schedule (Georgia DOAA, 2025). The money is not what separates them. What separates them is who is permitted to reach it. Where the arithmetic does the work is elsewhere: in Austria, where the law excludes from prior-service crediting any period already generating a pension, so former civil servants return at the teacher starting salary while former contract teachers have their service credited and broadly recover their pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Q19, p. 18); in Portugal, where returners are paid at the first step of the career scale, pro-rated by hours (Decreto-Lei 51/2024, Art. 5); in Germany, where the recurring Länder instrument is precisely lifting the earnings limits and topping up by around 10% where pension caps bind (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). England shows the same channel dissolving by accident: abatement applies only to final-salary Age and Premature benefits, not to career-average benefits accrued since 2015, so the constraint shrinks automatically as CARE membership matures (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.).

The Austrian case deserves emphasis because it separates a design choice from an artefact. Portugal *chose* to index returners to the bottom of the scale. Austria did not choose anything: the entry-scale outcome falls out of general civil-service pension-crediting law and lands on returners as a side-effect. Only one of those is fixable by a re-entry policy. A paper that recommends "pay returners for their experience" is asking Portugal to change a decree and Austria to change the architecture of the federal service.

**The eligibility screens.** These are not a price but an exclusion, and Georgia contains three of them doing three different jobs. The 30-year creditable-service rule is a *stock* screen: it removes people permanently and no one can negotiate around it. The one-year break is a *timing* screen, and the audit's finding that it pushes retirees into private schools or out of the labour force shows it is not a delay but a leak — during the wait the person acquires an alternative or settles. The high-need-subject test, defined annually at Regional Education Service Agency level from a five-year average of vacancy surveys, is a *matching* cost imposed at the wrong administrative distance: the district cannot know at the moment it wants someone whether the post will qualify. That the 2026 extension devolved the high-need determination to the school system while keeping the 30-year rule (TRS Georgia, 2026) is the legislature agreeing with that diagnosis — and it sets up the cleanest natural test in the whole corpus, discussed in §9.

**The signal.** Almost every instrument in the corpus carries an expiry date: Georgia to June 2026 then 2030, Virginia to July 2028, Michigan's loosening to October 2028 sitting on top of a permanent framework, Ireland's career-break substitution suspension renewed annually since about 2021/22 and formally "replacing" its predecessor each summer (TC/IN 0005/2025). New Zealand alone moved the other way, letting the Teacher Education Refresh waiver stop being an extension and become the rule, so that refresh training is now contingent mainly on *not* having a job offer (Teaching Council, 2026). The hypothesis that temporariness itself suppresses take-up is attractive and I cannot support it. North Carolina's collapse on expiry is the only measurement of an ending, and it is over-determined: when the window closed the arithmetic reverted to the half-salary part-time cap at the same moment. Nothing in the evidence base separates "the signal ended" from "the money ended". This is a hypothesis, and §9 says what would test it.

**The post at the end of the process.** Treated last here because it turns out to be first in importance; §3 is devoted to it.

**A correction to the whole framing, from France.** France's six-month délai de carence is routinely listed as a barrier. Its stated purpose, in the Government's own answer to a parliamentary question, is to prevent employers from immediately re-hiring their own just-retired staff at lower pay (Assemblée nationale, 2020). That is not a badly designed re-entry rule; it is a wage-floor rule that happens to constrain re-entry. Reading France's absent returner statistics as policy failure misreads the objective function. And the live French grievance is not the delay at all: the 2026 parliamentary exchange is about the *income caps* under capped cumul penalising teachers who left before the 2015 reform, capping combined income at around EUR 1,500 against roughly EUR 2,600 plus full salary for complete careers (Assemblée nationale, 2026), while the 2023 reform quietly added a real incentive nobody counts — full-cumul retirees now accrue new pension rights from resumed work (Loi 2023-270, art. 26). The waiting period is the visible feature; the arithmetic is the binding one.

**Policy implication.** The recommendation is not "be less restrictive". It is: audit each screen for what it is doing. Timing screens should be set at the shortest length the tax or pension law actually requires — the United States shows the floor, since a 90-day layout (New Mexico) or a bona fide termination (Michigan) satisfies plan-qualification requirements, and anything longer is a policy choice about optics that leaks people. Matching screens should be decided by whoever is doing the hiring. Stock screens are the most expensive per unit of fiscal protection and should be justified explicitly or dropped.

## 3. The product, not the price: the reserve wants small quantities of work

**Claim.** The single largest source of cross-country variation in headline take-up is not how much systems are willing to pay former teachers, nor how permissive their rules are, but *how much work they are trying to sell*. Systems offering small, low-commitment engagements record many returners; systems offering full-time permanent posts record few. The comparative literature has been treating these as the same product at different prices.

**Mechanism.** Former teachers are, on the evidence, disproportionately interested in re-entering at low intensity — a few days, a relief block, a quarter-time timetable — and disproportionately unwilling to re-enter at full intensity on unchanged conditions. Re-entry at low intensity is also the transaction that clears fastest, because casual and relief work is available continuously rather than in an annual hiring cycle, and requires no reorganisation of the returner's life. A rule that maximises permissible *income* is therefore optimising a margin most of the pool does not want to be on.

**Evidence for.** New Mexico is the closest thing to a controlled experiment, because four re-employment regimes run side by side in one state, one year, one retiree population. In FY23, the tier with no salary or hours limits drew 154 users; the sub-USD-15,000 earnings tier drew 390; and the quarter-time-or-less tier — no layout period, no contributions, no earnings ceiling because the hours are capped instead — drew 686 (NMERB, 2023). Permissiveness on income is *inversely* ranked against take-up; permissiveness on commitment runs with it. Everything else in the corpus points the same way. NFER's largest and most return-committed returner segment, the career breakers, returned at double the rate of other types and wanted part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). New Zealand's standing statutory on-ramp is 20 half-days a year of relief teaching without a current practising certificate (Education and Training Act 2020, Sch. 3). Ireland's supply measure is not a return scheme at all but the annual suspension of the cap on substitute work by teachers already on career break. Japan's retirees have effectively colonised the low-intensity margin: 52.7% of part-time adjunct hires in the prefecture studied were in their sixties and a further 7.1% in their seventies, roughly 60% of all adjuncts, while only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools now seek full-time re-employment (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203). NSW placed its 145 returners in casual and temporary work. Portugal pro-rates returner pay by teaching hours.

**Evidence against, and a measurement consequence that matters more than the claim.** The low-intensity margin is wide but thin. New Mexico's 686 quarter-timers are on the order of 172 full-time equivalents. Japan's dependence on a retiree adjunct pool is described by the same study as fragile, because ageing adjuncts increasingly decline the most demanding replacement work — a reserve composed of retirees depletes on its own schedule. So the product that sells is also the product that delivers least teaching per person recruited. This means headcount comparisons across countries are close to meaningless: Georgia's ~350 full-time rehires, New Mexico's 686 quarter-timers, NSW's 145 casuals and England's 27 permanent EBacc appointments are four different goods, and the ranking of countries by returner headcount is substantially a ranking by product granularity. **No comparison of re-entry programmes in this paper should be expressed in headcount without stating the intensity of the post.**

There is also a real tension with the paper's own premise. If what the reserve mostly wants is a few days of relief work, then the case for re-entry policy cannot rest on staffing arithmetic — a thin, wide, low-intensity margin will not fill establishment posts — and must rest instead on what these people bring back per hour of contact. That is precisely the ground the team has already chosen, and this is the evidence that forces it.

**Policy implication.** The first design question is not "how much may they earn" but "what are we asking them to do". Systems wanting headcount should sell small, continuously available engagements and remove the frictions on those specifically. Systems wanting FTE should stop expecting the returner channel to supply it and say so.

## 4. Who recruits, and who holds the record

**Claim.** Conversion rates rise sharply as the recruiting agent moves closer to the vacancy, and the operative asset is not enthusiasm or budget but *which administrative record the recruiter can read*. Employment records beat qualification registers, because they carry recency, subject, and the fact that the person actually taught.

**Mechanism.** A national ministry running a campaign can address everyone who ever held a licence and must then pay to discover which of them are suitable and available. An employer reading its own payroll addresses only people who taught in this system recently, and has already filtered on suitability at zero cost, inside a database, before the first letter is sent. The filter is not a nicety: it determines what the conversion funnel is measuring.

**Evidence for.** England's 2015-16 pilot recruited through a national marketing campaign and a registration website, drew 5,729 registrations — nearly double target — supported 354 people, and placed 27 in permanent EBacc posts in state secondaries by November 2016, at an estimated GBP 37,400 per additional returner, which NFER judged unlikely to represent good value for money (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 56-58, 91). The definition of "returner" in that pilot included people who had qualified but never taught, primary-trained teachers, overseas-trained holders of QTS and independent-sector teachers; fewer than a third of participants had taught in the previous decade. NSW contacted teachers who had resigned from the NSW public system in the previous five years, from the Department's own staffing records, and converted more than 1,500 contacts into more than 260 expressions of interest and 145 placements (NSW Government, 2024a). Vienna wrote to 300 retirees; Salzburg contacted every retiree up to age 67; Hesse wrote to roughly 7,000 (APA/ORF, 2023; Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). New Zealand takes the principle to its logical end and inverts it: rather than recruiting, it removes the re-certification requirement *conditional on the returner already having a job offer*, letting the school do all the selecting and costing the ministry nothing (Teaching Council, 2026).

**Evidence against.** Three problems, and they are serious.

First, the England–NSW comparison is confounded three ways at once and cannot identify the recruiter effect on its own. The contact lists differ in quality, the products differ (permanent secondary posts versus casual work), and the recruiter's proximity to the vacancy differs. All three push in the same direction. Nothing in the corpus varies one while holding the others: there is no record-based outreach into permanent posts, and no national campaign into casual work.

Second, the outcome data are asymmetric in a way that flatters NSW. England's failure is documented to the pound by an independent evaluator; NSW's success is a single ministerial snapshot at six months, never repeated, never costed, never evaluated, absent from the 2024-25 Annual Report and no longer named on the teach.NSW page (NSW DoE, 2025; NSW DoE, 2026). We are comparing a well-measured failure with an unmeasured success.

Third, France breaks the pattern. The Toulouse rectorat used exactly the employer-record instrument and got nothing. Why? It could reach only *retirees*, because France has no register of the qualified-but-inactive; it made the ask during the Omicron wave as an emergency; and it sent a letter without a process attached. NSW's letter came bundled with departmental help on NESA accreditation, approval to teach and the Working with Children Check in a single contact, and about 40% of its returners were not retirees at all. The refinement the "outreach works" lesson needs is therefore: *outreach converts when it is aimed at recent leavers rather than pensioners, and when it arrives attached to a one-stop re-licensure process rather than an invitation.* (The Toulouse source is a search snippet only; treat the case as illustrative, not load-bearing.)

**Policy implication.** The cheapest re-entry instrument in the corpus is an employer maintaining and using its own leaver register, with re-licensure bundled into the first contact. But it should be sold as a conversion device operating on recent, identified leavers, not as a way of finding people the system has lost track of — which is the thing it cannot do.

## 5. What varies that is not policy at all

Four structural facts explain more of the cross-country pattern than any design feature, and none of them is a lever.

**The measurement infrastructure selects the target group, and the target group caps the achievable result.** Register systems — the Netherlands, England, Michigan, Australia — can see a working-age reserve: roughly 62,000 post-2000 Dutch qualifiers outside education against a 13,600 FTE shortage (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024); some 260,000 out-of-service English teachers of working age plus 106,000 never in service (Worth, 2023); 61,252 Michigan certificate-holders not teaching, 93% of them under 60 (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021); about 19% of 553,300 Australian registrants not deployed in schools (AITSL, 2025). Germany, Austria and France have no such count. What Germany has is 457,000 retired teachers. So the German policy debate is about retirees, because retirees are the only population the state can enumerate — and 13 of 16 Länder duly recruit them, yielding just over 5,000 in service, under 1% of an 800,000-strong workforce (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023). Austria's nine Bildungsdirektionen do the same and produced 1,062 returners over six years against a workforce of about 123,000 (Rechnungshof, 2025). This is not a coincidence and it is not a data footnote: **systems that cannot see their working-age reserve run retiree policy by default, and retiree policy is bounded above by a stock that is old, shrinking and exiting on its own schedule.** Ontario's retirees average 72 years old. Japan's adjunct pool is ageing out. The ceiling was set by the register, not by the incentive.

**Whether teaching is a lifetime or a phase.** Across 19 OECD systems, about half of departures are resignations and half retirements, but the split is bimodal: over 80% of leavers resigned in Denmark, England and Estonia, while retirements exceed half of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025, Indicator D8, Table D8.4). The instruments follow. Resignation-heavy systems build registers, advisory services and returner programmes; retirement-heavy systems build pension-compatibility rules and postponement supplements. Portugal, whose salient margin is retirement, spent its money on a retirement-postponement supplement and ran the returner route as a 200-place sideline. This is the deep reason "restrictiveness" fails as an explanation: England and France are not two points on one dial, they are two different problems.

And it produces an uncomfortable corollary. The systems with the most promising reserve — young, large, reachable — are exactly the systems where a re-entry programme is hardest to justify at the margin, because the return is already happening without one. About 17,000 teachers return to English state schools each year, roughly a third of all entrants (DfE, 2023); the Dutch reserve shrank 17% in primary and 18% in secondary between 2015 and 2024 without any instrument to speak of (DUO, 2026). Against flows of that size, an instrument that produces 27 placements or 200 subsidies is not measurable, let alone attributable.

**Outside options make the standard offer worthless.** The comparative evidence on where leavers go is unusually consistent and it demolishes the poaching story: Australian leavers' weekly wages fall 6.2% one year out and 9.4% at three years, with hourly wages lower for a decade, while job satisfaction rises 0.13 SD at one year and 0.19 SD at three (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); English leavers accept about a 10% wage cut for shorter hours and higher satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015); US leavers' mean earnings remain slightly below pre-exit levels even eight years later (Brummet et al., 2024). These people bought conditions with money. It follows mechanically that **an offer returning them to the same conditions at the bottom of the salary scale is not an offer**, and three unrelated systems have independently constructed exactly that: Portugal by decree, Austria by pension-crediting artefact, England by placing returners into unchanged posts with almost no part-time availability. Michigan is the standing exception that keeps the claim honest — there, inactive certificate-holders name salary first, at 33% (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021) — though on a 30% response rate the report itself calls non-representative.

**The state of the entry pipeline decides what re-entry policy even is.** Ontario is the only system in the corpus to have moved its cap downwards, and the reason is the one the rest of the literature never confronts. In 2008-09 the ten largest boards spent CAD 108.3 million on retired supply teachers, who earned CAD 432.69 a day against CAD 227.36 for new teachers, while 12,774 newly certified graduates chased about 6,500 openings and one board handed retirees 2,861 supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). The cap was cut to 50 days in 2012 to protect new-graduate hiring. **The same 95-day rule is a supply policy in 1990 and a distributional transfer from new entrants to pensioners, priced at roughly 1.9 times, in 2012** — without a word of the rule changing. The identical warning appears at the opposite end of the political spectrum in Chinese commentary that localities use silver-age recruits to delay creating formal posts (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025, secondary source).

Ontario also shows that the *governance* of a rule determines its range of motion. The cap sits in Schedule 1 to the Teachers' Pension Act and can be changed only by agreement of the co-sponsors, the government and the Ontario Teachers' Federation — an actor whose membership includes both the pensioners and, in effect, the unemployed graduates. It is the only rule in the corpus with a standing veto player representing new entrants, and it is the only one that has ever gone down. The OTF's refusal of a fourth extension in March 2024 states the principle explicitly: the pension plan "is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024). Rules made unilaterally by legislatures — Georgia, Michigan, Virginia, Louisiana — ratchet in one direction only.

## 6. The Dutch anomaly: the constraint is absorption, not willingness

**Claim.** The country whose arithmetic launched the "the reserve could close the shortage" claim is also the country that most clearly refutes it, and it refutes it for a reason nobody expected: schools could not absorb the returners who came forward.

**Mechanism.** Placing a returner is not a transaction, it is an induction. It consumes a vacancy, a timetable, a mentor's time and a supervising colleague's attention — and in a system already short of teachers, the mentor is the scarce input. A per-head subsidy can lower the price of absorption; it cannot manufacture the capacity.

**Evidence for.** OCW ran two instruments at the reserve, and they failed in opposite directions, which is what makes the pair identifying. The *Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs* paid EUR 2,500 per returner to the school *board*, not the teacher, explicitly to cover induction and guidance, on the lesson of the earlier "Terug voor de klas" project — that is, OCW had already correctly diagnosed absorption as the constraint. It budgeted for 500, subsidised just over 100 in 2018 and nearly 200 cumulatively by late 2019, and lowered its 2019 and 2020 ceilings because the money had not been spent (Stcrt. 2017, 62002; Stcrt. 2019, 28510; Kamerstukken II 2019/20, 35 300 VIII, nr. 15). A subsidy paid per appointment cannot exceed the appointments. Meanwhile the regional schemes, RAL and then RAP, put roughly EUR 30 million across 71 partnerships and required each region to run an information desk for prospective entrants, with "activating the silent reserve" a fundable goal — and the commissioned evaluation found tailored support to returners among the *least*-realised activities, while several regions reported **more applications from side-entrants and returners than there were places** (De Jong et al., 2022, pp. 37, 60). Interest exceeded absorption in the very country whose reserve is 4.5 times its shortage.

**Evidence against.** The RAP evaluation is process-focused and self-reported, attempts no causal estimate, and attributes delays largely to COVID-19 — which contaminates precisely the 2020-21 window in question. The subsidy was never evaluated at all, and final grant counts for 2019 and 2020 were never published. DUO's own register series, which shows the reserve falling 17-18% over a decade, comes with the agency's flat statement that the reserve "does not solve the teacher shortage" (DUO, 2026). So the Dutch case is strong on the direction of the constraint and weak on its magnitude.

**Policy implication.** Where the reserve is large and visible, the marginal euro should buy absorption capacity — mentor release time, induction places, supervised timetables — rather than recruitment. And EUR 2,500 per head is almost certainly the wrong order of magnitude for that: it is priced as a transaction fee when the thing being bought is a senior colleague's term.

## 7. Two margins in one country: the deadweight inversion

**Claim.** Portugal's Decree-Law 51/2024 ran retention and re-entry side by side under one ministry, one year, one union environment and one set of schools, and produced the corpus's most-quoted asymmetry — 63 accepted returners against roughly 2,200 teachers taking the EUR 750 monthly supplement to postpone retirement. The received reading is that preventing an exit is far easier than reversing one. That reading is right about take-up and probably wrong about cost-effectiveness, and the paper should say both.

**Mechanism, on take-up.** Re-entry is a step; retention is a slope. Staying on requires filing nothing and revising no decision. Returning requires re-applying through a DGAE electronic pool, being ranked, being placed — nearly half the 2024/25 applicants were available for Greater Lisbon and the Setúbal Peninsula — and publicly reversing a socially marked, once-only life transition. Resch's Austrian interviews name the operative predictor from the other side: what converts availability into return is *continued professional contact with the former school*, which decays after exit (Resch, 2025, abstract-verified). Portugal's returners averaged about 68 years old; the people who came back were those with the least alternative use of their time.

**Why the received reading is probably wrong about cost.** Take-up is not effectiveness. Nobody returns from retirement by accident, so the returner route has low take-up and near-zero deadweight: essentially all 63 are additional. The postponement supplement has high take-up and, on the government's own testimony, high deadweight — in July 2026 Portugal narrowed the EUR 750 to shortage areas only, from ten pedagogical zones and 258 school units to eight and 235, with the minister conceding that the blanket version, while it "worked", "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026; CNN Portugal, 2026). That is an admission that a large share of the 2,200 were paid to do what they would have done anyway. **Cost per additional teacher-year on the two margins could be far closer than 63 against 2,200 suggests, and might not favour retention at all.** Nobody has computed it, in Portugal or anywhere.

**Evidence that strains.** The two instruments are not offering the same money — the returner gets a full pension plus first-step-indexed pay pro-rated by hours, the stay-on teacher gets a full career-step salary plus EUR 750 but forgoes the pension — and the evidence base does not contain the replacement rate needed to compare them. The quota is not the explanation (79 or 80 applied against 200 places, so it never bound), which is what makes the comparison usable at all. The union reading — Nogueira's "the idea that retirees would return was an illusion", Barreiros' "accumulated fatigue" — is testimony from an interested party, but it is consistent with the Austrian interview evidence and with the England pilot's finding that unchanged conditions are the deterrent.

**Policy implication.** State the ordering the evidence supports — retention windows are the higher-yield instrument at the retirement margin — but refuse the inference that re-entry is therefore poor value. And recommend the calculation that would settle it, because it is cheap: a system running both margins should report the counterfactual retirement rate of its postponement cohort.

## 8. Comparisons that are too confounded to license anything

Being explicit about this is part of the argument, not a disclaimer.

**Cross-country take-up levels are not comparable and no table should imply they are.** The denominators differ (workforce, eligible retirees, measured reserve, vacancies), the definitions differ, and some are internally inconsistent. Austria's March 2023 press snapshot has Land-level counts exceeding the national compulsory-school figure because Vorarlberg's 42 includes teachers who never fully stopped; Portugal's funnel moved between 3 and 19 December 2024; NSW's "more than 600 schools" is pool coverage, not placements. Use the audited figures (Austria's 1,062; Georgia's 635) and never compute a rate from a snapshot.

**Headcount is not FTE**, per §3. Four of the corpus's headline numbers describe four different products.

**China licenses one proposition and no others.** The Silver Age plan is government-reported and unaudited, its 120,000 target spans five education strands including volunteers and part-timers, and no independent evaluation exists. It shows that tightly geographically targeted, mission-framed packages of allowance plus housing and travel can move numbers where the counterfactual is no teacher at all. It says nothing transferable about OECD labour markets, and the reported use of silver-age recruits to delay creating formal posts is secondary-source only.

**The Bahamas is announcement-stage** and licenses a design idea only — a standing reserve built on an existing supply-teacher platform. Note the union's observation that many retired educators were *already* working full-time in supply roles: the programme names an existing practice rather than creating supply.

**England versus NSW cannot license a cost ranking.** Different products, no NSW cost data, no additionality estimate on either side.

**Half the corpus publishes rules and no outcomes** — Michigan, Virginia, Louisiana, France, New Zealand, Ireland, England's post-2022 adviser phase, China's progress toward 120,000. Those cases can carry design claims and nothing else. The honest comparative statement about them concerns the distribution of *accountability*, not of results.

**And the published evidence is selected.** The three most rigorous evidence products in the corpus — NFER's independent evaluation, Georgia's statutorily mandated audit, Austria's Court of Audit — all return negative or modest verdicts, while the cases reporting success (NSW, China) publish no evaluation at all. Systems evaluate what they suspect went wrong, and celebrate what they have not measured. A synthesis of published re-entry *evidence* is therefore biased downward; a synthesis of published re-entry *claims* is biased upward; and nothing in the corpus permits a correction. This is the strongest available argument for the paper's accountability recommendation, and it should be made as an argument about the evidence base rather than as a complaint about transparency.

---

## 9. What would settle the open questions

Each of these is cheap, and each would resolve a mechanism the paper currently has to hedge.

*Does returning at the bottom of the salary scale suppress return?* Austria can answer this for the price of a cross-tabulation. Its law splits returners into two groups by former employment status — ex-civil servants forced onto the starting salary, ex-contract teachers recovering their pre-retirement pay — and the Rechnungshof's 1,062 returners are not disaggregated by that status. Splitting them prices the pay-at-entry-scale mechanism directly, within one country, one law and one labour market. It is the single most decisive test available anywhere in the corpus and nobody has run it.

*Which kind of screen binds?* Georgia has, unintentionally, designed the experiment. From July 2026 the high-need-subject determination moves from RESA level to the school system, while the 30-year service rule stays. If matching cost was binding, take-up should jump; if the exclusion screen was binding, it should not. The audit infrastructure to observe it already exists.

*Is retention really better value than re-entry?* Portugal need only report the counterfactual retirement rate of the roughly 2,200 supplement users — the ministry's own July 2026 statement implies it already has a view.

*How large is the absorption constraint?* The Dutch RAP evaluation reports that applications exceeded places without saying by how much. The regional partnerships hold the numbers.

*Does temporariness itself suppress take-up?* No case in the corpus separates the signal from the terms, because whenever a window closed the money changed too. Michigan is the closest available design — a permanent framework with a loosening that sunsets in October 2028 — and it publishes nothing. Monthly take-up around that date, with terms otherwise held constant, would identify it.

*Is a light-touch advisory service additional?* England's adviser service records roughly 128 returns over two years against a natural flow of about 17,000 a year. Any additionality estimate at all would tell us whether cheap-per-head advisory support is a bargain or a bookkeeping exercise.

*Does the cap or the stock bind at the retiree margin?* Ontario should publish the age distribution of its retired membership alongside that of the 60 who expressed interest. If the median retiree is 72, the cap was never the constraint and three rounds of negotiation over it were misdirected.

## CANDIDATE PROPOSITIONS FOR THE PAPER

1. **Re-entry rules determine whether willing matches can be completed; they do very little to create matches** — which is why Georgia's screens block hires employers say they wanted and North Carolina's expiry killed the flow, while Ontario's loosening, the Netherlands' unspent subsidy and Portugal's unfilled quota show that liberalising a rule for people who are not already at the door changes nothing.

2. **The biggest driver of cross-country variation in returner numbers is the intensity of the post on offer, not the generosity of the terms**: New Mexico's quarter-time tier out-recruits its no-limits tier four to one, and the same low-commitment margin dominates in New Zealand's relief allowance, Ireland's substitute cap, Japan's adjunct pool and NSW's casual placements — so headcount comparisons across systems are largely comparisons of product granularity, and the reserve is wide, thin and unable to fill establishment posts.

3. **What a system can see determines who it can address, and therefore what it can achieve**: register systems act on a young, large working-age reserve, while Germany, Austria and France run retiree policy by default because retirees are the only population they can enumerate — a pool bounded above by its own ageing, which is why near-universal outreach there yields under 1% of the workforce.

4. **Where the reserve is large and visible, the binding constraint is schools' capacity to absorb returners, not former teachers' willingness to come** — the Netherlands, with a reserve 4.5 times its shortage, could not spend a per-head induction subsidy and simultaneously reported more returner applications than places.

5. **Whether retiree re-employment is a supply policy or a transfer from new entrants to pensioners depends on the state of the entry pipeline, not on the design of the rule** — Ontario's 95-day cap was a shortage remedy in 1990 and, unchanged, a CAD 108 million subsidy to retirees at roughly twice the new-graduate day rate by 2012; only a rule with a veto player representing new entrants has ever been moved back down.

6. **Take-up is not effectiveness, and the celebrated 63-against-2,200 asymmetry between Portugal's two margins probably understates re-entry's relative value**, because returning from retirement is never accidental while the government has itself conceded that much of the postponement supplement was paid to people who would have stayed anyway.
