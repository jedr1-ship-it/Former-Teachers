# US State Legislation on Re-employing Retired Teachers — Verified Case Notes

Research notes for "Former Teachers: Barriers, Incentives and Policy Lessons for Re-entry".
Covers the six Excel `legislation` entries assigned: North Carolina, Georgia, New Mexico, Louisiana, Virginia, Michigan.
All facts below were verified against the cited sources (opened directly unless flagged). Date of research: 4 August 2026.

## 1. United States (North Carolina) — Return-to-work (RTW) policy for retired teachers, 1999–2009

**Identification.** A state return-to-work policy in effect from 1999 to autumn 2009, enacted and repeatedly amended by the North Carolina General Assembly (session laws S.L. 1998-212, S.L. 1998-217, S.L. 2000-67, S.L. 2001-424, S.L. 2002-126, S.L. 2004-124, S.L. 2005-144, S.L. 2005-276, S.L. 2005-345, S.L. 2007-145, S.L. 2007-326, as listed in Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024, footnote 3). Administered through the Teachers' and State Employees' Retirement System (TSERS). The "2024 Paper" in the Excel is **Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler (2024)**, *Education Policy Analysis Archives*, Vol. 32 No. 4, published 30 January 2024, DOI 10.14507/epaa.32.8241.

**Exact conditions (per the paper).**
- Baseline rule (before 1999 and again after 2009): a retiree returning to full-time teaching had pension and retiree health benefits suspended; part-time return was allowed while keeping benefits only if earnings did not exceed a cap of **half of the previous full-time salary**.
- The RTW policy lifted this cap: retirees could return **full-time and draw full salary plus their pension concurrently** — up to ~50% more income than a regular full-time teacher.
- First year (1999): return was restricted to **low-performing schools** where the retiree's certification matched need, and only as **interim instructors or substitutes**; these restrictions were lifted in June 2000.
- Originally set to expire in 2003; extended multiple times; **expired in the fall of 2009** (reverting to the half-salary part-time cap).
- Retirement eligibility in NC (context for take-up figures): age 65 with 5 years of service, 60 with 25 years, or any age with 30 years.

**Scale and results (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024; NCERDC administrative data, ~169,000 teachers in 2,900 schools, 1999–2009).**
- About **3,500 teachers (2.1% of the sample)** returned under the policy; **over 1,500 schools (52%)** hired at least one RTW teacher.
- Up to **2% of the full-time teacher workforce** were RTW retirees at the policy's peak; by 2009 **just over 35%** of teachers who had been retirement-eligible in the previous year returned to full-time work.
- RTW teachers were 9.6 pp less likely to be White and 8.7 pp more likely to be Black than other teachers; more likely to teach math (+4.6 pp) and communication (+2.3 pp) classes and to work in middle/high and rural schools.
- They worked in schools with **3.71 pp more economically disadvantaged** and **4.86 pp more Black students**; 30% of RTW teachers taught in the top quartile of economically disadvantaged schools versus 21% in the bottom quartile (significant at 1%). **54% returned to the school they had taught in before retirement.**
- Cost argument made by the authors: the pension annuity should not be counted as a policy cost (it is paid regardless); the marginal cost of an RTW hire versus a novice is the salary difference minus the novice's health benefits. No formal cost-benefit or student-achievement analysis was possible (no vacancy data, no counterfactual); the authors judge induced early retirement ("double-dipping") unlikely given NC pension incentives.

**Status as of 2026.** Expired since 2009; standard earnings-cap rules apply. (2025 session bills to create new rehire provisions, e.g. NC House Bill 106, were pending — seen in search results, not tracked further here.)

**Corrections to the Excel.** Broadly accurate but incomplete. Nuance: the paper shows high-need schools disproportionately *hired* RTW teachers; it cannot show net "benefit" (no vacancy or achievement outcomes). Add the policy's design detail (it removed the part-time half-salary earnings cap) and the 1999-only targeting of low-performing schools.

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP.** Peer-reviewed evaluation with a clear policy design, published take-up numbers and equity-relevant findings — one of the strongest US evidence pieces on pension-rule-driven re-entry.

## 2. United States (Georgia) — HB 385 (2022) full-time rehiring of retired teachers, and 2025 state audit

**Identification.** Georgia **House Bill 385, passed 2022**, codified as **O.C.G.A. § 47-3-127.1**; lets selected retired members of the Teachers Retirement System of Georgia (TRS) teach full-time in public schools (preK-12) while keeping their full pension. In effect from July 2022 (the audit's review period runs 1 July 2022–31 December 2024); automatic **sunset 30 June 2026**. The statute itself required the Department of Audits and Accounts to audit the program: the Excel's "Audit Report" is **Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, Performance Audit Report No. 24-20, "Retired Teachers Return to Work", April 2025** (Greg S. Griffin, State Auditor).

**Exact conditions.**
- Certified teachers, retired **at least one year** (no "restoration to service" in that year), with **at least 30 years of creditable TRS service**.
- Must teach primarily in an **area of highest need** — statutorily one of the **three content areas with the greatest percentage of unfilled classroom-teacher positions in the school system's Regional Education Service Agency (RESA)**, determined annually by the Georgia Department of Education (GaDOE) in consultation with the Professional Standards Commission from a five-year average of vacancy surveys. Statewide, the identified areas have been special education, math, science, English language arts and elementary education.
- Employers must notify TRS within 30 days and **pay both employer and employee contributions** to TRS. Full-time rehire is not open to early retirees. (Outside HB 385, part-time work above half of final average salary counts as "restored to service".)

**Scale and results (audit).**
- **635 retired teachers** worked full-time under HB 385 for at least one school year between FY2023 and FY2025, in **133 (55%) of eligible school systems**; roughly **350 full-time retirees employed in any given year** — under 1% of the ~125,000 statewide teacher workforce, against ~6,000 vacant or not-fully-certified positions. Most taught special education, math or science; regions employing the most retirees report more vacancies.
- Related TRS contributions totalled **USD 11.1 million** (<1% of USD 31.5 billion total contributions) — "minimal effect" on both the teacher shortage and TRS.
- Average annual salary of full-time rehired retirees was **USD 78,487** (FY2023–24), reflecting placement high on the state salary schedule; ~65% (103/163) of surveyed systems plan to start or continue using HB 385; 45% said statutory provisions blocked a hire they would otherwise have made; the 30-year and one-year-break rules and the high-need-subject methodology were the main barriers. Eight other southern states passed similar laws; Georgia's is the most restrictive (only state limiting by both subject and geography or requiring 30 years).
- Audit recommendations: if extended past June 2026, reconsider the high-need-subject methodology; GaDOE should fix its vacancy survey. Agencies agreed.

**Status as of 2026.** Extended: **Senate Bill 150 (2026)**, signed 5 May 2026, effective no earlier than 1 July 2026, "expands the return-to-work program to allow an extension to 2030, maintaining the 30-year service requirement and allows the public school system to determine high-need areas" (TRS Georgia, 2026). (An earlier web-search snippet claiming SB 150 cut the requirement to 25 years and extended to 2034 is **contradicted by the TRS Georgia page opened directly** — do not use it.)

**Corrections to the Excel.** "At least 30 years of experience" should read **30 years of creditable TRS service**, plus certification and a one-year break; "~350 retirees" is the **per-year** figure — cumulative take-up is 635 (FY2023–FY2025); add the sunset (June 2026) and the 2026 extension via SB 150; the audit is now precisely citable (Report No. 24-20, April 2025).

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP.** A rehire law with an official arms-length performance audit quantifying uptake, costs and design barriers — rare, high-quality evidence on why restrictive eligibility depresses take-up.

## 3. United States (New Mexico) — HB 73 (2022), Return to Work for educational retirees

**Identification.** **House Bill 73, "Educational Retirees Returning to Work", 2022 Regular Session, Chapter 20**, sponsored by Reps. Joy Garratt, Phelps Anderson, T. Ryan Lane and Natalie Figueroa; passed 62-1 (House) and 41-0 (Senate); **signed by Gov. Michelle Lujan Grisham on 1 March 2022** (New Mexico Legislature bill page). It amended the Educational Retirement Act (NMSA 1978 § 22-11-25.1) administered by the NM Educational Retirement Board (NMERB). Publicised by the NM Higher Education Department (the Excel's source) on 6 July 2022.

**Exact conditions.**
- NMERB retirees may return to work for a local administrative unit (school district, charter, college or university) for up to **36 consecutive or non-consecutive months** with **no limits on salary or hours**, without suspending their pension.
- **90-day layout period** after the retirement date; **no formal or informal pre-arranged return agreement** before or within 90 days of retirement (IRC § 401(a) "bona fide retirement"); **application to and approval by NMERB before starting work**; **non-refundable employee and employer contributions** during re-employment (no new service credit).
- This sits alongside older options: work at **0.25 FTE or less** (no layout, no contributions); earnings **below USD 15,000/year** (90-day layout); and the pre-2022 RTW program requiring a **12-month layout** (that older program sunset on 1 January 2024).

**Scale and results.** NMERB reported to the legislature's Investments & Pensions Oversight Committee (31 October 2023): in FY23, the first year, **154 retirees** used the new 36-month program; 390 worked under the sub-USD 15,000 option, 686 at ≤0.25 FTE and 1,716 under the old 12-month-layout program — **2,792 working ERB retirees in total**; 56 RTW violations (<2%). Around 48,000 ERB retirees were potentially eligible (NM HED, 2022). No outcome evaluation identified. [No published evaluation of effects on shortages found — UNVERIFIED beyond participation counts.]

**Status as of 2026.** Liberalised further: **Senate Bill 133 (2025)** extended the 36-month program to **60 months** (consecutive or non-consecutive) and raised the low-earnings threshold from below USD 15,000 to **below USD 25,000 per fiscal year** (NMERB legislation page; also UNM HR). The program is ongoing.

**Corrections to the Excel.** "Up to three years" is out of date — **up to five years (60 months) since the 2025 amendment**; "no limits on salary or work hours" is correct but omits the 90-day layout, NMERB pre-approval and non-refundable contributions. Note the HED news release says the bill was "signed into law in May 2022"; the legislature's record shows it was **signed 1 March 2022** (the law *took effect* in May 2022 under NM's 90-day rule — [effective-date interpretation UNVERIFIED]).

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP.** A clean example of the "no-caps" end of the design spectrum (contrast with Georgia), with official participation data by program tier; useful even without an outcomes evaluation.

## 4. United States (Louisiana) — Critical-shortage re-employment of TRSL retirees (R.S. 11:710 et seq.; Act 394 of 2024; Act 344 of 2026)

**Identification.** Not one law but an evolving framework in the Teachers' Retirement System of Louisiana (TRSL) statutes, **La. R.S. 11:710 and 11:710.1**. The Excel's "KLFY.com" source is the article **"Retired teachers can now return to classrooms without pension reductions", KLFY (Lafayette), 17 July 2024**, which reports **Act 394 of 2024 (House Bill 967, Rep. Neil Riser)**, signed May 2024. The framework was then replaced by a single unified law, **Act 344 of 2026 (Senate Bill 14, Sen. Price), effective 1 July 2026**.

**Exact conditions.**
- Long-standing rule (R.S. 11:710): a **"grandfathered" group who retired on or before 30 June 2010** may be re-employed in critical-shortage positions without benefit suspension; for others, benefits are suspended if they return within **12 months** of retirement, and re-employment earnings outside the exceptions were capped at **25% of the annual benefit**.
- **Critical shortage certification**: the employer must advertise the position (postsecondary institutions, official journal, website) and receive **fewer than three certified applicants**; eligible roles include classroom teachers in shortage subjects plus speech therapists/pathologists, audiologists, school counselors, social workers, educational diagnosticians, school psychologists, interpreters and educators of the deaf/visually impaired.
- **Act 394 of 2024** extended the no-suspension critical-shortage route to **all TRSL retirees regardless of retirement date** (including post-July 2020 retirees), full-time or part-time, after a **12-month waiting period**; retiree and employer contribute to TRSL but no new service credit accrues; agency/contract hires remain subject to suspension (TRSL, 2024).
- **Act 344 of 2026** (effective 1 July 2026) consolidates all RTW rules: keeps the 12-month suspension rule and the pre-2010 grandfathered group; adds a **no-impact route for retirees aged 65+**; expands critical-shortage areas (adult education, orientation and mobility specialists, educators of the visually impaired); requires only general teacher certification for shortage hires; streamlines advertising; and raises the earnings limit for other retirees from 25% of benefit to **50% of final average compensation**. It also orders TRSL and the Legislative Auditor to report the actuarial impact before the 2034 session (TRSL, 2026).

**Scale and results.** No published state-level uptake counts or evaluation of the teacher-shortage effect were found; the only mandated review (actuarial impact) is due before 2034. Context: BESE reported nearly 7,000 educators left Louisiana classrooms in 2021-22, +14% on the prior year (KLFY, 2024). [Uptake numbers — NOT PUBLISHED/UNVERIFIED.]

**Corrections to the Excel.** The entry is correct but under-identified and now partly outdated: name the instruments (R.S. 11:710/710.1; Act 394 of 2024 as the law behind the KLFY piece; Act 344 of 2026 as the current unified law) and the conditions (12-month wait, fewer-than-three-certified-applicants test). Caution: KLFY's claim that returning retirees are "paid a first-year teacher salary" conflicts with R.S. 11:710, under which pay follows the salary schedule crediting prior experience — treat the KLFY salary claim as [UNVERIFIED].

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP-AS-BRIEF-MENTION.** Verifiable and a good illustration of an enabling pension framework being progressively liberalised, but with no published uptake or evaluation it cannot carry more than a paragraph.

## 5. United States (Virginia) — Executive Directive Three (2022) and the VRS critical-shortage rehire provisions

**Identification.** **Executive Directive Number Three, "Addressing Teacher Shortages in Virginia Schools", signed by Gov. Glenn Youngkin on 1 September 2022** (an executive *directive*, not an executive order as the Excel says), announced with the "Bridging the Gap" learning-loss initiative. The Excel's "Education Week" source is **Will (2022)**, *Education Week*, 11 October 2022. The directive rides on a statutory mechanism in **Va. Code § 51.1-155 et seq.**: since **2001**, VRS-retired teachers returning to designated **critical shortage positions** may draw both full salary and pension (Will, 2022; VRS).

**Exact conditions (current VRS rules).**
- Directive contents relevant to retirees: the Superintendent of Public Instruction may **expedite/issue renewal licenses to retired (and out-of-state) teachers whose licenses lapsed**, and must **coordinate with VRS so all vacant K-12 teaching positions are classified to allow retired teachers to fill them**; context cited: vacancies rose from 1,063 (2019) to 2,563 (2021) [vacancy figures as reported by The 74/Virginia Mercury coverage of the directive — article text not directly opened].
- Statutory rehire route: full-time work in a **critical shortage position** (teachers, principals, assistant principals; specialized student support — social workers, psychologists, nurses, behavior analysts; bus drivers) identified through annual VDOE shortage surveys, **while continuing to receive the VRS pension**; requires a Virginia license, **a break in service of at least six consecutive months** with no work (paid, part-time or volunteer) for any VRS employer, **no pre-arranged return commitment**, and is closed to those who retired under early-retirement incentive programs or disability retirement.
- The six-month break dates from the **2023 Acts of Assembly, Chapters 690 (SB 1289), 707 (HB 1630) and 708 (SB 1479)** — which cut the previous 12-month break to 6, added the specialized-support positions, required employer VRS contributions on the rehire's payroll, and **extended the provisions' sunset to 1 July 2028** (Chapter 708 verified directly; chapter-bill mapping per VRS/RD577 listing).

**Scale and results.** No Virginia-specific uptake counts for critical-shortage retiree rehires were found in official sources (Will (2022) gives counts only for other states, e.g. ~36 rehired retirees in New Jersey, 19 in Syracuse NY). No evaluation identified. [Uptake — NOT PUBLISHED/UNVERIFIED.]

**Corrections to the Excel.** "Executive order" → **Executive Directive Number Three (2022)**; the pension-plus-salary mechanism was **not created by the directive** — it is statutory and dates to 2001; the directive's contribution was licensure fast-tracking and position classification. Add the 6-month break, licensure and no-ERIP conditions and the 1 July 2028 sunset.

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP-AS-BRIEF-MENTION.** Well-documented and current, but the directive itself is largely administrative signaling on top of an older statute, and there is no published uptake or evaluation.

## 6. United States (Michigan) — PA 184 of 2022 and PA 147 of 2023 (MPSERS working-after-retirement rules)

**Identification.** Two amendments to the Public School Employees Retirement Act administered by the Office of Retirement Services (ORS): **Public Act 184 of 2022** (formerly House Bill 4375 of 2021; effective **25 July 2022**) and **Public Act 147 of 2023** (House Bill 4752, Rep. Matt Koleszar; **signed 10 October 2023, immediate effect**).

**Exact conditions.**
- **PA 184 of 2022**: replaced the previous patchwork (critical-shortage lists, special reporting for substitutes/coaches etc.) with two simple criteria — a **"bona fide termination"** (complete severance, no work in the retirement month, no pre-arranged return) plus **nine consecutive months retired** — after which a retiree may work in any Michigan public-school position **with no earnings limit** and no loss of pension or insurance subsidy. Applies to direct, third-party and independent-contractor hires. **No sunset.**
- **PA 147 of 2023**: shortened the wait from nine to **six months**, and allowed work **during** the waiting period up to **USD 15,100 per calendar year** (exceeding it triggers temporary forfeiture of pension and insurance subsidy); special limits remain for retired superintendents. **In effect until 10 October 2028** (five-year sunset; rules then revert to PA 184's nine-month regime unless renewed).

**Scale and results.** No official counts of retirees returning under PA 184/147 were found on ORS pages or in news coverage; no evaluation focused on retirees identified (consistent with the Excel's note). Context: the nine-month sit-out dated from 2010 anti-"double-dipping" rules, and Michigan's was described as uniquely strict before the 2023 change ("Michigan is the only state I know of that has an arbitrary nine-month sit-out" — Robert McCann, K-12 Alliance of Michigan, in Dellinger, 2023). [Uptake — NOT PUBLISHED/UNVERIFIED.]

**Corrections to the Excel.** The two public acts are now precisely identified (PA 184 of 2022; PA 147 of 2023). The "six-month wait" came only with the **2023** act (the 2022 act set nine months); the **sunset of 10 October 2028 applies only to PA 147's loosening** — PA 184's framework is permanent. The claim that Michigan is "the most permissive state on this issue among its Midwest peers" comes from a personal communication (Amy Feygin, AIR) and could not be verified in any published source — keep only as [UNVERIFIED], and note it sits oddly with the pre-2023 characterisation of Michigan as unusually strict.

**FILTER VERDICT: KEEP-AS-BRIEF-MENTION.** Cleanly verifiable statutory design — notable for its explicit sunset tying the loosening to the post-COVID shortage — but with no uptake data or evaluation it supports a design point, not an evidence claim.
