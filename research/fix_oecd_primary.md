## ITEM A1 / E row 3 — Primary pinpoints for the Chapter 1/2 headline OECD statistics

### A1.1 PISA 2022: principal-reported shortage 26% → 47%, and which countries rose >30 pp

**Source:** OECD (2023), *PISA 2022 Results (Volume II): Learning During – and From – Disruption*, Chapter 5 ("Investments in a solid foundation for learning and well-being"), pp. 171–172; Figure II.5.3 (p. 172); underlying data Table II.B1.5.4 (Annex B1, Chapter 5; StatLink for the chapter's figure data: https://stat.link/6jbfey). In-volume country-profile version: Table II.B2.24 "Shortage of educational staff" (pp. 431–434).

**What Volume II itself says (p. 171):**
> "Between 2018 and 2022, the share of students in schools whose principal reported that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff increased in 58 countries/economies (Figure II.5.3), and by more than 30 percentage points in **Australia\*, Belgium, Cambodia, Chile, France, Guatemala, Latvia\*, the Netherlands\*, Poland and Portugal**."

Volume II's own caveat (p. 171): "these measures are based on school principals' perceptions; they are not objective measures of staff shortage."

**Where the 26% → 47% OECD average is printed in an OECD source:** it is NOT stated as a sentence in Volume II's chapter text; the OECD itself prints it in *Education at a Glance 2025*, Chapter D8, p. 490:
> "On average, the percentage of students in schools whose principals reported that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff increased by 21 percentage points, from 26% in 2018 to 47% in 2022. In Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Latvia, the Netherlands, Poland and Portugal, the increase exceeded 30 percentage points. … (see Figure II.5.3 in PISA Results Volume II (OECD, 2023))."

The Figure II.5.3 data file (stat.link/6jbfey, sheet "Figure II.5.3") gives the OECD-average change as **+20.5 percentage points** for "lack of teaching staff".

**Corrected text to use in the draft:**
> "On average across OECD countries, the share of students in schools whose principals reported that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff rose by 21 percentage points between PISA 2018 and PISA 2022, from 26% to 47% (OECD, 2025, p. 490; OECD, 2023, Figure II.5.3 and Table II.B1.5.4). Among OECD countries, the increase exceeded 30 percentage points in Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Latvia, the Netherlands, Poland and Portugal; among partner economies, Cambodia and Guatemala also exceeded that threshold (OECD, 2023, p. 171). The OECD cautions that this is a perception measure reported by principals, not an objective vacancy count."

Notes for the draft team: (i) the 8-country list used so far silently drops Cambodia and Guatemala — either restrict explicitly to OECD countries or list all ten; (ii) Australia, Latvia and the Netherlands carry PISA's asterisk (sampling-standards caution); (iii) *Education Policy Outlook 2024* (p. 11) uses a different baseline — "up from 29% in 2015" to 46.7%/47% in 2022 — never mix the 2015 and 2018 baselines in one sentence.

**Supersedes:** `lit_shortages` item 7 [S] and the C-table row "Principal-reported shortage (PISA 2022)" [S]; also upgrades item 32 (Australia >30 pp) to [P].

### A1.2 Education at a Glance 2025, Indicator D8 (Chapter D8, "How severe are teacher shortages across countries?", pp. 484–507)

**Source:** OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025: OECD Indicators*, Chapter D8. Key tables (listed p. ~505): Table D8.1 (age distribution), Table D8.2 (non-fully qualified teachers), Table D8.3 (unfilled vacancies at the start of the year and prior-year teaching graduates), Table D8.4 (share of fully qualified teachers who left the profession by resigning or retiring, 2022/23).

Verified pinpoints, all read directly:

- **Attrition 6.5% average across 19 systems** — Highlights, p. 484, and main text p. 493 ("Teachers leaving the profession" section): "Across 19 OECD countries and economies with available data, on average 6.5% of fully qualified teachers from pre-primary to upper secondary education left the profession in 2022/23." Spread (p. 494): "from below 3% in **France, Greece, Ireland and Israel** to over 10% in **Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania** (Figure D8.6)". By level (p. 484): pre-primary 7.3%, primary 5.8%, secondary 5.9%. Data: **Figure D8.6 and Table D8.4**.
- **Resignations vs retirements split** (new, Chapter-5-relevant): "On average, 51% of teachers who left resigned" (Highlights, p. 484; main text p. 494: "about half (51%) of fully qualified teachers who left the profession resigned"). Over 80% of leavers resigned (rather than retired) in Denmark, England (UK) and Estonia; retirements exceed 50% of departures in Argentina (primary), France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (p. 494, Table D8.4).
- **Early-career leavers** (p. 495): among seven OECD countries with seniority data, 16–68% of resigning teachers had under five years' experience (Austria ≈58%, Israel ≈68%, Slovak Republic 40%, Poland 32%, Estonia 30%, Sweden 23%, Denmark 16%); on average one in three (34%). Note p. 495: "in most countries there are pathways for teachers to return to the profession if they wish" — direct OECD hook for this paper.
- **Austria vacancies** — p. 491: "from 0 in Korea and 320 in Bulgaria to **4 778 in Austria, 5 747 in Poland and 6 704 in Sweden** … In 12 out of the 14 countries and economies, unfilled positions at the start of the year account for less than 3% of the teaching workforce. Only **Austria (4.6%) and Sweden (5.0%)** report vacancy rates above 4%, while France (0.1%), Japan (0.2%), England (UK) (0.3%), Bulgaria (0.4%) show very low vacancy levels, and Korea reports no unfilled vacancies at all (Figure D8.4 and Table D8.3)." Also p. 491: Austria's unfilled positions in 2022/23 equalled **107% of the prior year's teacher-training graduates** (Poland 94%, Sweden 77%, French Community of Belgium 72%, Netherlands 50%).
- **Attrition trend Austria** (p. 494): "In Austria and Estonia, the share of fully qualified teachers leaving the profession increased by more than 2 percentage points between 2013/14 and 2022/23" (Table D8.4). The 3.2% → 6.2% Austrian series in the corpus comes from the EAG 2025 Austria country note and remains correctly cited there.
- **Second-career pathways** (Box D8.1, p. 489): "over half (**16 out of 28**) have introduced structured pathways tailored to second-career teachers (Figure D8.3)"; 12 out of 28 do not provide alternative routes. (Note this is about *second-career entrants*, not returners — do not conflate with re-entry.)
- Caution quote on cross-country vacancy comparability (definitions section, pp. 498–499; the version in the Sweden country note is a faithful rendering and may be cited to the main volume instead).

**Corrected text:** replace every "[S] — EAG 2025 spotlight" tag in `lit_shortages` items 9–10 with "OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025*, Chapter D8, pp. 491 and 493–495, Tables D8.3 and D8.4". The web "spotlight" URL and the chapter are the same text; cite the chapter.

**Supersedes:** `lit_shortages` items 9–10 [S]; C-table rows "Unfilled teaching posts" and "Attrition 2022/23".

### A1.3 TALIS 2024 first results (published October 2025)

**Source:** OECD (2025), *Results from TALIS 2024: The State of Teaching*, OECD Publishing, Paris (revised version, December 2025). NB: the publication is titled *Results from TALIS 2024*, **not** "TALIS 2024 Results (Volume I)" — correct the running citation everywhere.

- **23% shortage-hindered** — Chapter 1 ("Teaching for today's world"), pp. 41–42: "23% of teachers work in schools that report that a shortage of qualified teachers limits their school's capacity to provide quality instruction (**Figure 1.6**)… especially prevalent in **Bahrain, Latvia and the Netherlands\***, where around half of teachers work in schools that report this barrier. In **Albania, Costa Rica, Korea, North Macedonia, Norway\*, Poland, Shanghai (China) and Sweden**, less than 10%…" (Tables 1.17, 1.19). Change since 2018: +2 pp on average; largest increases Netherlands\* and Malta (+32 pp); decreases in Viet Nam (−43 pp), Colombia (−33 pp), French Community of Belgium (−22 pp) (p. 42, Table 1.18). → The corpus line "under 10% in Albania and Costa Rica" is incomplete: eight systems are below 10%.
- **17% of non-retiring teachers intend to leave within five years** — Chapter 7 ("Sustaining the teaching profession"), p. 234: "The share of teachers who are not about to retire, nonetheless, intend to leave the profession within five years is 17%, on average, ranging from less than 6% in **Korea, Portugal and Viet Nam** to over 30% in **Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania** (Table 7.6)."
- **Under-30 intentions** — Chapter 7, p. 232: "Two out of ten teachers under age 30 report intending to leave teaching within the next five years, on average across OECD countries and territories with available data (Figure 7.1)… more than four in ten … in **Latvia (53%), Lithuania (50%) and Estonia (49%)**" (Table 7.1); +3 pp since 2018 (p. 233, Table 7.2). The "~3% in Viet Nam" figure circulating from EI coverage is *not* printed in the report text — Viet Nam is merely the lowest-ranked system in Figure 7.1; quote the exact value only after checking online Table 7.1, otherwise write "fewer than one in ten in 13 education systems, lowest in Viet Nam" [text-verified].
- **33% multicultural preparedness** — Chapter 4 ("Developing teacher expertise"), Highlights p. 137 and main text p. 142: "Just 33% of recent graduates (teachers who obtained their first teaching qualification within the five years prior to the survey) feel prepared to teach in a multicultural or multilingual setting" — the area recent graduates feel least prepared for (Figure 4.3, Table 4.5). Extremes: Slovenia 11%, France 15%, Japan 17% (p. 142).
- **SEN and PD needs — CORRECTION:** in TALIS 2024, teaching students with special education needs is the **second** most common high PD need ("one in four teachers"), **behind use of AI (29%, "more than for any other content area surveyed")** — Chapter 4, Highlights p. 137 and p. 154: "Teaching students with special education needs is the second most common area where teachers report high levels of professional development needs, with one in four teachers across OECD education systems reporting needs in this area" (Table 4.27; >40% in Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Japan, Kazakhstan, Morocco, South Africa; 9% United States). Any draft sentence calling SEN "the top PD need in TALIS 2024" is wrong — that was TALIS 2018. A separate Chapter 1 statistic (p. 41, Table 1.17): **33% of teachers work in schools where a shortage of teachers competent in teaching students with special education needs is reported as hindering quality instruction** — keep it distinct from the PD-need figure.

**Supersedes:** `lit_shortages` items 13–14 [S/P via EI/TES] and item 44 [S/P]; C-table rows "Shortage hinders instruction (TALIS 2024)" and "Under-30 teachers planning to exit ≤5 yrs".

## ITEM E row 1 (and part of C6) — OECD (2005), *Teachers Matter*: what it says on re-entry/returners

**Source:** OECD (2005), *Teachers Matter: Attracting, Developing and Retaining Effective Teachers*, Education and Training Policy, OECD Publishing, Paris, ISBN 92-64-01802-6, https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264018044-en. Re-entry is a recurring strand, concentrated in Chapter 3:

- **Chapter 3 ("Making Teaching an Attractive Career Choice")**, p. 41: the chapter's scope explicitly covers "potential new entrants to teaching and potential re-entrants to teaching".
- **p. 68 (evidence on returners):** "Former teachers are a potentially important source of recruits. In the United Kingdom the number of returning teachers has increased in recent years, and in 2000/01 they comprised about 25% of the flow of full-time teachers into the maintained schools sector, and more than 50% of the inflow of part-time teachers." Returner profile: women outnumber men; ~12% under 30, 40% over 45. Motivations (Penlington, 2002, as cited): 29% enjoy teaching, 25% suits family needs, only 7% "only option".
- **Box 3.5, p. 70 ("Support for former teachers in England and Wales"):** the *Returning to Teach Programme* — dedicated help line, termly magazine, refresher courses of 6–12 weeks with training bursaries of GBP 150/week (max GBP 1 500) plus childcare support up to GBP 150/week; the Teacher Training Agency sponsored ~100 returner courses/year; ~20% of returners attended one. (Historical ancestor of England's RTTA — useful for Chapter 4.1/5 lineage.)
- **p. 70 (§3.3.2, Salaries):** relative salaries influence "the decision to return to teaching after a career interruption, as returning rates are generally higher among those teaching subjects that provide the fewest opportunities for employment elsewhere".
- **Policy implications, pp. 88–89 ("Expanding the supply pool of potential teachers"):** "Former teachers also provide a major potential source of recruits in many countries. Strategies to tap this source include maintaining contact with former teachers to keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities, and targeted retraining schemes to prepare them for teaching new school programmes." Also p. 88 ("Improving employment conditions"): part-time opportunities and leave schemes raise return rates.
- **Chapter 4, p. 121:** "A lot of former teachers return to teaching, often after having a family. Given this phenomenon, different forms of induction programmes could be of considerable assistance to returning teachers, and not just to beginning teachers."
- **Chapter 5, p. 157:** the Flemish Community of Belgium's replacement pool "also serves to provide … a route back into teaching for former teachers"; p. 165: career mobility should be encouraged via "the provision of flexible re-entry pathways to the profession".
- **Chapter 6, pp. 176–177:** science/high-credential leavers are "less likely to return to teaching once they had left"; family-friendly policies (childcare support, part-time work) help women "return to teaching later on".

**Use in draft:** Chapter 5 should anchor its "returner strand is canonical OECD policy, not a novelty" claim on Teachers Matter pp. 68–70 and 88–89, and the induction-for-returners recommendation (p. 121) as a design principle. Fixes E row 1 (source currently absent from the whole corpus).

## ITEM E row 2 (and part of C6) — OECD (2024), *Education Policy Outlook 2024*: re-entry content

**Source:** OECD (2024), *Education Policy Outlook 2024: Reshaping Teaching into a Thriving Profession from ABCs to AI*, OECD Publishing, Paris, https://doi.org/10.1787/dd5140e4-en. Launched 25–26 November 2024, Zagreb.

- **Executive summary, p. 11:** the report's roadmap for shortages includes "Get more teachers into the workforce, by reducing barriers to entering the profession, **supporting re-entry**, addressing targeted shortages, or proposing alternative pathways". Also p. 11: shortage trend "up from 29% in 2015" to 47% in 2022 (2015 baseline — see A1.1 note).
- **Chapter 2 ("Addressing teacher shortages"), pp. 43–74.** Figure 2.5 (p. 48, adapted from Santiago, 2002) lists "Supporting re-entry of teachers who left the profession" as one of four supply levers.
- **Dedicated section "Supporting re-entry of teachers who left the profession", pp. 50–51:**
  - Framing (p. 50): "Encouraging re-entry of teachers who have transitioned to other careers aligns with a broader vision of teaching as a dynamic profession. … not all types of departures should be viewed as problematic"; returners bring "diverse career experiences, which can enhance teaching quality and drive innovation".
  - Country examples (p. 51): **England** — "a pilot programme is testing the effectiveness of financial incentives in encouraging teachers to return to the workforce" (childcare-expansion context); **Portugal** — "'exceptional and temporary' measures … providing salary increases for teachers who are eligible for retirement" (Diário da República, 2024) [consistent with `cases_retiree_return`'s Decree-Law 51/2024 reading — the incentive is for postponing retirement, aimed at shortage-hit schools]; **Australia** — the National Teacher Workforce Action Plan "includes a review of the number of registered teachers not currently in the workforce", to be followed by a plan to attract them back, possibly into "alternative roles beyond traditional classroom teaching, such as mentoring".
  - **Data-gap finding (p. 51) — quote-worthy for Chapter 5:** "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically."
- **Strategic considerations, p. 68:** "reaching out to former teachers and exploring incentives and mechanisms for their return could help address teacher shortages while benefiting from the new potential skills they bring back."
- Related: p. 48 on flexible careers ("benefits of enabling teachers to transition in and out of the profession"); pp. 55–56 note the mobility levers also "offer … multiple pathways to enter or return to the profession".

**Use in draft:** EPO 2024 pp. 50–51 + 68 give Chapter 5 its current-flagship anchor; the p. 51 data-gap sentence directly supports the paper's "programmes are announced, rarely accounted for" lesson (GAPS B2 closing note). Fixes E row 2 (previously cited once, only in `lit_reserve`).
