# How OECD publications deploy country cases

**Slice:** country material — is it the organising unit, or evidence inside a thematic argument?

**Verdict up front:** the lead author is right, and the corpus is more emphatic than his charge. In the five
publications read, country names appear in **1 of 146 section headings**. Two of the publications
*explicitly disclaim* organising by country. The 2026 working paper closest to our topic puts its country
programme inventory in **Annex B** and gives its main text question-headings instead. Country material in
running prose is short — median 56 words in the flagship report, median 28–38-word single sentences in the
working papers — while country material longer than that is moved into boxes (median 404 words) or
annexes (1 100–6 200 words).

## 1. Are cases the organising unit, or evidence inside a thematic argument?

### The headline count

Country names in section headings, across the whole corpus:

| Publication | Headings counted | Headings naming a country | Source of heading list |
|---|---|---|---|
| C — EPO 2024 | 74 | **0** | PDF outline (programmatic) |
| B — WP342 | 15 (7 sections + 8 pathway subsections A–H) | **0** | Table of contents, p. 5 [PDF 5] + body |
| A — WP347 | 8 sections + 9 aspect subheadings = 17 | **0** | Table of contents, p. 5 [PDF 6] + body |
| D — WP48 | 57 numbered headings | **1** | extracted numbered headings |
| **Total** | **163** | **1 (0.6%)** | |

The single exception is D's `4. Policy orientations for Mexico` — and D is a paper commissioned about
Mexico. Even there, every analytic chapter (sections 1, 2, 3) is thematic; the country is confined to one
terminal application section.

### Structure of the country-heavy chapter, heading by heading

**C — EPO 2024, Chapter 2 "Addressing teacher shortages"** (pp. 45–76 PDF). This is the chapter with the
densest country content in the corpus — and it contains the section that is literally our paper's topic.

```
2  Addressing teacher shortages
   Introduction
   Policy context
   Policy responses
     Getting more teachers into the workforce
       Reducing barriers to entering the profession
       Supporting re-entry of teachers who left the profession      <-- our topic
       Addressing targeted shortages
       Proposing alternative pathways into the profession
     Better allocating teachers to the areas of greatest need
       Rethinking teams' skill mixes
         Enhancing teaching roles in collaborative structures
         Restructuring teachers' time into teams
       Increasing mobility within and outside of the teaching profession
       Ensuring an equitable distribution of teachers
     Making teaching a more attractive profession
       Enhancing career structures that support progression
       Reviewing relative salaries and incentives
       Developing campaigns to improve the status of teaching
   Some strategic considerations based on the views from participating education systems
   References
```

Every heading is a **policy lever stated as a gerund**. Not one is a country. Countries live only inside the
bullet lists under those levers.

**B — WP342, framework chapter** (pp. 15–35 [PDF 16–36]):

```
Towards a framework for alternative pathways into teaching
  A. Subject Specialist Accelerated Academic Pathway
  B. Subject Specialist Employment Pathway
  C. Non-Specialist Accelerated Academic Pathway
  D. Non-Specialist Employment Pathway
  E. International Teacher Pathway
  F. Alternative Provider Pathway
  G. Restricted License Pathway
  H. School-Embedded Progression Pathway
  Beyond programmes: Other pathways into teaching
```

Eight **pathway types**, derived from a four-criterion typology (Table 1, p. 17; Table 2, p. 18). Countries
enter only as fillers of the types.

**A — WP347** organises its whole main text as **six questions**, TOC p. 5 [PDF 6]:

```
Introduction: Why second-career teachers and why now?
Who are second-career teachers?
Where are second-career teachers?
How are second-career teachers prepared and supported?
How do second-career teachers integrate into schools?
What do SCTs contribute to education systems?
How can second-career teachers be retained?
Conclusions
Annex B. Examples of SCT training programmes in OECD countries      <-- all country programmes park here
```

And within "How are second-career teachers prepared and supported?" the subheadings are **design
aspects**, not places (pp. 22–24 [PDF 23–25]): *Opportunities to transfer expertise: Recognition of previous
professional experience* / *Self-directed learning and peer support* / *Intensive mentoring support* /
*Flexible programme structures: Timetabling and delivery* / *Assessment of aptitude to teach* / *Financial
support*.

### Two explicit disclaimers of country-organisation

This is the strongest evidence in the slice, because the authors say it in their own voice.

> "Furthermore, it is important to note that the programmes showcased in the boxes are not intended to
> reflect the most prevalent or dominant approaches within each country. Rather, they were selected to
> illustrate the specific pathways described in the framework. In many countries, multiple pathways coexist;
> therefore, the examples should not be interpreted as representative of the national system, but as
> illustrative of a particular type of pathway. … **Hence, the framework and examples do not aim to
> categorise countries or systems.**"
> — B, p. 19 [PDF 20]

> "There is no magical ―policy mix‖ that can be applied in each and every situation. This is why is it
> important for policy makers to have at their disposal **a repertoire of good practices**, as offered in this
> report." *(sic — grammar as printed)*
> — D, p. 3 [PDF 4]

D states the doctrine: cases are a *repertoire*, held at the disposal of a general framework. B states the
prohibition: examples illustrate types, they do not classify countries.

### The recurring paragraph shape

Every country-bearing passage in A, B and C has the same three-part shape. C's re-entry section (pp. 50–51
[PDF 52–53]) is the cleanest specimen, and it is on our exact topic:

1. **General claim, no country named** — "Encouraging re-entry of teachers who have transitioned to other
   careers aligns with a broader vision of teaching as a dynamic profession. While education systems should
   aim to reduce attrition due to factors such as poor working conditions and low job satisfaction, not all
   types of departures should be viewed as problematic."
2. **Hinge sentence introducing the evidence** — "Several education systems are implementing initiatives to
   facilitate easier re-entry into the profession and encourage teachers to return after career changes or
   breaks:"
3. **Three short country bullets** (England 40 words, Portugal 57 words, Australia 55 words), then
4. **General lesson reclaimed** — "This represents a significant opportunity for education systems to clarify
   re-entry routes at multiple stages across a career… However, many education systems may lack accurate
   data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees…"

The countries are sandwiched. They never open the section and never close it.

**Proportions (n = 12):**

| Class | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Illustration of a general claim | 4 | 33% |
| Existence proof | 3 | 25% |
| Contrast pair | 2 | 17% |
| Cautionary example | 2 | 17% |
| **Standalone description** | **1** | **8%** |

Two things follow. First, **75% of country mentions are subordinate to a claim already stated** (illustration
+ existence proof + contrast pair): the sentence's job is to license a generalisation, not to describe a
country. Second, **the single standalone description comes from the 2010 paper**. In the 2024 and 2026
publications there is no standalone country description in main text at all — that material has migrated to
boxes and annexes (§3–§4 below).

A grammatical count reinforces this. Of the 49 country-naming sentences in B's main text and boxes,
**1 (2%)** opens with "In *[Country]*…". In A, 9 of 40 (22%) do. In both, the country typically arrives
mid-sentence inside a subordinating frame — *such as*, *including*, *for instance*, *notably*, *provide
examples of*, *the latter case being depicted in* — after the general proposition has already been made.

## 3. How long is a country mention? Where does longer material go?

Measured by word count.

**In-prose mentions (short).**

- C, Chapter 2: 25 country bullet items parsed with indentation-aware extraction.
  Distribution (words): 25, 31, 39, 40, 41, 44, 47, 48, 49, 50, 53, 55, 56, 57, 57, 58, 62, 65, 69, 73, 80,
  94, 96, 102, 111.
  **Median 56, mean 60, 92% at or under 100 words, longest 111.**
- Country-naming sentences in running prose: **median 28 words (B), 38 words (A), 29.5 words (D)**. A country
  mention in a working paper is typically *one sentence*.

**Boxes (long).** B's programme boxes, body word counts: 404, 389, 103, 145, 541, 523, 466, 403, 45.
**Median 404 words** for the eight substantive ones — roughly **7× a running-prose mention** and 10–14× a
typical country sentence.

**Annexes (longest).**

| Location | Words |
|---|---|
| B, Annex A + B (incl. "Overview of the identified alternative pathway programmes across different OECD countries", Table A A.2) | 1 119 |
| A, Annex B, "Examples of SCT training programmes in OECD countries" | 2 296 |
| C, Annex A, "Deep dives" | 6 227 |

The escalator is explicit in the text. A repeatedly discharges country detail with a parenthesis:
"Germany, the Netherlands and the United States provide examples of programmes that adapt content explicitly
to candidates' prior knowledge **(see Annex B)**" (p. 22 [PDF 23]); "Targeted bursaries, stipends and
scholarships – linked to service commitments for several years – are used in programmes in Australia and New
Zealand **(see Annex B)**" (p. 24 [PDF 25]). C does the same: "An example of the actions Iceland has taken to
increase enrolments in initial teacher education **is included in Annex A**" (p. 50 [PDF 52]).

So the rule is not "no country detail". It is: **the main text gets one sentence; anything more is relocated,
and the relocation is signposted with a parenthesis.**

## 4. Boxes: do they carry the argument or park detail?

### Where country names are allowed to sit in a title

| Publication | Boxes | Box titles naming a country |
|---|---|---|
| B — WP342 | 10 | 9 |
| A — WP347 | 5 | 1 (Box 4, Netherlands) |
| C — EPO 2024 | 5 | **0** |
| E — WP213 | 1 | 0 |

C is worth dwelling on: a 147-page flagship report on teacher shortages has **five boxes and not one is a
country box**. They are cross-*sector* analogy boxes — "Insights from the health sector", "Insights from
other professions", "What lessons can be learned from attracting new entrants into other professions?",
"Insights from public administration". In the publication with the most country content in the corpus, the
boxes are reserved for the *most general* material, not the most particular.

And where B does put countries in box titles, the country is grammatically the **predicate**, never the
subject. The typology category comes first, after a colon comes the country:

- "Box 1. **Subject Specialist Accelerated Academic Pathway**: The STEP Programme from Finland"
- "Box 5. **International Teacher Pathway**: The ULV programme in Sweden"
- "Box 8. **School-Embedded Progression Pathway**: Diplôme d'études supérieures spécialisées in Québec, Canada"

**All six country-or-analogy boxes are deletable without loss of argument.** In each case the introducing
sentence already contains the proposition, and the box supplies programme mechanics — origins, ECTS credits,
enrolment, completion rates.

**The one box in the corpus that is *not* deletable contains no country at all.** B's Box 10, "How to Use the
Framework – Guidance for Different Audiences", introduced by "Box 10 suggests some ideas how this framework
can be used by different audiences" (p. 46 [PDF 47]), carries audience-specific guidance for policy makers,
researchers and providers that appears nowhere in the main text. So the pattern is sharp: **boxes carry
argument only when their content is general; when their content is a country, they park detail.**

## 5. Three passages that synthesise across countries, and the machinery that does it

### Passage 1 — B, "Towards coherence and quality in diversified teacher pathways", p. 46 [PDF 47]

> "The literature reviewed highlights three main potential contributions of alternative pathways: Expanding
> teacher supply, diversifying the profession and fostering adaptability in workforce planning. However, it
> also shows that these benefits are neither universal, automatic nor evenly distributed. They depend
> heavily on how systems design, regulate and support these routes. … Promising examples in this paper from
> across OECD countries show this is achievable when quality is prioritised."

Machinery:
- **The evidence base is the grammatical subject**, not a country: "*The literature reviewed* highlights…".
- **Pre-counted enumeration** ("three main potential contributions") announces the shape before the content.
- **A concessive that inoculates against over-generalisation**: "neither universal, automatic nor evenly
  distributed" — a tricolon of negations.
- **Variation is converted into a moderator, not a list**: "They depend heavily on *how systems design,
  regulate and support* these routes." The cross-country differences become a variable in the general claim
  rather than an inventory.
- **Cases are aggregated into an anonymous plural** — "Promising examples in this paper from across OECD
  countries" — which then licenses a modal: "show this *is achievable when* quality is prioritised."

### Passage 2 — A, "What the evidence supports", p. 38 [PDF 39]

> "Despite the heterogeneity of SCT pathways, profiles and contexts across OECD countries, the evidence
> reviewed in this paper points to a number of findings that are robust enough.
> **First, second-career teachers are a meaningful and growing presence in OECD education systems – not a
> niche category.** Using a broader five-year category, on average, 1 out of 5 of lower secondary teachers
> across participating OECD countries fit the SCT profile… The share varies widely across systems, from 36%
> in Iceland to below 5% in Japan, Türkiye and Korea…"

Machinery:
- **A concessive preamble that names the obstacle before overriding it**: "Despite the heterogeneity of …
  across OECD countries, the evidence … points to findings that are robust enough." The right to generalise
  is argued for, not assumed.
- **Ordinal scaffolding**: First / Second / Third / Fourth, each a **bolded claim-first sentence**.
- **Negative definition as a tail**: "– not a niche category"; "SCTs' preparation needs are different, **not
  lesser**"; "**organisational support, not entry pathway**, is the strongest predictor".
- **Countries appear only as range endpoints inside the supporting sentence** — "from 36% in Iceland to
  below 5% in Japan, Türkiye and Korea" — never in the claim itself.

The companion section, "Considerations for policy and practice" (pp. 40–41 [PDF 41–42]), converts these into
**imperative headings with no country in them**: "Treat retention, not recruitment, as the central design
question." / "Invest in preparation rather than compromising it." / "Design induction around
novice-teacher status, not professional seniority." / "Strengthen administrative monitoring of SCTs."

### Passage 3 — C, close of the re-entry section, p. 51 [PDF 53]

> "This represents a significant opportunity for education systems to clarify re-entry routes at multiple
> stages across a career, and effectively benefit from the support of teachers who are returning to the
> profession. However, many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of
> potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this
> opportunity strategically. Working with initial teacher education institutions to collect data about their
> graduates could be a first step for this."

Machinery:
- **A summarising demonstrative** — "*This* represents…" — whose referent is the whole preceding bullet list
  (England, Portugal, Australia), not any one of them. The three cases are collapsed into one antecedent.
- **The beneficiary is an anonymous plural**: "education systems", repeated three times.
- **A "However" that names the shared binding constraint** — missing data on returnees — which is precisely
  what none of the three cases individually shows.
- **A hedged, actionable close**: "could be a first step".

A fourth, smaller specimen worth copying, because it generalises *immediately* off a single case (C, p. 62
[PDF 64]):

> "In Victoria (Australia), learning specialists are teachers who remain close to the classroom, while being
> able to share expertise and contribute to professional development within schools. **Across OECD countries
> and economies, similar** new instructional specialist and master teacher roles for excellent teachers offer
> higher remuneration and different responsibilities."

Case sentence, then "Across OECD countries and economies, similar…" — a two-sentence move that promotes the
instance to a type and prevents the reader from filing it under "Australia".

## RULES THIS IMPLIES FOR OUR PAPER

1. **Delete every country from every heading.** 163 headings across four OECD publications; one names a
   country, and that paper was commissioned about that country. Head sections with policy levers as gerunds
   ("Supporting re-entry of teachers who left the profession"), with design aspects ("Recognition of prior
   experience"), or with questions ("Who are second-career teachers?").
2. **Reorganise the paper around a typology, not a tour.** B built a four-criterion framework and derived
   eight pathway types; A built six questions and nine design aspects. Our case-by-case skeleton must be
   replaced by one of these two spines, and the existing country sections re-cut to fill it.
3. **State the general claim before any country is named — in the same paragraph, in an earlier sentence.**
   Countries answer a proposition; they do not raise one.
4. **Put the country inside a subordinating frame, mid-sentence.** Use *such as*, *including*, *for
   instance*, *notably*, *provide examples of*, *the latter case being depicted in*. Only 2% of B's
   country-naming sentences open with "In [Country]".
5. **Cap an in-prose country mention at one sentence, or roughly 55 words in a bullet.** The measured median
   is 56 words (EPO bullets) and 28–38 words (working-paper sentences); 92% of EPO bullets are under 100
   words.
6. **Move anything longer to a box, and anything longer still to an annex — with a signposting parenthesis.**
   Write "(see Box 3)" or "(see Annex B)" in the main text at the exact point the detail is displaced. Box
   target: ~400 words. Annex: the full inventory.
7. **Title every box with the general category first and the country after a colon.** "Conditional Licensure
   Pathway: Pädagogische Einführung in North-Rhine Westphalia", not "North-Rhine Westphalia".
8. **Apply the deletion test to every box before submitting.** Delete the box; if the argument breaks, the
   box was doing work the main text should have done — move the proposition up into the introducing sentence
   and leave only mechanics behind. All six country boxes tested here passed.
9. **Consider making our boxes non-country boxes.** EPO 2024 has five boxes and zero country boxes; it
   reserves boxes for cross-sector lessons (health, nursing, public administration). A box on how *other
   professions* handle re-entry of former practitioners would be more OECD-like than a box on any one
   country's returner scheme.
10. **Sandwich the evidence.** General claim → hinge sentence ("Several education systems are implementing
    initiatives to…:") → short country bullets → general lesson reclaimed ("This represents a significant
    opportunity for education systems to…"). Never end a section on a country.
11. **Aggregate cases into anonymous plurals when generalising.** "Promising examples … from across OECD
    countries show this is achievable when…"; "Several education systems"; "Some systems – notably X and Y –".
12. **Earn the generalisation with a concessive.** Open synthesis passages by naming the heterogeneity and
    then overriding it: "Despite the heterogeneity of … across OECD countries, the evidence reviewed in this
    paper points to a number of findings that are robust enough."
13. **Convert cross-country variation into a moderator, not a list.** "They depend heavily on how systems
    design, regulate and support these routes" — one sentence that replaces a country-by-country paragraph.
14. **Use countries as range endpoints when reporting spread.** "from 36% in Iceland to below 5% in Japan,
    Türkiye and Korea" — the countries mark the ends of a distribution inside a supporting sentence, never
    the claim.
15. **Write the conclusions as bolded claim-first findings under ordinals, then as imperative
    recommendations.** "First, … – not a niche category." then "Treat retention, not recruitment, as the
    central design question." Zero countries in either.
16. **Add the disclaimer paragraph.** B states plainly that its examples "should not be interpreted as
    representative of the national system, but as illustrative of a particular type of pathway" and that
    "the framework and examples do not aim to categorise countries or systems." Ours should say the same, in
    the methods note, and then live up to it.
