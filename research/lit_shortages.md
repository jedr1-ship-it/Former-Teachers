## A. Key claims, statistics and citations

### A1. The global teacher gap (UNESCO)

1. **The world needs 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers by 2030** to achieve universal primary and secondary education; about **seven in ten of these are needed at secondary level**, and more than half of the total is needed to **replace teachers leaving the profession** (i.e., attrition, not expansion, drives most of the gap). **[P]** — UNESCO and International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024), *Global Report on Teachers* (figures confirmed on UNESCO's own summary page).
2. **Sub-Saharan Africa alone needs about 15 million new teachers by 2030.** **[P]** — same source.
3. **Global primary-teacher attrition nearly doubled, from 4.62% in 2015 to 9.06% in 2022**; teachers often leave within their first five years. **[P]** — UNESCO/Teacher Task Force (2024), confirmed on UNESCO summary page.
4. Historical framing: UNESCO's earlier (2016-based) estimate was **69 million teachers needed by 2030** (24.4M primary + 44.4M secondary); the 2024 Global Report revised this down to 44 million with updated data. Useful to cite both to show the gap persists across estimate vintages, not that it halved because the problem was solved. **[P]** — UNESCO (2022, updated 2023) World Teachers' Day article (fetched; article explicitly gives 69M, 24.4M, 44.4M).
5. Context contrasts from the same UNESCO 2022 article: **90% of secondary schools in sub-Saharan Africa face serious teaching shortages**, and the article contrasts **56 pupils per teacher in sub-Saharan Africa versus 15 in Europe/North America**; **6 of 10 countries pay primary teachers less than comparably qualified professionals**. **[P]**
6. A **"50 million teachers" figure (adding early-childhood education to primary and secondary)** circulates in 2025 coverage of TALIS 2024 (Education International) and UNESCO World Teachers' Day 2025 material. **[S]** — verify against a UNESCO source directly before quoting; safest formulation remains the 44M figure from the 2024 Global Report.

### A2. OECD-wide indicators: perceived shortages have surged (PISA)

7. **On average across OECD countries, the share of students in schools whose principals report that instruction is hindered by a lack of teaching staff rose from 26% in PISA 2018 to 47% in PISA 2022** (+21 percentage points). In more than half of participating countries/economies, reported shortages rose; **in Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Latvia, the Netherlands, Poland and Portugal the increase exceeded 30 percentage points**. **[S]** — OECD (2023), *PISA 2022 Results (Volume II)*; the 26%→47% figure was independently repeated in Education International's TALIS coverage **[P]** (fetched). Caveat (flagged in OECD's own text): this is a *perception* measure by principals, not an objective vacancy count.

### A3. OECD objective measures: vacancies, under-qualification, attrition (Education at a Glance 2025)

8. **Across the 14 OECD countries/economies with data, on average 1.6% of teaching positions were unfilled and 4.9% of teachers were not fully qualified.** **[P]** — OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025* (figures read directly in the OECD Sweden country note PDF, which reproduces the EAG 2025 spotlight text).
9. Cross-country spread: **Sweden — 5% of teaching positions unfilled and 18.7% of teachers not fully qualified** **[P]** (OECD Sweden country note). **Austria — 4,778 vacancies, 4.6% of all primary/secondary teaching positions**, second only to Sweden; unfilled vacancies remain **below 3% everywhere else with data**. **[S]** — EAG 2025 spotlight "How severe are teacher shortages across countries?".
10. **Attrition: across 19 countries/economies with data, on average 6.5% of fully qualified teachers (pre-primary to upper secondary) left the profession in 2022/23 — under 3% in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel; over 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania.** **[S]** — EAG 2025 spotlight (consistent snippets in two independent searches); Austria's attrition rose from 3.2% (2014/15) to 6.2% (2022/23) **[S]**.
11. OECD's own comparability warning (quote-worthy): cross-country vacancy comparisons "should be made with even greater caution than in other areas, as teacher recruitment processes vary significantly, ranging from centralised systems with competitive national exams to fully decentralised hiring at the school level". **[P]** — OECD (2025), Sweden country note.
12. Policy-relevant for the paper: **16 of 28 OECD countries with data offer dedicated alternative pathways into teaching for career changers.** **[P]** — OECD (2025), EAG 2025 via Sweden country note.

### A4. TALIS 2024 (first results published October 2025): shortages, attrition intentions, morale

13. **On average 23% of teachers work in schools whose principals report that a shortage of qualified teachers hinders quality instruction — around 50% in Bahrain, Latvia and the Netherlands, under 10% in Albania and Costa Rica.** **[S]** — OECD (2025), *TALIS 2024 Results (Volume I)*, reported by TES (fetched) **[P for the TES rendering]**.
14. **One in five teachers under 30 plans to leave teaching within five years** on average; **~53% in Latvia vs ~3% in Viet Nam** (roughly half of young teachers in the Baltics). Overall, **17% of non-retiring teachers intend to leave within five years** — below 6% in Korea, Portugal and Viet Nam, above 30% in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania. **[P]** — figures fetched from Education International's detailed summary of TALIS 2024; attribute to OECD (2025), TALIS 2024 Results.
15. Morale/wellbeing: **~90% of teachers report overall job satisfaction, but only about 2 in 5 are satisfied with their salary; fewer than 30% feel their views are valued by policymakers; ~19% experience a lot of stress at work** (over 30% in Alberta (Canada), Australia, Bahrain, Costa Rica, Malta, New Zealand; under 5% in Albania, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Viet Nam). Teachers satisfied with their jobs are about **5 times less likely** to say they want to leave. **[P/S]** — EI (fetched) and TES (fetched) coverage of TALIS 2024.

### A5. European Union

16. **Teacher shortages are reported in nearly all of the 2023 Education and Training Monitor country reports**; the lack of qualified teachers "persists across the EU" and was aggravated by COVID-19. **[P]** — European Commission (2023), *Education and Training Monitor 2023* (PDF fetched and read).
17. Forecasts cited by the Commission (JRC 2023): **Slovakia's teacher gap was estimated to rise from 1,300 to almost 8,600 in 2025; the Netherlands' shortage was expected to top 4,000 teachers in 2023-24, versus 2,322 in 2019.** **[P]** — ETM 2023, footnote 10 (read directly).
18. **Ageing**: the problem is worst in **Greece (secondary) and Portugal (primary and secondary)**, with the **Baltic countries (especially secondary in Latvia and Lithuania) and Hungary** also of concern; **Italy stands out with its share of teachers 55+ significantly outweighing teachers under 30**. Ageing worsens from primary to upper secondary. **[P]** — ETM 2023 (Figure 1 discussion, read directly; Eurostat UOE 2021 data).
19. **Only about one-third of teachers in Europe are under 40.** **[S]** — European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice (2021), *Teachers in Europe*.
20. **Subject imbalances**: shortages are most severe in STEM; most EU systems report STEM teacher shortages, the exceptions being **Greece, Italy, Cyprus, Hungary, Portugal and Romania** (2020/21). Several countries also lack foreign-language and native-language teachers. **[P]** — ETM 2023 (read directly; citing Eurydice 2022 and the EENEE-NESET 2023 report).
21. **Policy coverage is uneven**: in 2023, **21 of 29 EU education systems had policies for subject-specific shortages, 14 for geographic imbalances, and only 8 for disadvantaged schools facing shortages**. **[P]** — ETM 2023, Figure 3 (read directly; Eurydice 2023 data).
22. **Netherlands (register-based measurement)**: at end-2023 the primary-education shortage was **9,800 FTE (9.7% of all positions)** and secondary **3,800 FTE (5.8%)** (Centerdata); meanwhile the **"silent reserve" of qualified teachers outside education is ~62,000 people — if ~22% returned full-time, the shortage would be fully closed**. **[P]** — Somers, Groot and van Merode (2024) in *ESB* (fetched); DUO register data put the narrower silent reserve at **34,000 (primary) and 64,000 (secondary/MBO) qualification-holders no longer teaching**, down 17-18% from 2017 **[S]**. *Note: this ESB piece is almost certainly the "2024 Paper (Netherlands)" row in the team Excel.*
23. **EU-level measurement gap** (quote-worthy for the caveats section): "There is currently no comparative indicator for teacher shortages" at EU level; national monitoring methodologies vary too much. **[P]** — ETM 2023 (read directly).

### A6. England (UK)

24. **Secondary initial teacher training recruitment reached only 50% of the government's target in 2023/24 and 62% in 2024/25** (12 of 17 subjects below target); 2025/26 improved to **11% below target** (best in four years), with 2026/27 forecast **14% below**. **[S]** — NFER (2024, 2025), *Teacher Labour Market in England Annual Report*; DfE ITT census data.
25. TALIS 2018 context for England: England had *above-average* exposure to diversity (see B1) but *below-average* reported PD needs — a reminder that shortage and preparedness patterns do not travel 1:1 across countries. **[P]** — OECD (2019), TALIS 2018 England country note (PDF read).

### A7. United States

26. **At least ~36,000 vacant teaching positions plus ~163,000 positions held by underqualified staff** nationally (≈1.67% of positions vacant; ≈5.16% underqualified), with **large state-by-state variation** and inconsistent state reporting. **[S]** — Nguyen, Lam and Bruno (2024), *AERA Open* (systematic compilation; original EdWorkingPaper 2022).
27. Most recent scan: **45,582 vacancies (31 states + DC) and 365,967 underqualified teachers (48 states + DC) in 2024-25 — together ~411,500 positions, about 1 in 8 US teaching positions vacant or filled by someone not fully certified**. Special education is a shortage area in 45 states, science in 41, maths in 40. **Schools with the highest concentrations of students of colour are 4 times as likely to employ uncertified teachers** as those with the lowest. **[P]** — Learning Policy Institute (2025), *An Overview of Teacher Shortages* factsheet (fetched).
28. **Long-run health of the profession: prestige, interest among students, entry into preparation, and on-the-job satisfaction are at or near 50-year lows**, after a sustained decline that began around 2010. **[S]** — Kraft and Lyon (2024), *American Educational Research Journal* (also NBER WP 32386).
29. Counter-narrative (important for balance; matches two rows in the team Excel): US teacher turnover is **comparable to other similarly educated licensed professions** (nursing, accounting, social work) and has been broadly stable for decades; national turnover was **15.1% in 2021-22 (8.0% movers + 7.1% leavers)**, concentrated in early-career, uncertified, hard-to-staff-subject teachers and high-poverty schools. **[S]** — the Excel's "2007 Paper" (national CPS comparison across professions; pension rules explain unusually high *early-retirement*-age exits) and "2026 Report" (NTPS/TFS-based). *The 2007 paper's description matches Harris and Adams (2007), Economics of Education Review — plausible match, confirm with the team before citing.* The 15.1% figure is as recorded in the team Excel from its "2026 Report" [source not independently opened here — confirm the exact report].

### A8. Australia

30. **The federal Department of Education projected a shortfall of about 4,100 secondary teachers by 2025** (demand exceeding supply over 2021-2025), in the *Issues Paper: Teacher Workforce Shortages* released **8 August 2022**, citing declining graduate numbers, growing enrolments and an ageing workforce. **[S]** — Australian Government Department of Education (2022) (figure confirmed in ministerial media centre search results; PDF itself could not be downloaded in this session).
31. **In 2023, 39% of Australian teachers intended to leave the profession before retirement** (up on 2021), with another 35% unsure; top drivers were **workload (75%), work-life balance (69%) and wellbeing/stress (68%)**. **~19% of registered teachers were not employed in classrooms** and **17% of teachers were aged 60+**. Only 48% of early-career teachers held ongoing contracts. **[P]** — AITSL (2025), *National Trends: Teacher Workforce* (ATWD, June 2025; fetched).
32. PISA 2022 places Australia among the countries with a **>30 percentage-point rise** in principal-reported shortage between 2018 and 2022. **[S]** — OECD (2023), PISA 2022 Vol II.

### A9. Bottom line on magnitude (for the chapter's framing)

- The shortage is real, growing in most OECD systems, and *heavily concentrated* — by subject (STEM, special education, languages), by geography (rural/remote, disadvantaged urban), and by school type (high-poverty). It is **not uniform across countries**: objective unfilled-vacancy rates range from ~0% to 5%, attrition from <3% to >10%, and some systems (Southern Europe) combine teacher *oversupply* with severe ageing. This heterogeneity is precisely the paper's premise: re-entry policy answers depend on context.

## B. Second motivating trend: increasingly diverse classrooms, and the ITE/PD lag

### B1. Migration-driven diversity

33. **13% of 15-year-old students across the OECD had an immigrant background in PISA 2022**; the share rose in 20 countries/economies since 2012 — e.g., **Germany: from 13% (2012) to 26% (2022)**. **[S]** — OECD (2023), PISA 2022 Results (Vol. I) and Germany country note.
34. TALIS 2018 exposure measures (OECD averages, read directly from the OECD England country note): **18% of teachers work in classes where ≥10% of students have a first language different from the language of instruction** (England: 27%); **30% of teachers work in schools with ≥1% refugee students** (England: 55%). In England the share of teachers in schools with multilingual settings rose **from 28% (2013) to 41% (2018)** — one of the largest increases in TALIS. **[P]** — OECD (2019), TALIS 2018 Country Note: England.

### B2. Special educational needs (SEN)

35. **27% of teachers across the OECD teach in classes where ≥10% of students have formally identified special needs** (England: 41%). **[P]** — OECD (2019), TALIS 2018 Country Note: England (OECD-average figure stated therein).
36. **SEN identification rates vary enormously across Europe — from 3.3% to 14.2% of pupils (ISCED 1-2) across 19 countries in 2018/19** (EASNIE data), with outliers reported around **1.5% (Sweden) and ~24-25% (Iceland, and upwards of 25% in some counts for Lithuania/Estonia); England ~17.6%**. The variation mostly reflects **definitions, assessment and financing rules, not true incidence** — a caveat worth stating explicitly. **[S]** — European Agency for Special Needs and Inclusive Education statistics as summarised in European Commission EPIC policy memo and related literature.
37. In the US, **special education is the single most widespread shortage area (45 states)**. **[P]** — Learning Policy Institute (2025). In TALIS 2018, **32% of principals across the OECD said a shortage of teachers competent in teaching students with special needs hinders quality instruction** — the top resource-shortage item. **[P]** — OECD (2019), TALIS 2018 Country Note: England (OECD average reported therein; England: 23%).

### B3. Socioeconomic heterogeneity and unequal exposure to shortage

38. PISA 2022: schools serving disadvantaged students suffer **more staff shortages than advantaged schools**; disadvantaged students also report lower teacher availability (64% vs 70% say teachers are available when needed). **[S]** — OECD (2023), PISA 2022 (Vols. I-II equity chapters).
39. US: the 4x uncertified-teacher gap by student-of-colour concentration (item 27) **[P]**; turnover concentrated in high-poverty, high-minority and charter schools (team Excel's NTPS-based "2026 Report") **[S]**.
40. EU: only **8 of 29 systems** have policies specifically supporting disadvantaged schools facing shortages (item 21) **[P]** — an explicit policy gap.

### B4. Evidence that initial teacher education (ITE) and professional development (PD) lag behind

41. **Teaching in multicultural/multilingual settings was part of initial training for only ~35% of teachers** across OECD countries in TALIS 2018, one of the least-covered ITE elements. **[S]** — OECD (2019), TALIS 2018 Results (Vol. I), Fig. I.4.4 (figure structure confirmed in the OECD slide deck opened during research; exact percentage from search results — re-check table I.4.13/I.4.20 before final).
42. **PD needs, TALIS 2018 (OECD averages)**: the highest self-reported "high need" for PD was **teaching students with special needs (22% across the OECD; 24% TALIS average)**, followed by **ICT for teaching (20% TALIS average)** and **teaching in multicultural/multilingual settings (15% across the OECD)** — the same three areas teachers say ITE under-covered. **[P]** — OECD (2019) TALIS 2018 Country Note: England (22%, 32%, 15% OECD averages read directly) and NCES (2019) compiled TALIS 2018 US results (24% and 20% TALIS averages read directly in the PDF).
43. **Self-efficacy gap**: only **67% of teachers across the OECD** feel they can cope with the challenges of a multicultural classroom "quite a bit" or "a lot" (England 72%; US 58%; TALIS average 63%). **[P]** — OECD (2019) England country note (read); NCES (2019) compiled results (read).
44. **No improvement by 2024**: in TALIS 2024, **only 33% of recent graduates felt prepared to teach in multicultural or multilingual settings — the area they feel least prepared for** — and **about one in four teachers still reports high PD need in teaching students with special educational needs**. **[S/P]** — OECD (2025), TALIS 2024 Results (Vol. I), as reported by TES (fetched) and consistent with OECD's "Developing teacher expertise" chapter.
45. **The teaching workforce itself is far less diverse than its students**: of 12 EU countries with data, **Denmark, Germany, Ireland, Italy and Portugal showed high under-representation of teachers with migrant backgrounds**; only Hungary and Slovakia had a relatively narrow teacher-student gap. **[P]** — ETM 2023, footnote 13 (read directly; citing a 2016 European Commission study).

Key narrative contrasts:
- **Shortage vs oversupply**: Sweden/Austria/Netherlands/Baltics face acute recruitment gaps, while several Southern European systems (Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain in several subjects) historically show oversupply and candidate queues — yet they face the OECD's worst *ageing* cliff, i.e., a *future* replacement shortage rather than a current recruitment one. This is exactly the space where retiree re-engagement and return policies (the paper's subject) differ in rationale: filling vacancies now (NL, SWE, AUT, NSW) vs smoothing a retirement wave (PT, IT, EL). [P for components; synthesis ours]
- **Same indicator, different meaning**: 47% principal-reported shortage (OECD avg) coexists with a 1.6% average measured vacancy rate — perception, under-qualification and unfilled posts are different margins and countries rank differently on each. [P/S]
- **Rich vs poor countries**: the UNESCO gap is dominated by sub-Saharan Africa (15M of 44M; 90% of secondary schools short), where the problem is absolute supply; in the OECD it is mostly attrition, ageing and allocation. [P]

## D. Caveats and limitations

1. **No comparable cross-country shortage indicator exists.** The EU explicitly acknowledges this (ETM 2023, Box 3 [P]); OECD EAG 2025 warns that vacancy data are shaped by national recruitment institutions [P]. Numbers like "1.6% unfilled" cover only 14 systems that can report it.
2. **Perception vs measurement.** PISA/TALIS "shortage hinders instruction" items are principal perceptions; they can rise with expectations, not only with vacancies (OECD's own caveat) [S].
3. **US national counts are assembled from inconsistent state data** (Nguyen et al. 2024 explicitly frame their 36k/163k as lower bounds; LPI's 45.6k covers only 31 states + DC) [S/P].
4. **Turnover ≠ crisis everywhere**: US long-run evidence shows teacher turnover comparable to similar professions and stable over decades; the policy problem is concentration in specific subjects/schools, plus pension-driven early retirement (team Excel's 2007 paper and 2025 brief) [S]. This nuance should temper Chapter 1's framing.
5. **SEN prevalence differences reflect counting rules**, not underlying need — cross-country SEN "exposure" comparisons must not be read as epidemiology [S].
6. **TALIS is self-reported**; PD "need" and "preparedness" are subjective and culturally benchmarked (e.g., England reports low SEN PD need at 6% despite high SEN exposure — reference-group effects) [P].
7. **Attrition definitions differ** (resignation vs retirement vs moving within education); EAG 2025 Table D8.4 mixes systems that cannot separate resignations from retirements [P — noted in Sweden country note figure notes].
8. **The 44M/69M/50M UNESCO figures are model-based projections** with different vintages and level coverage; cite vintage and coverage explicitly.
