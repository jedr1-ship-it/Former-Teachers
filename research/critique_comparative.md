# Adversarial critique of `arg_comparative.md`

*Hostile reading of the comparative memo against the evidence files it relies on. Every objection below is anchored in the verified corpus; where I claim the analyst has outrun his sources, the source is quoted back. Section 0 states what survives, because a critique that finds nothing standing is not a critique, it is a demolition order.*

---

## 0. What is genuinely good, and should be protected from the rest

Three things in this memo are better than anything in the rejected draft and must survive whatever else is cut.

The first is §8, "Comparisons that are too confounded to license anything". Its central observation — that the three most rigorous evidence products in the corpus (NFER's evaluation, Georgia's statutory audit, Austria's Rechnungshof) all return negative or modest verdicts while the cases reporting success (NSW, China) publish no evaluation at all — is a real finding about the evidence base, not a complaint about transparency, and the analyst is right that it is the strongest available argument for an accountability recommendation. It is confirmed by the corpus's own inventory (`ch5_synthesis.md`, Lesson 12).

The second is the single sentence at the end of §3: "**No comparison of re-entry programmes in this paper should be expressed in headcount without stating the intensity of the post.**" That is the most useful operational instruction in the memo and it is correct: Georgia's ~350 full-time rehires, New Mexico's 686 quarter-timers, NSW's 145 casuals and England's 27 permanent EBacc appointments are, as the analyst says, four different goods.

The third is the refusal at §2: "The hypothesis that temporariness itself suppresses take-up is attractive and I cannot support it." An analyst who declines his own most attractive hypothesis because North Carolina's expiry is over-determined has earned some credit, and the paper should keep that discipline.

Everything from here is attack.

---

## 1. Claims that outrun their evidence

### 1.1 The New Mexico "controlled experiment" is not one, and the tier it omits reverses its own ranking

This is the most damaging error in the memo, because §3 — the proposition the analyst calls the single largest source of cross-country variation — rests almost entirely on it.

> "New Mexico is the closest thing to a controlled experiment, because four re-employment regimes run side by side in one state, one year, one retiree population. In FY23, the tier with no salary or hours limits drew 154 users; the sub-USD-15,000 earnings tier drew 390; and the quarter-time-or-less tier … drew 686 (NMERB, 2023). Permissiveness on income is *inversely* ranked against take-up."

The analyst says four regimes and reports three. The fourth, from the same NMERB presentation, is the largest of all: "**1,716 under the old 12-month-layout program**", out of "**2,792 working ERB retirees in total**" (`cases_us_legislation.md` §3). That tier is 2.5 times the size of the analyst's winner, and its defining feature is a *long layout period*, not a low-intensity post. Its earnings terms are nowhere described as capped. Silently dropping the modal tier from a ranking whose entire point is the ranking is not a presentational choice; it is the difference between a finding and its opposite.

Worse, the three retained numbers are not commensurable. The corpus is explicit that the 154 is "**in FY23, the first year**" of a programme signed in March 2022 — a first-year *flow*. The ≤0.25 FTE and sub-USD-15,000 options are standing arrangements of long date, and the 1,716 is the accumulated *stock* of a programme that was about to sunset on 1 January 2024. A hostile reader with any labour-economics training will see a new programme's first-year intake being compared with mature programmes' stocks and stop reading.

And the tiers differ in far more than income permissiveness. The 36-month tier carries a 90-day layout, **mandatory NMERB pre-approval before starting work**, non-refundable employee and employer contributions, and a hard 36-month duration limit. The ≤0.25 FTE tier has "**no layout, no contributions**" and needs no approval. So the tier with the fewest users is also the tier with the most administrative friction, the longest wait, the shortest permitted duration and a bar on pre-arranged returns; the tier with the most users is the one a retiree can enter immediately and indefinitely. The observed ordering is at least as consistent with *transaction cost and permanence* as with *product intensity* — and the analyst does not mention the confound, let alone dispose of it.

**What the evidence actually supports:** that in one US state in one year, more retirees worked under low-friction, low-commitment arrangements than under a newly created high-permission tier in its first year of operation. That is a much smaller claim, and it cannot carry a proposition about "the single largest source of cross-country variation".

### 1.2 The career breakers are the smallest segment, not the largest

> "NFER's largest and most return-committed returner segment, the career breakers, returned at double the rate of other types and wanted part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018)."

The evaluation's typology, as recorded in `cases_england_nz.md` §1.5, is: "idealists 35%, pragmatists 24%, opportunists 16%, movers 13%, **career breakers 11%**". They are the *smallest* of the five types. The sibling memo `arg_tension.md` gets this right — "career breakers — **11% of participants** — returned at roughly double the rate of the other four types". The analyst has inflated the one segment that supports his low-intensity thesis into the dominant one. The corpus does support "most return-committed"; it flatly contradicts "largest". A policymaker who checks one footnote will check this one, because it is the England evidence in a proposition about England.

The substantive damage is not merely factual. If the part-time-hungry segment is 11% of a self-selected registration pool, then "the reserve mostly wants a few days of relief work" is asserted on the preferences of roughly one participant in nine.

### 1.3 Ontario's "60 of 142,000" is a number taken before the policy could work

> "Ontario raised its re-employment cap from 50 days to 95 for the pandemic years, and the Ontario Teachers' Federation reported that roughly 60 of 142,000 retired members had expressed interest since September 2021 (Global News, 2022)."

The corpus dates this precisely: the 95-day amendment for 2021-22 was "**announced 10 January 2022** by Minister Stephen Lecce after agreement with OTF", and in that same coverage "OTF cautioned it did 'not expect that many retirees are interested' — only about 60 of 142,000 retired members had expressed interest since September 2021" (`cases_other_legislation.md` §2.4). The 60 is a *sceptical union forecast published on the day of the announcement*, not an outcome of the liberalisation. The analyst then makes it one of the three "liberalisations that produced nothing" on which the whole memo is built ("Three of those four are *liberalisations that produced nothing*"), and later builds a §9 research proposal on it. The corpus contains no post-liberalisation Ontario take-up figure at all. Nor does the memo mention the rule's crucial feature: "**Retirees can always work beyond the limit if they accept temporary pension suspension**" (OTPP, 2026). The cap was never a prohibition; it is a price. That misclassification matters, because §2's entire taxonomy turns on distinguishing prices from exclusions.

### 1.4 "Portugal, whose salient margin is retirement" is not in the source

§5 argues that instruments follow the exit margin, then applies it: "Portugal, whose salient margin is retirement, spent its money on a retirement-postponement supplement." But the OECD table cited is explicit about which systems are retirement-heavy: "retirements exceed 50% of departures in **Argentina (primary), France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel**" (`fix_oecd_primary.md` A1.2). Portugal is not on that list. What the corpus *does* say about Portugal is that fewer than 6% of non-retiring teachers intend to leave within five years — one of the three lowest in TALIS 2024. The analyst has read Portugal's margin off Portugal's policy and then explained Portugal's policy by its margin. That is not evidence; it is a circle (see §2.2 below).

### 1.5 Smaller escalations, each individually survivable, cumulatively corrosive

- "the split is **bimodal**" — the source gives an average (51%) and two tails. Nothing in Table D8.4 as reported establishes a bimodal distribution across 19 systems.
- "Ontario's retirees average 72 years old" is used twice, once as a structural fact about the retiree stock and once as the basis of a proposed test. The source is a union spokesperson's remark in a January 2022 news story.
- "England, by placing returners into unchanged posts with **almost no** part-time availability" — the evaluation lists "limited part-time availability" as one of three barriers; "almost no" is the analyst's amplifier.
- "45% of employers turned away at the counter" — the audit's base is 163 *surveyed school systems*, of which 65% plan to start or continue using HB 385, and only 133 of the eligible systems used the law at all. The claim is defensible; the rhetoric ("turned away at the counter") converts a survey response into an observed event.
- The France refinement in §4 — the memo's own stated correction to the "outreach works" lesson — rests on the Toulouse rectorat letter, which the corpus flags as "**seen in search snippet only — article returned HTTP 403**". The analyst discloses this, then leans on it anyway to refine his headline proposition. Disclosure is not load-bearing capacity.

## 2. Explanations that are really redescriptions

### 2.1 "Rules bind matches, not motivation" is unfalsifiable as stated

The mechanism is asserted with confidence — "Cross-country variation in take-up is therefore variation in two nearly independent quantities — the number of latent matches, and the share of them the rules permit" — but the corpus contains no measurement of the first quantity anywhere. The analyst's operational rule is: where liberalisation produced returners, there was a stock of blocked matches; where it produced none, "the stock of blocked matches was thin". The stock is inferred from the outcome it is invoked to explain. Any result confirms it.

This is the most consequential redescription in the memo because it is Proposition 1. Stripped of the vocabulary, it says: removing a barrier helps people the barrier was stopping. That is a tautology dressed as a mechanism, and a well-briefed reader will say so.

It is also contradicted by its own best case. The memo predicts a distinctive signature — "Systems should expect a **spike and a decay**" — because a backlog is harvested and then exhausted. North Carolina shows the opposite: "**take-up rose until** just over 35% of previously retirement-eligible teachers returned to full-time work **in 2009**" (`lit_reserve.md` Claim 6), i.e. monotonic growth over a decade, peaking in the policy's final year. A harvested backlog does not grow for ten years. The one case with a decade of data falsifies the predicted dynamic, and the memo cites that case as its lead evidence.

The collapse-on-expiry evidence is weaker than presented, too. "Returns dropped to zero when the policy expired" is, on the corpus's wording, a statement about returns *under the RTW policy*, a category that ceased to exist in autumn 2009. Whether full-time retiree return actually went to zero, or merely reverted to the half-salary part-time channel that had always existed, is not settled by anything in the files. The analyst calls it "the strongest available proof that the rule and not preferences had been doing the work". It may be a definitional artefact.

### 2.2 §5's four "structural facts" are three redescriptions and one good argument

**"The measurement infrastructure selects the target group."** The claim — "systems that cannot see their working-age reserve run retiree policy by default" — renames the observation that Germany, Austria and France debate retirees. It is falsified inside the corpus. Michigan has the register (61,252 certified non-teachers counted, 93% of them under 60) and its actual legislation, PA 184 of 2022 and PA 147 of 2023, is *entirely* retiree working-after-retirement rules. England has the register (260,000 out of service) and its pension instrument is retiree abatement. Georgia, New Mexico, Louisiana and Virginia all sit in a country with certification registers and all legislate exclusively for retirees. Seeing the working-age reserve has not, in a single documented case, produced legislation aimed at it. The register does not select the target group; something else does.

**"Whether teaching is a lifetime or a phase."** Same circularity as §1.4. The predicted mapping — "Resignation-heavy systems build registers, advisory services and returner programmes; retirement-heavy systems build pension-compatibility rules and postponement supplements" — is tested on essentially one confirming case (England) and one that fits (France). Ireland is on the OECD's retirement-heavy list and its instrument is neither a pension-compatibility rule nor a postponement supplement; it is an annual suspension of the substitute-work cap for teachers on career break — an instrument aimed at temporary leavers of working age. Every other system in the corpus is unclassified by D8. A hypothesis with one clean confirmation, one fit and one misfit is not "the deep reason 'restrictiveness' fails as an explanation".

**"The state of the entry pipeline decides what re-entry policy even is."** This one is genuinely good and is discussed in §4 below.

### 2.3 "The mentor is the scarce input" is invented

§6's mechanism reads: "Placing a returner is not a transaction, it is an induction. It consumes a vacancy, a timetable, a mentor's time and a supervising colleague's attention — and in a system already short of teachers, the mentor is the scarce input."

Nothing in the corpus identifies mentors as the binding input in the Netherlands. What the corpus shows is that OCW *designed* the EUR 2,500 as a contribution to boards' "costs of induction, guidance and support", on the lesson of the earlier "Terug voor de klas" project. That is evidence about the ministry's theory, not about the constraint. The analyst has promoted a policy rationale into a mechanism, and then priced it — "EUR 2,500 per head is almost certainly the wrong order of magnitude … it is priced as a transaction fee when the thing being bought is a senior colleague's term" — on no evidence about what absorption costs.

### 2.4 "Re-entry is a step; retention is a slope"

An elegant line, and it does describe something real (default effects). But as an explanation of 63 versus 2,200 it is a metaphor doing the work of an eligibility analysis — see §3.5.

## 3. Alternative explanations the analyst did not consider

### 3.1 Employment law, not measurement, explains the German/Austrian/French retiree fixation

The systems the memo classifies as "register-blind" — Germany, Austria, France — are precisely the systems whose teachers are tenured civil servants. Austria's own pay rule turns on the distinction ("*beamtete Lehrpersonen*" versus "*Vertragsbedienstete*"; BMBWF, 2023, Q19, p. 18); France's six-month wait exists because a retiring *agent public* returns to their *last employer* (Assemblée nationale, 2020); OECD D8 puts France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel — career-service systems — in the retirement-heavy group. In a civil-service teaching corps, resignation is rare, so there is little working-age reserve *to* see, and retirement is the dominant exit. On this reading the missing register is a *symptom* of the employment model, not the cause of the policy choice, and the analyst's causal arrow runs backwards. This alternative also explains Michigan, which his does not: the United States has registers and still legislates for retirees, because retiree re-employment is where the pension law bites, not where the reserve is.

### 3.2 The Dutch underspend has at least three explanations, and the analyst picks the one his own §1 denies

The EUR 2,500 subsidy was applied for by the **school board**, allocated **first come, first served**, and required an appointment of at least six months of someone who had **not taught for at least twelve consecutive months**, dated within a fixed window. Underspend of a discretionary, board-applied, window-limited grant is at least as consistent with (a) board awareness and administrative effort, (b) the twelve-month and six-month eligibility screens excluding the appointments boards were actually making, and (c) the sheer smallness of the sum relative to the effort of claiming it, as with absorption capacity. The analyst asserts the third reading — "A subsidy paid per appointment cannot exceed the appointments" — which is true and empty: it tells us appointments were few, not why.

The corroborating RAP finding is thinner than presented. The evaluation reports "**meer aanmeldingen van zij-instromers én herintreders … dan dat er plekken zijn binnen de regio**" — more applications from *side-entrants and returners* than there are *places within the region*. "Plekken" in a RAP context most naturally denotes funded regional traineeship/guidance places, not classroom vacancies; the two are not the same object, and the memo's "schools could not absorb the returners who came forward" reads the stronger of the two meanings without acknowledging the weaker. The corpus itself notes the finding sits in a self-reported table (Table 3.17, p. 37) in a process evaluation that "attempts no causal estimate" and attributes delays to COVID-19.

Most damaging: §1 lists the Netherlands as a case where "the stock of blocked matches was thin" — too few willing people — and §6 says interest exceeded places — too many willing people. The memo advances both readings of the same country as evidence for two different propositions. One of them is wrong, and the paper cannot print both.

### 3.3 England versus NSW differ in what was counted, not only in who recruited

The analyst names three confounds (list quality, product, proximity) and is right to. He misses a fourth, which is arguably decisive: the outcome definitions are not the same object. England's 27 is "in a **permanent EBacc post in a state-funded secondary school** by November 2016", measured against an administrative census; England's number on any comparable basis is 62 in "any state-funded teaching post", and its front-end is 5,729 *self-selected registrations*, not 1,500 addressed contacts. NSW's 145 is "placed in casual/temporary roles" at a six-month self-reported snapshot. Ratio arithmetic across these two funnels measures the strictness of the outcome definition at least as much as the recruiter's proximity.

And the "closer recruiter converts better" mechanism has direct counter-evidence in the memo's own evidence list. Vienna wrote to 300 retirees, Salzburg contacted *every* retiree up to 67, Hesse wrote to roughly 7,000 — all employer/authority-record outreach, the instrument the analyst says is cheapest and best. Austria's audited national yield across six years is 1,062 returners against a 123,000 workforce; Germany's is "just over 5,000 pensioners … under 1% of the ~800,000-strong workforce". These cases are cited as *evidence for* the proposition while supplying no conversion rate, and where yields are known they are the lowest in the corpus.

### 3.4 The NSW retiree share contradicts the lesson drawn from it

The refinement the analyst offers as his §4 payoff is: "*outreach converts when it is aimed at recent leavers rather than pensioners*". His own case says the opposite. NSW's funnel is "**~60% returning from retirement**" (NSW Government, 2024a; Annual Report 2023-24, p. 14). The memo renders this as "about 40% of its returners were not retirees at all" — technically true, rhetorically inverted. The single best-documented outreach success in the corpus is majority-retiree. The lesson the evidence supports is about *recency and bundling* (five-year window, one-stop accreditation/WWCC/approval-to-teach), not about retirees versus resigners.

### 3.5 Portugal's 63 is explained by the memo's own §2 framework, which it forgets to apply

Decree-Law 51/2024, Art. 5(2) opens the returner route **only to those retired five years or less**. That is precisely a *timing screen* and a *stock screen* of the kind §2 says are the most expensive per unit of protection — and it truncates the addressable population to a narrow recent-retirement cohort, while the Art. 6 supplement is open to every teacher reaching pensionable age. The 63-versus-2,200 asymmetry is therefore partly, and possibly largely, a difference in the size of the eligible pool. The analyst instead explains it with "Re-entry is a step; retention is a slope" and the DGAE ranking process, and treats the non-binding quota as proof the comparison is clean: "The quota is not the explanation (79 or 80 applied against 200 places, so it never bound), which is what makes the comparison usable at all." A non-binding quota does not make the two populations comparable; the five-year rule makes them incomparable, and it is in the article he cites.

### 3.6 Georgia's 45% is stated preference from an interested party

"Georgia's audit is the strongest evidence anywhere on this point, because it does not infer a counterfactual — it asks employers for one." Asking is not stronger than inferring; it is a different weakness. School systems reporting that a statute blocked a hire they wanted are reporting a counterfactual they have every incentive to assert, in a survey attached to an audit of that statute. The memo applies an exacting standard to union testimony in §7 ("testimony from an interested party") and none at all here. Either standard is defensible; using both is not.

## 4. Propositions that are true but too weak, safe or obvious — with sharper versions the evidence still supports

**Proposition 1 (rules bind matches).** Too weak *and* circular. The sharper claim the corpus will bear is about *price versus permission*: the only liberalisation anywhere that moved a measurable share of a workforce also handed returners roughly 50% more income than a working full-time teacher (North Carolina: full salary plus full pension, ~3,500 returners, up to 2% of the FTE workforce). Every instrument that changed permission without changing the money — Ontario's 95 days, Michigan's nine-months-to-six, Louisiana's and Virginia's critical-shortage routes, New Zealand's refresh waiver — has produced no published take-up at all, and the two that did publish (Portugal's unfilled quota, the Netherlands' unspent subsidy) produced almost nothing. **Sharper: permission is cheap and buys accordingly; whole posts come at a premium price, and a system that will not pay the premium should not budget for the posts.** That is uncomfortable, actionable, and traceable to exactly the same evidence.

**Proposition 2 (intensity of the post).** True in direction, over-claimed as "the single largest source of cross-country variation", and built on a broken New Mexico ranking. The defensible core is the measurement consequence, which the analyst buries: **the international league table of returner numbers is largely a league table of post granularity, and once intensity is held constant no system in the corpus has been shown to return teachers to full establishment posts at scale except by paying a large premium.** Note the sharp corollary the analyst reaches but does not press: Japan's own evidence says only "about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools" now seek full-time re-employment even where re-employment is routine — so the intensity constraint is on the supply side, not a design choice systems can undo.

**Proposition 3 (what a system can see).** Falsified as causal (Michigan, England, Georgia). Salvageable in a weaker and more useful form: **re-entry policy everywhere is written where the pension law bites, which is why almost every instrument in the corpus addresses retirees while every large measured reserve is of working age** — the addressable population and the counted population are different populations, in register and non-register systems alike. That version survives Michigan and is a genuine finding about the mismatch between the paper's subject and the world's policy.

**Proposition 4 (Dutch absorption).** Currently the weakest link: one unevaluated subsidy, one process evaluation, an ambiguous word ("plekken"), and a direct contradiction with Proposition 1. Either demote it to a hypothesis with a named test (how many applications, how many places, in how many regions — the memo's own §9 asks this), or drop it. It cannot be a headline proposition of a policy paper on the strength of a self-reported row in a table.

**Proposition 5 (Ontario / entry pipeline).** The strongest thing in the memo and, if anything, under-claimed. It is currently framed around a single jurisdiction. It generalises: the corpus contains the *same* warning from a system with no institutional resemblance to Ontario whatsoever — Chinese reporting that localities use silver-age recruits to delay creating formal posts — and the Bahamas union's observation that "many retired educators now work full-time in supply roles", i.e. a programme that names an existing dependence rather than creating supply. **Sharper: retiree re-employment is the only teacher-supply instrument that can be net-negative for the workforce, because it substitutes for post creation and for new-entrant hiring; and the only jurisdiction that has ever wound one back is the only one whose rule requires the consent of a body that also represents new entrants.** Governance, not design, is the finding.

**Proposition 6 (take-up is not effectiveness).** Genuinely sharp, and the analyst then declines to do the arithmetic he says nobody has done. He writes: "Nobody has computed it, in Portugal or anywhere." The corpus contains enough to *bound* it: the supplement is EUR 750 a month for roughly 2,200 teachers, and the minister has conceded that the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" while narrowing it from ten pedagogical zones and 258 school units to eight and 235. An illustrative order-of-magnitude calculation — clearly labelled as such, deadweight left as a stated parameter — would convert a rhetorical point into a policy exhibit, and it is the kind of exhibit an OECD policy paper is for. Leaving it as "could be far closer" is the timid version of a brave claim.

## 5. Which propositions survive a hostile, well-informed policymaker

**Survives intact: Proposition 5 (entry pipeline and governance).** The evidence is documentary, from an identified investigation with hard numbers (CAD 108.3 million; CAD 432.69 versus CAD 227.36 per day; 12,774 graduates against ~6,500 openings; 2,861 retiree supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates), the policy response is on the record in both directions, and the union's stated principle — "The Pension Plan … is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" — is a quotable statement of the distributional issue by a party that bore the cost of saying it. A hostile reader will press two points: that a higher day rate for a 31-year veteran is a salary schedule, not a subsidy, and that crowding-out assumes boards would otherwise have hired graduates for those days. Both are answerable by narrowing the claim to what the memo already says — that the *same rule* has opposite distributional meanings depending on the pipeline — and that narrowed claim is unassailable.

**Survives with surgery: Proposition 6 (take-up ≠ effectiveness).** Must lose the assertion that "essentially all 63 are additional" — the Bahamas evidence shows retirees already working in supply roles are not additional, and Portugal's returners were selected through a quota that never bound. Must gain the five-year eligibility rule as a joint explanation of the asymmetry. With those two changes it becomes what the paper needs: a defensible refusal of the corpus's most-quoted number.

**Survives reformulated: Proposition 2, reduced to the measurement claim.** "Headcount comparisons are comparisons of product granularity" is unattackable and immediately useful to a policymaker reading any of the tables in this literature. "Intensity is the single largest source of cross-country variation" collapses the moment anyone opens the NMERB presentation.

**Collapses: Proposition 1.** Circular as stated, tautological when decoded, contradicted in its predicted dynamics by North Carolina's decade of rising take-up, and reliant on an Ontario number recorded before the policy began and on a Dutch instrument that is not a rule liberalisation at all. Replace with the price-versus-permission formulation.

**Collapses: Proposition 3.** Michigan, England and the four US states falsify the causal claim in the corpus's own files. The employment-model explanation is better and the analyst has all the evidence for it. Replace, do not defend.

**Collapses: Proposition 4.** One unevaluated subsidy plus one ambiguous line in a process evaluation cannot support "the binding constraint is schools' capacity to absorb returners", particularly when the same memo argues the opposite about the same country eighty lines earlier. Demote to an open question with a named test.

## 6. What would settle the questions this critique opens

The memo's §9 is good and should be kept, with three additions and one deletion.

*Add:* the New Mexico tiers must be re-read with all four programmes and with new-entrant flows separated from stocks before any intensity claim is printed; NMERB reports annually, so this is a reading task, not a research task.

*Add:* Portugal's Art. 5 five-year eligibility window means the addressable population for the returner route is knowable — DGAE knows how many teachers retired in the preceding five years. Until that denominator is stated, the 63-versus-2,200 comparison has no rate in it, only two counts.

*Add:* the England–NSW comparison should be restated on a common outcome definition (any paid teaching post within twelve months of first contact). Both systems hold the data; neither has published it that way.

*Delete or downgrade:* the Austrian cross-tabulation, which the memo calls "the single most decisive test available anywhere in the corpus". The two groups it would compare — former *Beamte* forced onto the starting salary and former *Vertragsbedienstete* who recover pre-retirement pay — are not randomly assigned to pay treatment. They are different employment cohorts, with different entry vintages, ages, tenure and, plausibly, different pension wealth and different attachment to the school. A cross-tab would price pay-at-entry-scale *plus everything else that distinguishes a career civil servant from a contract teacher in Austria*. It is a useful descriptive split; it is not a decisive test, and claiming it is repeats in the recommendations the identification looseness the memo criticises elsewhere.
