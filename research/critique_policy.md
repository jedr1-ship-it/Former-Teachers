# Adversarial critique of `arg_policy.md`

*Hostile reading. Every quotation is from `arg_policy.md` unless otherwise marked; every counter-fact is from the verified files named, with `fix_*.md` and `ch5_synthesis.md` governing. Nothing here introduces a fact from memory or the web.*

---

## 0. What is being attacked, and what is not

The memo is the best-organised thing in the folder. It has a spine, it prefers mechanisms to magnitudes, and §6 (the Dutch subsidy that could not be given away) and §8 (what would settle the open questions) are genuinely new work that the paper should keep. It is also, in places, an argument that has decided its conclusion and is spending the corpus on it. Four structural problems recur and they are the subject of what follows: a taxonomy that absorbs its counter-examples by renaming them; comparisons built on non-comparable denominators; a central section (§3) refuted by a list the memo itself prints two paragraphs later; and a systematic non-appearance of the three cases in the corpus that most embarrass the thesis — Ontario 2020-23, the Portuguese pay structure, and the New South Wales pay deal.

The memo also breaks one binding team rule. `TEAM_INPUT.md` §2 requires that "where the evidence is genuinely split across countries … state the split as a finding with numbers, not as an excuse." `ch5_synthesis.md` Lesson 2 states such a split explicitly: Michigan's inactive certificate-holders lead with salary, Dutch, Australian and English leavers lead with conditions. §5 of the memo adopts one side of that split as its universal premise and does not mention the other. That is dealt with at 1.7 and it is the single most exploitable error in the document.

## 1. Claims that outrun their evidence

### 1.1 "Returns fell to zero" — the outcome is the instrument

> "when the window closed in 2009 returns fell to zero. That instantaneous collapse is diagnostic. Inducements decay when withdrawn; penalties bite the moment they are reimposed."

and, in Proposition 1:

> "removing one earnings cap in North Carolina produced roughly 3,500 returners and its expiry produced none"

`cases_us_legislation.md` §1 says only that the policy "expired in the fall of 2009 (reverting to the half-salary part-time cap)". No source in the corpus reports a post-2009 count of returners. Worse, the sentence is not falsifiable: an "RTW returner" is a person returning under the RTW rule, so when the rule lapses the count of them is zero as a matter of definition, not of behaviour. Return itself did not become illegal in 2009 — the baseline regime allowed part-time return at up to half the previous salary, which is what the state reverted to. What the evidence supports is: the policy ran 1999–2009 and about 3,500 teachers returned under it, 2.1% of a ~169,000 sample. The "instantaneous collapse", the contrast between decaying inducements and biting penalties, and the "produced none" clause in the headline proposition are all built on a number nobody has published and a comparison that cannot fail.

### 1.2 "Effort and outcome are inversely related in this field" — refuted inside the memo

> "Any theory worth the name must explain why effort and outcome are inversely related in this field"

The relation is an artefact of choosing three cases (North Carolina, England, the Netherlands). The corpus's most designed, most budgeted, most branded re-entry programme is China's: ten ministries, an explicit ¥20,000-per-person-per-year allowance, a 120,000 corps target across five strands, housing, travel and insurance from host provinces. It also has the corpus's largest reported yield — "over 20,000 retired primary/secondary teachers" recruited since 2018 (`cases_retiree_return.md` §2.2). §2 handles this by reclassifying China (see 2.1). But the reclassification is a rescue of the *taxonomy*; it does nothing for §0's claim, which is about **effort**, and China is maximal effort with maximal yield. What the evidence supports: designed programmes occupy both ends of the yield distribution; design intensity does not predict take-up. The paper cannot open on an inverse law.

### 1.3 The NSW-versus-England conversion comparison is not a comparison

> "That is a conversion of roughly one in ten of the people contacted. England, over the same kind of horizon, ran a national marketing campaign and a registration website, attracted 5,729 self-selected registrants, supported 354 and placed 27 in permanent posts in the target subjects"

Three problems, each of which a well-informed reader will find in thirty seconds.

First, the outcome definitions differ at the extremes. NSW's 145 is *placement in casual and temporary roles* (`cases_nsw_bahamas.md` §1.3). England's 27 is *a permanent post, in an EBacc subject, in a state-funded secondary school, by November 2016* — the tightest outcome measured anywhere in the corpus. The same evaluation reports 62 in **any** state-funded teaching post (`cases_england_nz.md` §1.4). Scored on comparable outcomes the England figure is 62, not 27.

Second, the denominators differ in kind and the memo picks the flattering one for each side. Against the supported cohort, England converted 62 of 354 — 17.5%, *higher* than NSW's 145 of 1,500 (9.7%). Against registrants, England converted 62 of 5,729 — but registrants were self-selected volunteers, i.e. a warmer population than a cold list, which cuts against the memo's own logic.

Third, and most damaging: the evaluation records that "around 4% of *non-supported* registrants returned over the same period" (`cases_england_nz.md` §1.4). Four per cent of roughly 5,375 non-supported registrants is on the order of 215 people. England's despised campaign is therefore associated with something like 275 returns, against NSW's 145, and the memo's flagship contrast points the wrong way once the corpus's own arithmetic is completed.

What the evidence supports: NSW published the only complete contacts→interest→placements funnel in the corpus at near-zero marginal cost, and that is worth saying. It does not support a conversion-rate ranking against England.

### 1.4 "The list must be recent and warm" — the repair fails on its own case

> "Germany is the control that stops this from being a platitude about direct mail… A register of retirees is a list, and it still failed, which locates the operative variable more precisely: the list must be recent and warm."

Hesse's own *Handlungskonzept*, the primary source verified for this case, says its short-term measures target "erst kürzlich in den Ruhestand getretenen Lehrkräften" — recently retired teachers (`fix_netherlands_more_cases.md` §3, section 1.10, p. 8). Hesse's list *was* recent. It wrote to about 7,000 people and the combined short-term package — retirees plus service extensions plus part-time top-ups together — filled about 550 posts. The repair is falsified by the very case it was invented to absorb.

There is also a denominator sleight here. Germany is scored against a national workforce ("under 1% of a workforce of roughly 800,000"); NSW is scored against nothing at all. In absolute terms Germany's ">5,000 retirees teaching" is thirty-five times NSW's 145, and is the second-largest returner count in the corpus after North Carolina and China.

### 1.5 "No uptake counts exist for… Ireland" is a research artefact, not a fact about Ireland

The §4 and §3 lists of instruments that "generated no numbers at all" include Ireland's career-break substitution suspension, described as having "no published uptake". `cases_other_legislation.md` §1 says something different: "the Department publishes counts of teachers on career break (by year, sector, gender) in its *Teacher Statistics* series on gov.ie, but specific figures were not retrievable in this session [UNVERIFIED — data exists but numbers not obtained]." The data are published; our researcher did not obtain them. A memo whose §4 rests on "systems that can count do not, unless required" cannot afford a case where the system does count and we did not look.

A second point on Ireland, smaller but conceptual: teachers on a career break retain the right to return to their post. They are not former teachers in this paper's sense at all. Using the scheme as an exhibit for re-entry accountability stretches the object of the paper for the sake of a list.

### 1.6 New Mexico's tiers: the largest group is missing

> "New Mexico's own tiers are the tidiest demonstration: alongside the 154 users of the unrestricted 36-month route, 686 retirees worked at a quarter time or less and 390 under the low-earnings threshold, within 2,792 working retirees overall… The intensity of the post, not the generosity of the terms, sorted people."

The corpus reports four tiers, not three: "154 retirees used the new 36-month program; 390 worked under the sub-USD 15,000 option, 686 at ≤0.25 FTE and **1,716 under the old 12-month-layout program** — 2,792 working ERB retirees in total" (`cases_us_legislation.md` §3). The omitted tier is 61% of all working retirees and the corpus nowhere states its hours or earnings restrictions. The low-intensity tiers the memo relies on account for 1,076 people, 39% of the total. The claim "the intensity of the post, not the generosity of the terms, sorted people" is drawn from the minority of the data after the majority has been dropped, and the majority's intensity is unknown. As written, the sentence is unsupported.

### 1.7 §5's premise contradicts the corpus's main "what would bring them back" survey

> "What former teachers say would bring them back is a redesigned job — lower workload, control over hours, autonomy, relief from administrative load."

The corpus's principal survey of what would bring former teachers back says the opposite. `lit_why_leave.md` §111 and `lit_reserve.md` §31: "in Michigan, certified non-teachers most often named **higher salary as both the top reason for not teaching and the top incentive to return** (REL Midwest, 2021)"; 33% selected wanting a higher salary among their three most important reasons for not teaching; "An increase in salary was the most frequently selected incentive to return; allowing **retirees to keep retirement benefits**, better benefits, and student-loan forgiveness also ranked highly."

`ch5_synthesis.md` Lesson 2 states this as a *split* — Michigan salary-first, Dutch/Australian/English conditions-first — and `TEAM_INPUT.md` §2 makes stating such splits with numbers a binding rule. §5 states one arm of the split as the premise of the whole section, and the memo nowhere mentions the Michigan finding. This is the error most likely to be caught, because Michigan is the only large survey in the corpus that asked the question the section claims to answer.

It also damages Proposition 2 twice over: the stated-preference evidence says money is what they say would move them, and the second-ranked item — letting retirees keep their pension — is exactly the North Carolina instrument.

### 1.8 A metric is invented, declared decisive, and never computed

> "The defensible ordering rule is therefore not 'retention because retention is bigger' but the comparison stated in the right units: **cost per additional teacher-hour delivered where the hours are actually scarce**. Re-entry loses that comparison decisively for establishment posts."

No source in the corpus reports cost per teacher-hour for any instrument in any country. The corpus contains exactly two cost figures: GBP 37,400 per additional returner (itself resting on the evaluators' assumption that half would have returned anyway) and GBP 5,400 per recorded returner, which is our own calculation with additionality explicitly unknown (`cases_england_nz.md` §2.3). Proposing the metric is a real contribution and belongs in §8 with the other unresolved questions. Announcing its result — "loses that comparison decisively" — without computing it once is the memo doing to itself what the lead author objected to in the rejected draft: asserting a comparison instead of performing one.

### 1.9 "Roughly 17,000 a year and rising" sits on an [UNVERIFIED] flag

> "England's returners are the second-largest category of entrant, running at roughly 17,000 a year and rising"

`lit_reserve.md` §37 reports that in 2024/25 "41,700 FTE teachers entered the state-funded sector … of whom **17,000 (41%) were newly qualified**" — a different 17,000 — and then flags: "[UNVERIFIED: a precise FTE count of the 'returners' category for 2024/25 (~17,000 has been reported) could not be confirmed in the published headline text]". The defensible returner figures are DfE (2023)'s "c. 17,000 teachers return to state schools each year, about one-third of all new entrants" and the NFER series 16,010 (2011) → 17,230 (2016) (`cases_england_nz.md` §§1.5, 2.3). "Second-largest category" and "and rising" are the memo's own inferences on top of a coincidence of numbers. The claim is probably true; as sourced it is not.

### 1.10 Georgia's 45% is an employer opinion doing the work of a count

> "The willingness existed, the vacancy existed, the match existed, and the state's own rule stopped it."

The audit finding is that 45% of surveyed school systems said statutory provisions blocked a hire they would otherwise have made (`cases_us_legislation.md` §2). That is a self-report by employers about a counterfactual, from a survey with 163 respondents, and employers have an obvious interest in attributing unfilled posts to a state rule. It establishes that systems *say* a rule blocked a hire. It does not establish how many hires, nor that a willing and eligible retiree stood on the other side of each. §1 then elevates this single percentage into "the standard of proof" against which every eligibility screen in every system should be judged. That is a lot of weight for one survey question.

### 1.11 The incentive-compatibility barrier is asserted and contradicted

> "a returner-only concession on conditions would make exit-and-return a strictly dominant strategy for every serving teacher with the same complaint, converting a recruitment instrument into a retention risk"

No case in the corpus tests this. What the corpus does contain is several very large **returner-only concessions on money**: North Carolina's full salary alongside a full pension, which the memo itself prices at roughly 150% of a normal teacher's income; Georgia's and New Mexico's full pension plus unrestricted salary. If a 50% income premium available only to those who exit and return did not induce strategic exit — and Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler judged induced early retirement "unlikely given NC pension incentives" (`cases_us_legislation.md` §1) — then the claim that a workload concession would do so needs an argument. Presented as a "mechanism", it is a conjecture.

## 2. Explanations that are really redescriptions

### 2.1 The three-class taxonomy classifies after the fact and therefore explains nothing

The memo's central analytical device is the constraint / motivation / product taxonomy. Applied to the corpus's decisive case, it says two incompatible things.

§1: "Its entire content was the removal of a penalty."

§2: "North Carolina's implicit premium — full salary alongside a pension, worth on the order of half as much again as a normal full-time salary — was current, simple and legible, **which is why it moved people**."

Both sentences claim to say why North Carolina worked, and they name different causes: the absence of a penalty, and the presence of the largest cash premium in the corpus. If the second is right, North Carolina is the best-performing *motivation* instrument ever observed, and §2's "close to empty for motivation instruments" is false. If the first is right, §2's own explanation of North Carolina is wrong.

China gets the same treatment in reverse: "That is not payment for a preference; it is compensation for relocation, priced against a friction that can be itemised. Read this way China stops contradicting Portugal and starts confirming the same rule from the other side." The reclassification is available for any payment whatsoever. Every bonus can be narrated after the event as the price of *some* friction — travel, forgone leisure, the cost of a refresher course, the inconvenience of a commute. A category that absorbs counter-examples by renaming them has stopped being an explanation and become a vocabulary.

The repair, if the paper keeps the taxonomy: state in advance the rule by which an instrument is assigned to a class — for instance, an instrument is a constraint instrument if and only if the returner's post-return income is no higher than a comparable serving teacher's — and accept that under that rule North Carolina is a motivation instrument and the section's conclusion inverts. That is uncomfortable, and it is the honest version.

### 2.2 "Reach and accountability are the same property" is refuted by the memo's own list, two paragraphs later

§3's claim is that pension-rule and record-based instruments share a single property — riding a transactional register — that delivers reach and self-measurement together. Then:

> "Now set against these the instruments that generated no numbers at all: no uptake counts exist for Michigan's PA 184 of 2022 or PA 147 of 2023, Virginia's critical-shortage rehires, Louisiana's Acts 394 and 344, France's cumul emploi-retraite for teachers, New Zealand's Teacher Education Refresh waiver, England's post-2022 advisory phase, the Netherlands' final subsidy years, or China's progress toward 120,000"

Four of those eight ride precisely the registers the section says count themselves. Michigan's PA 184/147 are administered by the MPSERS Office of Retirement Services; Virginia's critical-shortage rehires by VRS; Louisiana's Acts 394/344 by TRSL, which requires employer and retiree contributions exactly as Georgia's TRS does; France's *cumul emploi-retraite* by the pension system itself. Every one of them is a pension-fund instrument with no published count. Riding a pension register therefore predicts nothing about being counted.

The memo half-notices and repairs: "each attaches to a **money flow with an independent custodian** — a pension fund, an auditor, or a statutory reporting duty." But TRSL, VRS and MPSERS *are* pension funds. What actually distinguishes Georgia, Austria and New Mexico is that somebody outside the sponsoring ministry was under an obligation to publish — a statutory audit requirement, a supreme audit institution acting on its own mandate, a standing report to a legislative oversight committee. That is §4's claim. §3's "single property" is a name laid over two different mechanisms — an employment record for reach, an independent reporting duty for counting — that happen to co-occur in two cases.

The consequence for the paper is structural: §3 and §4 are one proposition, and it is §4's.

### 2.3 The "aggregate price" is asserted, never assembled

> "A system's re-entry policy is not the programme it launches; it is the aggregate price that its pension rules, service-crediting rules, licensure rules and salary schedule charge a qualified person for returning… it is almost always higher than any government intended."

The phrase is good and the recommendation (audit it) is sound. But no case in the corpus contains an aggregate price, and the memo never assembles one, not even for Austria, where it has the service-crediting rule but not the licensure rule, the abatement rule or the salary schedule. "Almost always higher than any government intended" is a claim about the intentions of every government in the corpus, supported by nothing. The paper can recommend the audit; it cannot report its result in advance.

### 2.4 §4's mechanism restates the observation

> "A shortage-episode measure yields its political product — visible action — on the day it is announced. Every subsequent datum can only subtract."

This is a plausible motive story, and it predicts exactly what was already observed: nobody publishes counts. It is not tested anywhere and it cannot be, since the corpus records no minister's reasoning. There is a testable version, and the corpus supports it: counting occurs where the counting body is institutionally separate from the sponsor and has its own mandate or its own reason to know, and does not occur otherwise — including where the register exists (2.2). Say that, drop the psychology.

### 2.5 "Less job, not better job" dissolves under the memo's own leaver evidence

> "What it can supply is a smaller quantity of the same job."

The memo has already established what these people bought when they left: leavers' job satisfaction is 0.13 SD higher one year out and 0.19 SD at three years while weekly wages are 6.2% and 9.4% lower (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, per `fix_hilda_fleck.md`); English leavers accept roughly a 10% wage cut "for shorter hours and higher satisfaction" (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015). Fewer hours at lower pay in exchange for a better life *is the trade they made*. For that population "less job" is not a degraded substitute for "better job" — it is the same good. The memo constructs an opposition its own evidence dissolves, then presents the opposition as a discovery about the limits of policy. The finding underneath is real and better: re-entry can supply precisely the trade leavers already revealed they would accept, which is why hours-based offers out-recruit post-based offers everywhere. That is developed at 4.4.

## 3. Alternative explanations the memo did not consider

### 3.1 It is the size of the current-cash premium, not the class of the instrument — and Ontario 2020-23 proves it

This is the most important omission in the document.

The corpus contains three clean constraint removals. New Mexico removed essentially every restriction and its unrestricted tier drew 154 users against roughly 48,000 potentially eligible retirees. North Carolina removed the earnings cap and paid full salary alongside a full pension, roughly 150% of normal income, and drew about 3,500. And Ontario — which the memo cites twice, for crowding-out and for cap history, and never for this — restored the 95-day cap for 2020/21 through 2022/23 during an acute staffing crisis, and the result is recorded in `cases_other_legislation.md` §2.4:

> "OTF cautioned it did 'not expect that many retirees are interested' — only **about 60 of 142,000 retired members** had expressed interest since September 2021 — and noted the average age of retirees was 72."

Sixty people out of 142,000, in a system in crisis, from a pure constraint removal of the exact type the memo's Propositions 1 and 2 recommend. Within the memo's own preferred instrument class the variance is enormous, and it tracks one thing almost perfectly: how much current cash the returner ends up with. New Mexico and Ontario removed a restriction and offered no premium; almost nobody came. North Carolina removed a restriction and thereby handed over half a salary again; thousands came.

The rival hypothesis — **re-entry responds to the size of the current, legible cash premium, and North Carolina is the only case in the corpus that offered a large one** — explains everything §1 and §2 explain, plus New Mexico and Ontario, which §1 and §2 must treat as unexplained exceptions. It is also what Fitzpatrick actually supports: the 20-cents-on-the-dollar result is about the *form* of money (current versus deferred), not about money being ineffective. The memo cites Fitzpatrick in support of a claim Fitzpatrick does not make.

The paper cannot publish §1 and §2 without confronting Ontario 2020-23. A well-informed policymaker in Ontario, Wellington or Santa Fe will produce it immediately.

### 3.2 Portugal's 63 is a pay cut, not a rejected preference — and the memo's own §1 says so

> "The money was comparable in kind. What differed was that Article 6 asked people to defer a transition and Article 5 asked people to reverse one."

The money is not comparable in kind. Article 5 pays the returner their pension plus "an additional compensation pegged to the pay index of the **1st step (1.º escalão)** of the teaching career salary scale, **pro-rated by the number of teaching hours assigned**". Article 6 pays EUR 750 a month **on top of a full career-scale salary** to a teacher at or near the end of the scale (`cases_retiree_return.md` §3.2; `fix_austria_portugal.md` D1.1). For a teacher with thirty-five years of service these are opposite offers: the bottom rung of the scale for part of a timetable, versus a supplement on top of the top rung.

And the memo's own §1 has already named this instrument and called it the paradigm state-imposed penalty. Austria's civil-servant returners are excluded from prior-service crediting and re-employed at the "Lehrpersonen-Einstiegsgehalt" (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Question 19, p. 18), which §1 presents as pension law imposing an unintended price on return. **Portugal wrote the same penalty directly into the returner offer.** Article 5 is not a test of whether money can reverse a preference; it is another observation of the salary-step penalty §1 is about.

If that reading is right — and it is at least as well supported as the memo's — then Proposition 2 is refuted by the case it leads with, and §7's "direct observation" for putting retention ahead of re-entry evaporates, because the two margins were not offered comparable money.

Three further strains on the same comparison, none acknowledged: Article 5 was capped at a **200-place quota** and restricted to those retired **five years or less**, while Article 6 was open to every teacher reaching pensionable age, so the populations and the ceilings differ by construction; 63 were accepted out of 79–80 applicants, so a selection screen removed a fifth of the demonstrated willingness; and the Portuguese government's own July 2026 verdict on Article 6 was that the blanket version "in many cases was not justified", i.e. much of the 2,200 is deadweight. The memo notes the last of these and then keeps using the raw ratio anyway.

### 3.3 New South Wales had just raised pay and cut admin load — and the memo never says so

§3 attributes NSW's 145 placements to the warm list. `cases_nsw_bahamas.md` §1.2 attributes them elsewhere:

> "**Financial incentives:** no program-specific re-entry bonus. The pull factors cited are system-wide: the October 2023 pay deal (described as the largest teacher pay increase in decades, with removal of the public sector wage cap) and measures to reduce administrative workload… The teach.NSW returner page still leads with the pay rise as the incentive."

The letters went out from November 2023, weeks after the pay deal. The obvious rival explanation for NSW's funnel — the offer changed, and the letter told people it had — is never considered. This is doubly costly because NSW is simultaneously the memo's best evidence for §3 and the corpus's best counter-example to §5. §5 says re-entry policy is "structurally incapable" of delivering a redesigned job, on grounds of jurisdiction. NSW's re-entry outreach rode on exactly the two system-level changes §5 says a recruitment channel has no authority over: pay and administrative load. The correct lesson — outreach is a channel that transmits a changed offer, and is worth little when the offer has not changed — is more useful than either §3's or §5's, and the memo has the material for it.

### 3.4 NSW's own composition undercuts §3's conclusion

> "the register has to be an employment register, not a pension register, if the object is working-age leavers"

NSW used an employment register of people who had resigned from the NSW public system in the previous five years — and about **60% of the 145 returned from retirement** (`cases_nsw_bahamas.md` §1.3). The employment register delivered mostly retirees. Either the contact list was not confined to working-age resigners, or working-age resigners converted at a much lower rate than retirees did. Both readings damage the conclusion, and the memo, which quotes the 60% figure approvingly in §3's opening paragraph, never notices it contradicts the paragraph's own conclusion.

### 3.5 A demographic ceiling that moves when no instrument moves

Three independent systems show retiree return falling while the rules stayed constant or loosened. Japan: "only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools" now seek full-time re-employment, and ageing adjuncts increasingly decline the most demanding cover (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203). Ontario: 60 interested of 142,000 members, average age 72, during the maximum-generosity window. Austria: intakes peak in 2021/22–2022/23 and fall thereafter, with the Rechnungshof stressing "the temporary character of the measure" (`fix_austria_portugal.md` D2).

A theory of *instruments* cannot explain a series that moves when no instrument moves. The memo mentions the ceiling once, in §7, as a reason not to spend. It never asks the prior question: how much of the corpus's uniformly small retiree numbers is measuring a design failure at all, rather than a stock that is old, shrinking and mostly uninterested? On the corpus's own evidence, that is the more parsimonious account of Germany, Austria, Ontario and Japan together — four cases the memo explains with four different design stories.

### 3.6 Cheaper explanations for an unspent EUR 2,500

> "willingness was not scarce and induction money was not scarce. Placements were. This is the most informative single fact in the file."

Competing readings the corpus supports and the memo does not weigh. The subsidy required the appointee not to have taught for at least twelve consecutive months, an appointment of at least six months, primary education only, first-come-first-served, and a board that knew the scheme existed (`fix_netherlands_more_cases.md` §1.1). EUR 2,500 may simply be below the real cost of inducting a returner, in which case boards that hired anyway had no reason to claim — non-claiming is not non-hiring. The definition of *herintreder* covers people who "never started" a career in primary education, so the subsidised population is not the memo's population.

Most importantly, the absorption evidence is thinner than its billing. It is one line of a self-reported process evaluation — "meer aanmeldingen van zij-instromers en herintreders … dan dat er plekken zijn binnen de regio" (De Jong et al., Table 3.17, p. 37) — about side-entrants **and** returners jointly, and the least-realised-activity finding is flagged for secondary and MBO regions (p. 60) while the subsidy was primary-only. Sector and population both mismatch. And the whole claim sits uneasily beside a national shortage of 13,600 FTE: a country short 13,600 teachers is not, on its face, a country that cannot place returners. There is a reconciliation available — absorption capacity means supervision capacity for people who need induction, not vacancies — but the memo does not make it, and without it §6's conclusion is an over-read of one table row.

### 3.7 England failed on timing; the memo draws a lesson about audience

> "Stop national marketing to an anonymous population: England's own evaluation found national advertising played a small role relative to online search, and the campaign converted 27 people."

The evaluators' own diagnosis of the failure is: "timing (support delivered after the hiring cycle), recruitment challenges, suitability of some participants, and lack of practical teaching opportunities in some schools" (`cases_england_nz.md` §1.5). Marketing is not on the list. Registrations came in at 5,729 against a target of ~3,000 — nearly double. The campaign was the one part of the pilot that beat its target. §7 even names the correct fix, which England itself adopted immediately: the successor programme "tied funded places to actual vacancies" — a *timing and conversion* fix. The memo then bills it as a lesson about whom you address. Recruitment interest was not the constraint; the memo's own §5 heading ("Interest is cheap; conversion is the constraint", `ch5_synthesis.md` Lesson 5) says so.

## 4. Propositions that are true but too weak, safe or obvious — with sharper versions the evidence still supports

### 4.1 Proposition 4 (accountability in the founding instrument) — a drafting tip in the costume of a finding

Every finance ministry in the OECD knows the words "sunset clause" and "mandated evaluation", and knows they are the first clauses traded away in committee. As stated — mandate a count, name a custodian, add a sunset — the proposition tells a policymaker nothing they have not refused to do before.

**Sharper version the evidence supports:** counting survives only where it is a **by-product of somebody else's obligation**, never where it is the sponsor's own good intention — and the register alone is not enough. Georgia's numbers exist because the statute ordered an arms-length audit; Austria's because a supreme audit institution acted on its own mandate; New Mexico's because the Educational Retirement Board reports tiered participation to a legislative oversight committee. Michigan, Virginia, Louisiana and France all sit inside pension systems that hold the same data and publish nothing. So the instruction is not "require a report" but: **put the count inside the standing duty of a body that does not answer to the sponsor, and if you cannot, expect no evidence and budget on that assumption.** That version is falsifiable, explains the four negative cases the memo's §3 cannot, and absorbs the surviving half of Proposition 3.

### 4.2 Proposition 1 (audit the price) — correct, cheap, and as stated undemanding

"Look at your own rules before you spend money" is not a proposition a minister can disagree with, which is why it will change nothing. It is also, as written, unfalsifiable: no observation could show that a system's aggregate return price is *not* higher than intended, since nobody computes it.

**Sharper version the evidence supports:** the single most consequential number in re-entry policy is **the salary step a returner is placed on**, and most systems set it inadvertently, in pension law, without knowing they have set it. The corpus contains two documented settings of that dial with opposite results. Austria excludes pensioned service from prior-service crediting, so civil-servant returners drop to the *Einstiegsgehalt*: 1,062 returners over six years against 123,000 teachers. North Carolina paid full salary alongside a full pension: about 3,500 over ten years against 169,000. Portugal wrote the Austrian setting into its returner offer (first-step indexation, pro-rated) and got 63 (3.2 above). This version is specific, falsifiable, names an instrument a minister can actually move, and — unlike the cap-removal framing — survives Ontario 2020-23 and New Mexico, because it is about the level of pay, not the removal of a limit.

### 4.3 Proposition 3 (registers) — split it or lose it

As a single property it dies to §2.2 above. Split, both halves are usable and both are weaker than the memo claims.

The reach half: **outreach to a named list of the employer's own recent leavers, bundled with one-stop re-licensure, is the cheapest instrument documented anywhere in the corpus** — no legislation, no budget line, only the Department's own staffing records. State it with its three honest weaknesses: no counterfactual exists anywhere (§8 says so); England's supported cohort converted at a higher rate than NSW's contacted list (1.3); and Hesse wrote to recent retirees and got very little (1.4). It remains the best value-for-money candidate in the corpus precisely *because* it is nearly free, not because its conversion rate is proven superior. The counting half belongs to 4.1.

### 4.4 Proposition 5 ("structurally incapable") — the best material in the memo, weakened by an impossibility claim it cannot defend

"Structurally incapable" is not established. Of the three barriers, jurisdiction is an argument, incentive compatibility is an unevidenced conjecture that the corpus's own returner-only cash concessions strain (1.11), and the cost barrier is a claim about affordability with no costing. And NSW is a documented case of a system doing the redesign — pay and administrative load — and running re-entry outreach into it (3.3).

**Sharper version the evidence supports, and the strongest single claim available to this paper:** what leavers bought when they left was fewer hours and more control, at a price they were willing to pay in wages — satisfaction 0.13 SD higher at one year and 0.19 SD at three, wages 6.2% and 9.4% lower (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano); roughly a 10% wage cut accepted for shorter hours in England (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015); Dutch inactive teachers rating conditions in their current jobs *more* favourably than active teachers rate theirs (Somers et al., 2024). **A part-time, intermittent or role-specific offer is therefore not a consolation prize but the only offer that matches the trade these people have already revealed they will accept** — which is why every system that recruits former teachers at any scale sells hours rather than posts: New Zealand's twenty half-days, Ontario's fifty days, Ireland's substitute route, Japan's adjunct pool at roughly 60% retirees, New South Wales's casual and temporary placements, New Mexico's low-intensity tiers, Portugal's pro-rating by hours, and NFER's career breakers returning at double the rate of other types while wanting part-time work schools rarely advertise.

This version explains the same cases, drops an impossibility claim a hostile reader will break with NSW, and converts a limitation into an instruction: **advertise the hours, not the post.** It must carry the Michigan qualifier from 1.7 — American certificate-holders name salary first — stated as the split the team has ruled must be stated.

### 4.5 Proposition 6 (four conditions for zero spend) — the most operational content in the memo, and it is in the reserve slot

This is the only place where the paper is willing to say re-entry policy can be actively harmful, which is exactly the kind of position `TEAM_INPUT.md` §5 demands and the rejected draft lacked. Condition two — a loose entry pipeline turns rehiring into a transfer from new graduates to pensioners, documented at CAD 108.3 million a year and CAD 432.69 against CAD 227.36 a day — deserves promotion to the main list, not reserve status.

Condition four should be hedged or dropped. It rests on 23 physics returners "from more than 111 physics leads given an adviser, against 56 in modern foreign languages and 49 in mathematics". The corpus records a leads denominator for physics only (`cases_england_nz.md` §2.3), so the memo compares a conversion rate against two raw counts and concludes that selection into return runs against scarcity. It may well; on this evidence it is a hypothesis, and belongs in §8 with the others.

## 5. Which propositions survive a hostile reading, and which collapse

**Proposition 1 — survives, wounded, and only if rewritten.** The audit recommendation is sound and cheap. Two things must go: the "its expiry produced none" clause (1.1, unsupported and definitional) and the implication that removing a return penalty reliably produces returners, which Ontario 2020-23 and New Mexico refute (3.1). Recast on the salary-step dial (4.2), it survives a hostile reading and gains a falsifiable edge. Left as written, the first question from a well-briefed official is "we lifted our cap and sixty people were interested — what does your theory say about that?", and the memo has no answer.

**Proposition 2 — collapses.** It leads with a case where the returner offer was structurally worse money than the stayer offer (3.2), rescues itself from its largest counter-example by reclassification (2.1), and asserts "never" against the corpus's own stated-preference evidence that salary is both the top reason for not teaching and the top incentive to return (1.7). The word "never" cannot be defended in a room where anyone has read the Michigan survey. There is a defensible residue — deferred, conditional or complex money is discounted almost to nothing, per Fitzpatrick's twenty cents on the dollar, so pay current and legible — but that is a claim about the *form* of money, not about its impotence, and it should be folded into Proposition 1's salary-step version rather than carried as a proposition of its own.

**Proposition 3 — collapses as stated; half survives separately.** The "single property" claim is refuted by the memo's own list of uncounted instruments, four of which ride pension registers (2.2), and the conversion-rate contrast that anchors it does not survive completing the England arithmetic (1.3). The reach half — a warm list of your own recent leavers plus one-stop re-licensure is the cheapest thing you can do — survives as a modest, honest claim with no counterfactual anywhere. The counting half is Proposition 4.

**Proposition 4 — survives as true and dies as interesting, unless recast.** No policymaker will contest it; none will act on it either. Recast around the independent custodian and the four negative pension-register cases (4.1), it becomes both defensible and non-obvious, and it absorbs what is left of Proposition 3. Merge them; the paper is one proposition better off, not two worse.

**Proposition 5 — survives, and is the paper's best material, once the impossibility claim is dropped.** "Structurally incapable" invites a rebuttal the corpus supplies (NSW). The trade-matching version (4.4) says something a minister has not heard, follows from the leaver evidence rather than from a jurisdictional argument, explains every hours-based instrument in the corpus, and yields an operational instruction. It must carry the Michigan split honestly. This is the proposition to build the chapter on.

**Proposition 6 — survives; promote it out of the reserve.** Condition two is the sharpest thing in the memo. Condition four should be demoted to §8.

**The section that should be kept almost untouched:** §8. Naming five administrative discontinuities that are natural experiments, and observing that what is missing is "not data but permission and instruction — a mandate to link pension-administrator records to school employment records around the date of a rule change", is a genuine contribution, converts "more research is needed" into a costed request, and is exactly the kind of finding the lead author asked for. The one addition it needs: Ontario 2020-23 is a sixth discontinuity, and the one whose answer we can partly guess.
