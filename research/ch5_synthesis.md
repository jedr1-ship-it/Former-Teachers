## ITEM C6 (BLOCKING) — Chapter 5 evidence memo: design lessons for re-entry policy

### C6.0 The OECD anchors (fixes GAPS E rows 1–2 jointly with `fix_oecd_primary.md`)

Chapter 5 is not inventing a policy strand. Re-entry has been canonical OECD teacher policy for two decades:

- **OECD (2005), *Teachers Matter*** treats former teachers as "a potentially important source of recruits" (p. 68: UK returners were ~25% of the full-time inflow and >50% of the part-time inflow in 2000/01), recommends "maintaining contact with former teachers to keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities, and targeted retraining schemes" (pp. 88–89), part-time opportunities and leave schemes to raise return rates (p. 88), induction programmes extended to returners, not only beginners (p. 121), and "flexible re-entry pathways to the profession" (p. 165). Pinpoints verified in `fix_oecd_primary.md` from the official PDF.
- **OECD (2024), *Education Policy Outlook 2024*** lists "Supporting re-entry of teachers who left the profession" as one of four supply levers (Figure 2.5, p. 48; dedicated section pp. 50–51; strategic considerations p. 68), and prints the sentence Chapter 5 should quote for the accountability lesson (p. 51): "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically."
- **OECD (2025), *Education at a Glance 2025*, Indicator D8** (Chapter D8, pp. 484–507) supplies the diagnostic split Chapter 5 needs: across 19 OECD systems, 6.5% of fully qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23 (Table D8.4), and **on average 51% of leavers resigned rather than retired** — over 80% resigned in Denmark, England (UK) and Estonia, while retirements exceed 50% of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland, Israel (and Argentina, primary) (pp. 484, 493–495, Table D8.4). EAG 2025 also notes (p. 495) that "in most countries there are pathways for teachers to return to the profession if they wish" — the OECD's own hook for this paper. (Exact table pinpoints per `fix_oecd_primary.md`; coordinate final page numbers with that memo.)

### C6.1 Twelve design lessons, each with supporting cases and citations

**Lesson 1 — Measure the reserve before legislating for it.** Register countries can see a reserve several times the shortage (Netherlands: ~62,000 vs 13,600 FTE ≈ 4.5×, Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024, ESB; DUO, 2026; England: 260,000 out-of-service + 106,000 never-served, Worth, 2023, citing DfE 2018; Michigan: 61,252 certified not teaching, Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, pp. 4–5; Australia: ~19% of 553,300 registrants not in schools, AITSL, 2025). Systems without registers (Germany, Austria, France) default their debate to the only visible pool — retirees (`lit_reserve` Claim 7–8). EPO 2024's data-gap sentence (p. 51) makes this an OECD-endorsed recommendation: build the returner data infrastructure first.

**Lesson 2 — Diagnose the exit margin; re-entry policy inherits the exit's cause.** EAG 2025's resignations-vs-retirements split (D8, above) shows the leaver pool is a different animal by system: resignation-heavy systems (Denmark, England, Estonia) should target working-age resigners; retirement-heavy systems (France, Greece, Ireland, Israel) face an actuarial, not motivational, margin. Layer on the conditions-vs-pay heterogeneity: Michigan's inactive certificate-holders lead with salary (33% top-three; Lindsay et al., 2021, Figure 1), while Dutch (Somers et al., 2024), Australian (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025 — leavers' job satisfaction +0.13 SD at one year, +0.19 SD at three years, weekly wages −6.2% at one year, per `fix_hilda_fleck.md`) and English leavers (~10% wage cut accepted for conditions; Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015) reveal conditions-first exits. Identical re-entry incentives will not travel.

**Lesson 3 — At equal money, preventing an exit beats reversing one.** Portugal's Decree-Law 51/2024 ran both margins simultaneously: the Article 5 returner route (pension + first-step-indexed pay, pro-rated) drew 79–80 applicants and 63 accepted returnees against a 200-place target, while the Article 6 EUR 750/month stay-on supplement was used by ~2,200 teachers in 2025/26 — a 63-vs-2,200 asymmetry (`cases_retiree_return` §3; `fix_austria_portugal` D1). Consistent causal analogues: pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4–10 pp (Fleck et al., 2025), and Fitzpatrick (2015) shows teachers value current cash roughly five-to-one over deferred pension wealth. Chapter 5's ordering: retention windows first, re-entry as the second-best channel.

**Lesson 4 — Remove the pension penalty and the eligibility screens before adding bonuses.** North Carolina's 1999–2009 return-to-work window (full salary plus pension) produced the only large-scale revealed-preference success: ~3,500 returners, just over 35% of the retirement-eligible returning by 2009, disproportionately hired by high-need schools — and returns collapsed when the window closed (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). The design spectrum in current US law runs from Georgia's stacked screens (30 years' service + one-year wait + subject/region caps → ~350 full-time rehires per year, 635 cumulative FY2023–FY2025, vs ~6,000 vacant or not-fully-certified posts; Georgia DOAA, 2025, Report 24-20) to New Mexico's no-caps regime (90-day layout, no salary/hours limits → 154 users in year one, 2,792 working retirees across all tiers; NMERB, 2023) and Michigan's simplification (PA 184 of 2022; PA 147 of 2023). The Georgia audit is direct official evidence that stacking screens strangles take-up.

**Lesson 5 — Interest is cheap; conversion is the constraint. Fund conversion and synchronise it with the hiring cycle.** England's Return to Teaching pilot: 5,729 registrations → 354 supported → 27 in permanent EBacc posts; GBP 37,400 per additional returner; "unlikely to represent good value for money" (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 56–58, 91). Its successor (REP) tied funded places to actual vacancies (NCTL, 2017) — the correct fix. The standing RTTA adviser service is far cheaper per recorded returner (~GBP 5,400 implied, 2019–21; UK Parliament, 2022) but with unknown additionality. Design corollary: measure additionality or the cost-per-returner number is meaningless.

**Lesson 6 — Use the employer's own records for direct, personalised outreach, bundled with one-stop re-licensure.** NSW Teachers Re-Engage contacted >1,500 recent leavers from departmental records, packaged accreditation, approval-to-teach and background checks into one contact, and placed 145 returners across >600 schools, ~60% from retirement (NSW Government, 2024a; NSW DoE Annual Report 2023–24, p. 14). Vienna wrote to 300 retirees; Salzburg contacted all retirees to age 67 (APA/ORF, 2023). This operationalises Teachers Matter's "maintaining contact with former teachers" (p. 88) at near-zero marginal cost — the only published full funnel anywhere.

**Lesson 7 — Sell part-time, flexible and redesigned roles, not full classroom return on unchanged conditions.** NFER's "career breakers" — the most return-committed segment — returned at double the rate of other types and want part-time work that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). Portugal's returner route pro-rates pay by hours (Art. 5); union leaders attribute low uptake to "accumulated fatigue" and an uninviting school environment, not pay (SÁBADO, 2024). EPO 2024 (p. 51) reports Australia exploring returns into "alternative roles beyond traditional classroom teaching, such as mentoring". Teachers Matter said it in 2005 (p. 88: part-time opportunities raise return rates).

**Lesson 8 — Make re-licensure friction removal a standing rule, not a crisis waiver.** New Zealand's Teacher Education Refresh waiver for returners with a relief-teaching offer was repeatedly extended and then effectively mainstreamed into the Teaching Council's registration policy, alongside the statutory 20-half-day relief allowance and government-paid certification fees to 2028 (`cases_england_nz` §3; Ministry of Education, 2026a; Teaching Council, 2026). Virginia's ED-3 fast-tracked lapsed licences (2022); Ireland's career-break substitution suspension has been renewed annually since at least 2021/22 (TC/IN 0005/2025). The pattern to avoid: annual temporary waivers that returners cannot plan around.

**Lesson 9 — For retirees specifically, the best-evidenced roles are tutoring and mentoring — justified on instructional support and retiree wellbeing, not on mentee retention.** Number Rockets: 86 tutors recruited from local pools of retired and substitute teachers reproduced, at scale (994 first graders, 76 schools, four urban districts in four states), effects first obtained with graduate students (ES +0.34 on TEMA-3; Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). Experience Corps: reading gains ES 0.13–0.16 for students (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; WWC, 2010) and RCT-documented health benefits for the older volunteers themselves (Fried et al., 2004; Carlson et al., 2015) — a rare dual-benefit result that lets re-entry policy borrow a healthy-ageing rationale. But the one RCT of retirees as new-teacher mentors (Aurora) is a qualified null under WWC standards — no significant effects on reading, math or teacher retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; WWC, 2019, Study #88756) — so mentoring claims must stay modest. Teachers Matter's induction-for-returners recommendation (p. 121) completes the loop: returners themselves need induction.

**Lesson 10 — Re-employment caps are two-way policy dials; build in equity guardrails against crowding-out.** Ontario moved its OTPP cap 20→95 days (1990, shortage), 95→50 (2012, after the Globe and Mail documented CAD 108.3 million/year spent on retired supply teachers while new graduates went short), 50→95 (pandemic, 2020/21–2022/23), and back to 50 — with the OTF refusing a further extension in March 2024: "The Pension Plan is… not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; OTF/OCEOTA/Alphonso, 2024; `cases_other_legislation` §2). Chinese coverage of the Silver Age plan carries the same warning — localities using retirees to delay hiring new teachers (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025, secondary). Retiree re-entry is a valve, not a workforce strategy.

**Lesson 11 — Target incentives where friction and need are highest to cut deadweight.** China's Silver Age plan conditions the ¥20,000/year allowance on service in poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority and border counties (MOE and MOF, 2018; MOE et al., 2023); North Carolina's returners flowed disproportionately to high-need schools without a mandate (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024); Florida's incentives were conditioned on shortage subjects (Feng and Sass, 2018); Portugal narrowed the EUR 750 supplement to shortage zones in July 2026 after conceding the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026). Deadweight is what made England's pilot expensive (half the 27 might have returned anyway; Buchanan et al., 2018).

**Lesson 12 — Announce less, account more: the absence of follow-up data is itself a finding.** Across the corpus (GAPS §B2), the modal re-entry programme publishes a launch release and nothing after: NSW Teachers Re-Engage vanished from the 2024–25 Annual Report (NSW DoE, 2025); no uptake counts exist for Michigan PA 184/147, Virginia's critical-shortage rehires, Louisiana Acts 394/344, France's cumul emploi-retraite (teachers), NZ's TER waiver, England's post-2022 RTTA phase, or China's progress toward 120,000. The exceptions prove accountability is a design choice, not an afterthought: Georgia wrote the audit into the statute (DOAA Report 24-20), Austria's Rechnungshof produced the only audited national take-up figure in Europe (1,062 returners, 2018/19–2023/24; Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24–25), New Mexico's ERB reports tiered participation annually (NMERB, 2023), and Louisiana's Act 344 mandates an actuarial report before 2034. Chapter 5 recommendation: pair every re-entry instrument with a sunset, a mandated uptake count and an arms-length review — and quote EPO 2024 (p. 51) as the OECD statement of the gap.

### C6.2 Overarching evidence caveat (carry from Chapter 3)

No quasi-experimental study anywhere directly prices *re-entry* incentives; the causal base (Fleck et al., 2025; Sims, Lowes-Belk and Routledge, 2026) concerns retention and entry, and its elasticities cannot be silently borrowed for people who have already revealed a preference against current conditions (`lit_return_incentives` §C1 — keep that caveat verbatim). The lessons above therefore rest on: (i) audited/administrative take-up under different rule designs (NC, GA, NM, AT, PT, ON), (ii) one full independent programme evaluation (England RTT pilot), (iii) RCTs of retiree *roles* rather than re-entry *incentives* (Aurora, Experience Corps, Number Rockets), and (iv) OECD comparative indicators (EAG 2025 D8; TALIS; PISA).

## ITEM D7 — Experience Corps student RCT numbers and WWC dates: reconciled

Verified this session against (i) the ResearchGate abstract of the CSD report (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009, CSD Research Report 09-01), (ii) the Washington University press release (source.washu.edu, April 2009), and (iii) the WWC Quick Review PDF and WWC Study #88756 page.

**The numbers (all three renderings reconciled):**
- **883** students in grades 1–3 were enrolled/randomised in 23 schools in Boston, New York City and Port Arthur (TX) — this is the trial's headline N.
- The **final pretest (analytic) sample was 881: 430 treatment / 451 control**; the posttest sample was 822 (CSD 09-01 abstract; WashU press release: "The Experience Corps program tutored 430 of these students, and 451 were in the control group").
- The **"434 treatment / 454 control" split in `lit_retiree_mentoring` §A4 is wrong** (it sums to 888, matching neither 883 nor 881) — purge it.
- 174 volunteers served the treatment students (per `cases_mentoring`, from the report); effects: passage comprehension ES 0.13, teacher-rated grade-specific reading skills ES 0.16, no significant effects on receptive vocabulary or phonemic awareness; gains larger with ≥35 sessions.
- **Recommended draft formula:** "an RCT enrolling 883 grade 1–3 students in 23 schools (analytic sample 881: 430 treatment, 451 control)".

**The WWC dates (two different reviews were being conflated):**
- The **Experience Corps** review is a **WWC Quick Review dated February 2010** (PDF opened this session; header "February 2010"), rating: "The research described in this report is consistent with WWC evidence standards", strengths: "a well-implemented randomized controlled trial". There is no 2017 or 2019 WWC product on Experience Corps in the corpus.
- The **Aurora Retired Mentors** review (WWC Study #88756) was conducted in **August 2019** under the Review of Individual Studies Protocol 4.0 and Standards 4.0 (verified on the WWC study page this session): rating "Meets WWC standards without reservations"; domains reviewed were **reading achievement, mathematics achievement and teacher retention only — all "indeterminate effect", no statistically significant positive findings**. **Teacher evaluation ratings was NOT a WWC-reviewed domain** (it appears only in the REL report itself). `lit_retiree_mentoring` §A2 (August 2019) is correct; the "What Works Clearinghouse (2017)" bibliography entry and in-text "WWC, 2017" in `cases_mentoring` are **misdated — change to WWC (2019)**; `cases_mentoring`'s note that evaluation ratings was not among the WWC domains is confirmed.

**Supersedes:** `lit_retiree_mentoring` §A4 "883 students (434 treatment, 454 control)" → "883 randomised; analytic sample 881 (430 T / 451 C)"; `cases_mentoring` §2 and bibliography "WWC (2017)" → "WWC (2019), reviewed August 2019, Standards 4.0"; any text dating the Experience Corps quick review to anything other than February 2010. GAPS.md D7 — **CLOSED**.
