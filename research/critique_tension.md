# critique_tension.md — An adversarial reading of `arg_tension.md`

*Prepared 10 August 2026. Every counter-claim below is traceable to the same verified corpus the analyst used; where I say the evidence does not support something, I name the file and the sentence that governs. Nothing here comes from memory or the web. The fix files, `ch5_synthesis.md` and `TEAM_INPUT.md` govern conflicts.*

*The memo under review is the best-argued document in the folder. It is also, in several places, doing precisely what the lead author's diagnosis forbids — contrasting numbers and calling the contrast a mechanism — while sounding rigorous enough that nobody will check. That is why it needs a hostile reading before it becomes the paper's spine. Four charges follow, then a verdict on the six candidate propositions.*

---

## PART 1 — CLAIMS THAT OUTRUN THEIR EVIDENCE

### 1.1 The English cost estimate is presented as the price of an activity when NFER presented it as the price of four fixable mistakes

The memo's opening claim: **"This is the only such estimate in the corpus, and the paper should treat it as the price of the activity, not as one country's misfortune."** And, as the proposition heading: **"the funnel shape that produced that result is a general property, not an English accident."**

What the evidence actually supports is narrower and points the other way. NFER's own headline conclusion attributes the failure to four named, contingent causes: the pilot was unlikely to represent good value for money, **"driven by timing (support delivered after the hiring cycle), recruitment challenges, suitability of some participants, and lack of practical teaching opportunities in some schools"** (`cases_england_nz` §1.5, quoting Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 91). The same source records that the successor programme, REP, **"had to tie funded places to actual vacancies, directly addressing the 2015–16 pilot's timing/jobs mismatch"** (§1.6; NCTL, 2017). A cost figure whose evaluator identifies the design fault and whose successor implements the fix is not the price of the activity; it is the price of one badly sequenced pilot, and the corpus records no evaluation of the corrected version. `ch5_synthesis` Lesson 5 states this in the same terms the memo suppresses: interest is cheap, conversion is the constraint, and REP is **"the correct fix"**.

The generalisation claim is also unsupported on its own terms, because the corpus contains a second full funnel that behaves differently. NSW contacted more than 1,500 former teachers, drew more than 260 expressions of interest and placed 145 (`cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.3). That is a 56% conversion from interest to placement. England converted 5,729 registrations into 27 permanent EBacc posts — 0.5%. Two funnels, two orders of magnitude apart. "A general property" cannot be asserted from a sample in which the only other observation contradicts it by a factor of a hundred.

**Aggravating detail.** The memo describes the funnel as one that **"sheds an order of magnitude at every stage"**, then three paragraphs later concedes that **"England had 5,729 registrants and could support only 354"**. Those are different phenomena. The 5,729→354 step is capacity rationing by a grant-funded programme, not attrition of interest. Treating a budget constraint as evidence of a behavioural funnel is the single clearest instance in the memo of a number being narrated rather than explained.

### 1.2 The deadweight arithmetic is mis-sourced, and the direction of the inference is muddled

The memo: **"The GBP 17,800 cost per supported returner employed becomes GBP 37,400 once one assumes, as NFER does, that half would have returned without the programme. That assumption is the whole ballgame, and it is not conservative: around 4% of non-supported registrants returned over the same window."**

The 4% is not evidence against the assumption; it is the assumption's origin. Eight per cent of supported participants reached a permanent EBacc post and about 4% of non-supported registrants returned (`cases_england_nz` §1.4), which is exactly where "half would have returned anyway" comes from. The evaluators' caveat — that the comparison **"overstates programme impact because supported returners were more certain of returning to begin with"** — pushes the true cost *above* £37,400, not below. The memo reaches the right conclusion by an argument that inverts its own evidence, and a reader who has the NFER report open will notice.

### 1.3 "Wherever a target was set, the yield fell short of it… with a consistency that rules out coincidence"

Two problems. First, the corpus contains **three** verifiable targets with verifiable outcomes: Portugal (200 places, 63 accepted), the Netherlands (budget for 500, ~200 cumulative), England (~650, 27 in permanent EBacc posts). China's 120,000 has **"no official tally of progress… located as of August 2026"** (`cases_retiree_return` §2.2), so it is neither hit nor missed. Georgia, New Mexico, Michigan, Virginia, Louisiana and North Carolina set no volume targets at all. "Rules out coincidence" is a statistical claim resting on n = 3.

Second, the proposition's own second clause — **"and the shortfall is largest where the offer asks the returner to change the most"** — is stated in the heading, promised as "the pattern in the shortfalls is informative", and then never demonstrated anywhere in the section. No ordering of the three shortfalls by "amount of change asked" is attempted, and none is available: the corpus has no measure of how much change each offer demanded. A heading that the section does not deliver is exactly the kind of claim a hostile reader tests first.

### 1.4 New Mexico is quoted with the wrong numerator

The memo: **"New Mexico's flagship no-caps route drew 154 users in its first year from a pool of roughly 48,000 potentially eligible educational retirees."**

The same source records that in the same fiscal year **2,792 ERB retirees were working** across four routes — 154 under the new 36-month programme, 390 under the sub-USD 15,000 option, 686 at ≤0.25 FTE and 1,716 under the older 12-month-layout programme (`cases_us_legislation` §3). The honest denominator/numerator pair is 2,792 of ~48,000, or roughly 6% of educational retirees working. Pairing the 154 with the 48,000 makes New Mexico look like a failure of willingness when the state's own data show re-employment already happening at eighteen times that scale under pre-existing rules, with the new route most plausibly *reshuffling* participants between tiers. This also contradicts the memo's own Tension H, which correctly argues that re-entry **"is already large and mostly unprogrammed"** and that programmes cannot separate themselves from the flow. New Mexico is a Tension H case; the memo files it under Proposition 2 as a small-yield case.

### 1.5 Georgia is counted twice, on both sides of the ledger

Proposition 2 uses Georgia's 635 rehires and the auditors' "minimal" verdict as evidence that yields are small. Proposition 1 argues that yields depend on rules. Georgia is the corpus's cleanest demonstration of the second claim, not the first: the audit found the 30-year service rule, the one-year break and the high-need-subject methodology were the main barriers, and **45% of surveyed systems said statutory provisions blocked a hire they would otherwise have made** (`cases_us_legislation` §2). A state that screens out most of its eligible pool and then produces a small yield is evidence for barrier removal, not against re-entry. Using the same observation as a demerit for re-entry and a credit for deregulation is double-counting.

### 1.6 The Ontario "60 of 142,000" figure does not measure what the memo makes it measure

This is the load-bearing evidence for candidate proposition 5, and it will not bear the load. The memo: **"only about 60 of 142,000 retired plan members had expressed interest between September 2021 and January 2022"**, offered as proof that **"Displacement and yield are two readings of the same number."**

The context in the source is the *temporary raising of the cap from 50 to 95 days*, announced 10 January 2022 (`cases_other_legislation` §2.4). Retirees who wanted to work up to 50 days needed to express no interest in anything: the standing rule already allowed it. The 60 therefore measures appetite for the 45-day *increment*, not appetite for returning. That Ontario's retirees do return, at scale, is documented in the same section: the ten largest boards spent **CAD 108.3 million a year on retired supply teachers**, and one board gave retirees **2,861 supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates** (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). A memo cannot cite both facts and treat them as "the same number read twice". They are two different numbers measuring two different things, and the one that measures returning is large.

### 1.7 The retention-penalty arithmetic is inflated relative to the gap it rests on

The memo calls the returner retention finding **"the most under-used figure in our corpus"** and infers that **"the cost per teacher-year is meaningfully higher than the headline."** The underlying gap is 32% of the 2011 returner cohort out of service after four years (plus 2% retired) against 29% of the 2011 NQT cohort (`cases_england_nz` §1.5) — three percentage points on a single uncontrolled cohort comparison. Carried through, that adjusts a cost per teacher-year by something in the order of five to eight per cent: £37,400 becomes roughly £40,000. "Meaningfully higher" is doing rhetorical work the magnitude does not support, and the memo has just spent Proposition 1 insisting that magnitudes be respected. The separate DfE finding — odds of leaving after the first year 60% above average — is an odds ratio on an unstated base and cannot be converted into a teacher-year adjustment without the base rate, which the corpus does not carry.

### 1.8 Moyer's declining return rate is asserted as a trend the corpus explicitly flags as possibly artefactual

The memo: **"The propensity is not merely modest. It is declining."** `lit_reserve` caveat 5 says, in terms: **"Grissom-Reininger (NLSY79) vs Moyer (NLSY97) differ in window length as well as cohort. 'Ever returns' also depends on observation horizon."** A one-third-to-one-fifth fall between two cohorts observed over different windows is exactly what a shorter observation window produces mechanically. The memo elevates a flagged comparability problem into a trend and puts it in candidate proposition 3.

### 1.9 Smaller traceability faults, all fixable, all quotable against us

- **"Austria alone reported 4,778 unfilled teaching posts… (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24–25; OECD, 2025, *EAG 2025*, p. 491)."** The vacancy figures are EAG 2025 p. 491 (`fix_oecd_primary` A1.2). Rechnungshof pp. 24–25 is the returner count. The double citation attributes the vacancy figure to a source that does not contain it.
- **"Ontario's… average retiree age was 72."** The source says the average age of *retired plan members* is 72 (`cases_other_legislation` §2.4) — a demographic fact about a mature pension plan, true of every such plan, and silent about the age of recent retirees, who are the returner-relevant pool. It cannot support Tension C's "shortest remaining horizon" claim. Portugal's returner average of ~68 (`cases_retiree_return` §3.2) can; use that.
- **"England's… GBP 5,400 per recorded returner… The cheap instrument is cheap because it barely intervenes; its additionality is unknown and, on the physics numbers, unlikely to be high."** The physics numbers say 23 returners from **more than 111 physics leads given an adviser** (`cases_england_nz` §2.3) — a 21% conversion, against the pilot's 8% into permanent posts. On the memo's own conversion metric the adviser service outperforms the pilot by a factor of two and a half. "Unlikely to be high" is an inference the data contradict.
- **"366,000"** is used in Proposition 6 as a stock of "long-absent and weakly attached" *returners*. It is 260,000 out of service **plus 106,000 who have never been in service** (`lit_reserve` Claim 4). A third of that number has nothing to return to. See §3.4 below — this is not a citation slip, it is a category error with consequences for the paper's premise.

## PART 2 — EXPLANATIONS THAT ARE REALLY REDESCRIPTIONS

### 2.1 "Switching costs" names the observation instead of explaining it

Proposition 4's central mechanism: **"Staying requires no re-entry decision… Returning requires all of them. Re-entry policy pays to undo a transition that has already been consumed."**

Everything in that passage is true and none of it is an explanation. "Switching costs are higher for returners than for stayers" is a restatement of "fewer people return than stay", promoted to a mechanism by being given an economic name. It has no independent evidence in the corpus — no returner anywhere is recorded describing a switching cost — it generates no prediction the raw ratio did not already generate, and it cannot size anything: it is equally consistent with a ratio of 3 and a ratio of 35.

There is a real, testable mechanism available in the same corpus, and it is not switching costs but *decay of attachment* (see §3.5). Switching costs are a property of the individual; attachment is a property of the relationship between the individual and a specific employer, and the corpus can measure it: Portugal restricted Article 5 to those retired **five years or less**, NSW contacted resigners of the **previous five years**, North Carolina's returners had **54% return to the school they taught in before retirement**, and Resch finds that **keeping professional contact with the former school is what converts availability into return**. That set of facts explains the same pattern, predicts who returns, and tells a ministry what to do on Monday. Switching costs do none of the three.

### 2.2 "Instrument complexity" borrows Fitzpatrick to do work Proposition 3 forbids

Proposition 4: **"Fitzpatrick's five-to-one preference for current over deferred compensation predicts exactly this ordering: the more motivated population got the simpler instrument."** Proposition 3, two pages earlier: retention elasticities **"cannot stand in for"** re-entry evidence **"because the two populations are selected on opposite sides of the same decision."**

Fitzpatrick (2015) is a study of *serving Illinois teachers* offered the chance to buy pension wealth (`lit_return_incentives` A3). It is the purest possible instance of the cross-population borrowing the memo has just prohibited, and it is used twice — in Proposition 3 itself ("Now note what currency re-entry offers are actually denominated in") and again in Proposition 4. Either the prohibition holds, in which case Fitzpatrick must go, or it does not, in which case Proposition 3 collapses. A reviewer who spots this reads the whole memo as motivated.

### 2.3 The Ontario "known quantity" mechanism is invented on top of a newspaper's spending table

The memo: **"Note the price inversion: retirees cost nearly twice as much per day and were still preferred. The preference was not for cheapness but for a known quantity — which tells us displacement operates through principals' risk aversion in decentralised casual hiring, not through wage undercutting."**

Hammer and Alphonso (2010) is an investigation into board spending. `cases_other_legislation` §2.4 records what it contains: aggregate spend, the per-diem rates, the graduate-to-vacancy ratio, and one board's assignment split. It contains no evidence whatsoever about *why* principals called retirees. "Principals' risk aversion" is a hypothesis the memo has supplied and then reported as a finding — the exact move the lead author's diagnosis names ("cuando comparas, solo contrastas números sin pensar"), performed at a higher level of sophistication. At least two rival mechanisms fit the same table equally well (§3.1).

### 2.4 "Michigan's respondents may be naming the wrong instrument for the right reason"

This sentence is unfalsifiable, and it is deployed selectively. The memo discounts the one survey in the corpus that contradicts its conditions-first thesis on methodological grounds — 30% response, self-declared non-representative — while relying without demur on Brandenburg's **self-selected survey of 256 leavers**, AITSL's single stated-willingness item, and nine paywalled Austrian interviews **verified from the abstract only**. If the Michigan survey is too weak to carry "pay first", the Australian survey is too weak to carry "leadership and workload first" at 17.71% and 16.57%. Consistent scepticism would leave the conditions-versus-pay question genuinely open, which is a finding the paper can state — the memo's Tension B nearly gets there and then flinches.

### 2.5 "The reserve is a stock of qualifications, not a supply of willingness"

As a corrective to the 22% headline this is right and useful. As an explanation it is a relabelling, and the memo's own best anomaly refutes it in the next paragraph: in the Dutch RAP evaluation **"several regions received 'more side-entrant and returner applications than places' available"** (De Jong et al., 2022, p. 37). In the country with the best-measured reserve, willingness exceeded absorption. A proposition that says the reserve is "not a supply of willingness" cannot survive its author's own strongest counter-example being printed underneath it. The memo notices this — **"Both constraints… cannot bind in the same system at the same time"** — and then keeps Proposition 7 unamended.

## PART 3 — ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS THE ANALYST DID NOT CONSIDER

### 3.1 Ontario: availability and pay-grid mechanics, not principal psychology

Two explanations fit the Ontario data better than risk aversion, and neither is considered.

*Grid mechanics.* CAD 432.69 against CAD 227.36 is what a supply pay scale does when one substitute has thirty years of credited service and the other has none. Boards paying by grid have no per-hire discretion over the rate, so the "price inversion" is not a revealed preference for expensive labour; it is the arithmetic of seniority pay applied to a casual roster.

*Availability.* A pension-drawing retiree can accept a 7 a.m. call for a single day and decline the next; a new graduate seeking a career cannot build a livelihood on that and takes long-term assignments or leaves the casual list. On this reading retirees dominate the *short-notice* segment because they are the only people who can supply it — which is displacement of a kind, but produced by the structure of casual work rather than by hiring bias, and cured by different policy (guaranteed-hours contracts for new graduates, not caps on retirees).

The distinction is not academic. If the memo's mechanism is right, the remedy is hiring rules; if either alternative is right, caps on retirees reduce cover without helping graduates — which is precisely what the 2012 reduction to 50 days would have done, and the corpus has no evidence on whether new-graduate hiring improved after it.

### 3.2 Retirees are targeted because they are the only former teachers the state still has a contract with

Tension G's mechanism is visibility: **"Retirees are the only pool most systems can see."** The memo then demolishes it itself — **"England, the most resignation-heavy system in the OECD table, is precisely the one that built returner-targeted rather than retiree-targeted instruments… and produced the worst measured yield of all"** — and keeps the proposition anyway.

The better mechanism is *leverage*, not sight. A ministry can price a retiree's return because it still owes them money every month: abatement thresholds, service crediting, waiting periods, day caps are all adjustments to a live contractual relationship. It has no comparable instrument over a thirty-four-year-old former teacher now working in insurance; the only levers there are licensure friction and the job itself, one trivial and one expensive. This explains everything visibility explains and three things it cannot: why Germany, Austria and France — which can see their retirees perfectly well through pension payroll — nonetheless produce yields under 1%; why the instrument set is overwhelmingly *pension law* rather than outreach; and why those instruments are denominated in exactly the deferred, complex currency Fitzpatrick shows teachers discount five to one. The state reaches for pension rules not because it is short-sighted but because pension rules are all it holds. That is a mechanism-level explanation of the mismatch, and it survives the England counter-example that kills the visibility story.

### 3.3 North Carolina's sorting may be a map of vacancies, not a map of preferences

Tension H places the paper's constructive weight on destination: **"North Carolina's returners went disproportionately to high-need schools with no mandate."** Three alternatives are unconsidered, and each weakens the policy inference.

First, **54% returned to the school they had taught in before retirement** (`cases_us_legislation` §1). If returners were already disproportionately in high-need schools, the sorting is inherited, not chosen — the gradient reproduces the pre-retirement distribution of the returning cohort.

Second, high-need schools are where posts go unfilled. Returners land there because that is where the vacancies are. Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler explicitly could not test this: the paper has **"no vacancy data, no counterfactual, no achievement outcomes"**. The corpus therefore cannot distinguish "returners prefer high-need schools" from "high-need schools were the only ones hiring", and the difference decides whether sorting is a policy lever or an accounting identity.

Third, the gradient is modest: 30% in the top quartile of disadvantage against 21% in the bottom. That is a real but unspectacular tilt, and the memo's "flowed *toward* disadvantage" is stronger than 30-versus-21 warrants.

### 3.4 The reserve is two populations, and only one of them ever left

This is the alternative that most damages candidate proposition 3, and the memo does not raise it. The reserve is not simply the residue of exit. It is jointly produced by attrition **and by credentialing in excess of posts**:

- England's stock is 260,000 out of service **plus 106,000 with QTS who have never been in service** (`lit_reserve` Claim 4).
- Michigan: **~10% of the non-teaching could not obtain a full-time position — 53% among those who had never taught** (`lit_reserve` Claim 3).
- Ontario: **~12,774 newly certified teachers in 2008 chasing about 6,500 annual openings** (`cases_other_legislation` §2.4).
- The Dutch *herintreder* definition is explicitly **"a qualified teacher who interrupted, **or never started**, a career in primary education"** (`fix_netherlands_more_cases` §1.1, Art. 1), and England's pilot counted never-taught QTS holders as returners (`cases_england_nz` §1.2).

So a large and unmeasured share of every headline reserve figure consists of people who were credentialed and never hired. Reading the whole stock as "how badly systems keep the teachers they trained" is therefore wrong for that share — it measures how loosely training volume is tethered to vacancies, which is a different failure with a different remedy (initial-training places, not retention). It also matters for our own premise: people who never taught have no craft to bring back and are not former teachers in any sense `TEAM_INPUT` §3 recognises. Every reserve number in the paper needs splitting on this line, and doing so is a contribution nobody in the corpus has made.

### 3.5 Recency and attachment, the mechanism the memo splits in half and never assembles

The memo treats principals rehiring known retirees (Proposition 5, displacement) and employers successfully re-contacting their own leavers (Lesson 6, outreach) as two separate phenomena with opposite signs. They are one mechanism: **schools re-hire people they already know, and former teachers return to employers they already know.** Assembled, the corpus is unusually consistent for once:

| Observation | Source |
|---|---|
| 54% of NC returners went back to their own former school | `cases_us_legislation` §1 |
| Continuing contact with the former school is what converts availability into return | Resch, 2025 (`fix_austria_portugal` A4.3) |
| NSW contacted its own leavers of the previous five years from departmental records: 1,500 → 260 → 145, ~60% from retirement | `cases_nsw_bahamas` §1.3 |
| Vienna wrote to its own 300 retirees; Salzburg to all retirees to 67; Vorarlberg posts re-entry information with the retirement paperwork; Hesse wrote to ~7,000 | `cases_retiree_return` §1.2; `fix_netherlands_more_cases` §3 |
| Portugal restricted Article 5 to those retired **five years or less** | `cases_retiree_return` §3.2 |
| Ontario principals preferred retirees at twice the day rate; Japanese retirees are ~60% of adjuncts in the prefecture studied | `cases_other_legislation` §2.4; `fix_netherlands_more_cases` §2 |
| England broadcast a national marketing campaign to an anonymous stock including never-served QTS holders: 5,729 → 27 | `cases_england_nz` §§1.2–1.4 |

The England–NSW contrast is the cleanest comparison in the corpus and the memo never draws it: national advertising to strangers converted 0.5% of expressed interest into placements; employer outreach to identified recent leavers converted 56%, at near-zero marginal cost, using records the employer already held. That is a mechanism (the employer knows the teacher's quality, the teacher knows the school, and no party has to re-screen the other), it explains displacement and sorting with the same cause, and it yields a design rule with a date attached: contact leavers in the year they leave, not in the decade after. The memo's failure to assemble it is why it ends with no positive proposition at all — a defect for a paper whose central chapter is a compilation of programmes and whose fourth chapter must recommend something.

### 3.6 The proposed identification strategy is weaker than the memo claims

Proposition 3: **"A difference-in-differences on retiree re-employment rates across states and reform dates is feasible with existing administrative data… Every one of these systems administers pension re-employment and therefore counts it."**

Three objections, all from our own files. (i) *The data are not "existing" in any usable sense.* The corpus records "no published uptake counts" for Michigan PA 184/147, Virginia's critical-shortage rehires, and Louisiana Acts 394/344 (`cases_us_legislation` §§4–6); Georgia's counts exist because the statute ordered an audit. Administering something is not publishing it, and the memo's own Tension E rests on that distinction. (ii) *The outcome is contaminated by the treatment.* A rule that lets a retiree work without pension suspension converts previously suppressed or differently-classified work into counted re-employment; part of any measured increase is reclassification. (iii) *The reforms are not usefully staggered.* Georgia 2022, New Mexico 2022, Michigan 2022 and 2023, Virginia 2023, Louisiana 2024 are one clustered policy response to one common post-COVID shock, adopted by states selected on shortage severity — the identifying variation the design needs is largely the thing being explained.

The memo's own Tension A contains a stronger design and demotes it: DUO has tracked the Dutch reserve annually **from 2015**, and the *herintreders* subsidy ran 2017–2020 inside that window (`fix_netherlands_more_cases` §1.1). A register-linked panel there would estimate what share of a *measured* reserve an instrument can activate — the parameter the field actually lacks. And one study needs no new data at all: England already computes returner-versus-NQT retention, so England can publish cost per additional returner **teacher-year** tomorrow. Recommend the cheap identified studies first; a hostile reader will not forgive a research agenda whose flagship design cannot be executed.

## PART 4 — TRUE BUT TOO WEAK, TOO SAFE OR TOO OBVIOUS

### 4.1 "Rule changes cost nothing per head" — false as stated, and the memo knows it

Candidate 1 asserts that barrier-removal instruments **"cost nothing per head and reach the whole eligible population without a funnel."** Proposition 1 itself concedes the opposite: North Carolina's returners drew **"full salary *plus* pension, up to about 50% more total income than a regular full-time teacher"**, and the memo adds, correctly, that **"the distinction the paper needs is between cheap-per-head and cheap-per-vacancy-closed, and North Carolina is only the former."** Two further costs go unmentioned: Georgia's employers must **pay both employer and employee TRS contributions**, totalling **USD 11.1 million** over the audit window (`cases_us_legislation` §2); New Mexico's re-employment contributions are **non-refundable** (§3); and `cases_mentoring` §5 records that several systems levy employer surcharges on rehired retirees, which **"can erase the claimed savings"**.

*Sharper formulation the evidence supports:* rule changes do not cost nothing — they move the cost off the education budget and onto the pension and payroll accounts, where no one evaluates it. That is why they survive politically and why they are never assessed. Stated that way the proposition is both true and uncomfortable, which is what the lead author asked for.

### 4.2 "The reserve measures how badly systems keep the teachers they trained"

Elegant, quotable, and a hostile policymaker's reply is one word: *and?* It is a rhetorical inversion, not a finding; it converts our subject into an argument for someone else's chapter; and per §3.4 it is simply wrong for the never-served share of every figure quoted.

*Sharper:* the reserve is two populations with opposite policy needs — the never-served, who are a symptom of training volume untethered from vacancies and need hiring reform, and the once-served, who are the only re-entry population and the only one with craft to bring back. No system in the corpus reports the split. Publishing it is a concrete, cheap, OECD-shaped recommendation, and it makes the reserve statistic usable instead of merely deflatable.

### 4.3 "Because the yield is small, the displacement risk is small"

As written this is a logical constraint dressed as an empirical finding, and it is false in the form that matters. Yield is measured against the national workforce; displacement is felt in the casual and supply market, where the denominators are hundreds of times smaller. Both can be large simultaneously and in the corpus they are: retiree re-employment is **under 1% of Germany's workforce** and **~2% of North Carolina's at peak**, while retirees took **2,861 of one Ontario board's supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates** and constitute **roughly 60% of adjuncts** in the Japanese prefecture studied.

*Sharper:* re-entry is immaterial to the national shortage and can be decisive in the entry-level supply market, because those two claims are measured on different denominators — which is why the guardrail belongs where the effect is (publish new-graduate hiring rates for casual and supply pools), not where the headline is.

### 4.4 "Re-entry without a change in the job recruits people into the hazard rate that produced them"

The hazard argument is quantitatively thin (§1.7). The durability problem is real but the memo has the wrong variable. What kills cost per teacher-year is not a three-point retention gap; it is *horizon*. Portugal's returners averaged about 68; Austria's intakes peaked and fell within six years and the Court of Audit **"stresses the measure's temporary character"**; Japan's willingness is visibly eroding at ~35% of elementary age-based retirees; almost every instrument in the corpus targets retirees. A trainee costing £33,300–46,900 offers a career; a retiree returner offers a handful of years. That comparison, not the hazard rate, is where the English cost figure becomes genuinely damning — and it is available in the corpus today.

*Sharper:* the relevant unit is cost per additional teacher-**year**, no system reports it, and on the retiree margin the horizon is three to eight years — which means a retiree-based instrument must be priced as short-term cover and re-run continuously from a pool the previous run depleted.

### 4.5 "The instrument set is shaped by measurement capacity"

See §3.2. As written it is refuted by England inside the memo's own text. *Sharper:* policy prices what it still owes. The instruments are pension instruments because the pension is the last contract the state holds with a former teacher — which explains why the tools are deferred and complex, why they are aimed at the smallest and shortest-horizon segment of the leaver pool, and why moving the resignation margin has never been attempted at scale anywhere in our evidence.

### 4.6 The premise section overstates its own verdict

**"Our premise is a reasonable prior with no direct causal support, and in the one place it has been tested head-on it failed."**

The Aurora trial tested retired teachers as *mentors of new teachers*, with student achievement and mentee retention as outcomes. It is not a head-on test of whether returning teachers bring craft into their own classrooms, and its retention arm compares **11 programme leavers against 5 controls** (`lit_return_incentives` A6) — a comparison that could not detect an effect if one existed. WWC rated the domains **"indeterminate"**, which is a statement about evidential power, not a negative finding. Tension D's own conclusion is correct — label the craft claim a hypothesis with a named test — and then the summary section contradicts it with "it failed". Pick one. The defensible sentence is that the craft claim is untested, that the two positive trials concern scripted tutoring (where craft is deliberately designed out) and, in Experience Corps' case, **volunteers for whom being a former teacher is not an eligibility requirement** (`cases_mentoring` §4), and that nobody has ever compared a returner-staffed classroom with a novice-staffed one.

Similarly, *Teachers Matter*'s recommendation that induction be extended to returners (p. 121) is read as **"The canonical view is that returners need re-skilling, not that they arrive skilled."** Induction is not re-skilling, and a 2005 policy recommendation is not evidence about returner capability. Drop that inference.

## PART 5 — VERDICT: WHAT SURVIVES A HOSTILE, WELL-INFORMED POLICYMAKER

Assume the reader is a senior official who has read Buchanan et al. (2018), the Georgia audit and the OTPP rules, and who is looking for a reason to stop reading.

**Candidate 1 — barrier removal, not programmes. SURVIVES, WOUNDED.** The core is right and it is the memo's best work. It must lose two things: the claim that the funnel generalises (§1.1) and the claim that rules cost nothing per head (§4.1). It must gain NFER's own diagnosis, otherwise the first reader who opens p. 91 destroys the sentence. Rewritten: *funded programmes must buy conversion at the point of vacancy or they price themselves above initial training; rule changes reach everyone at no programme cost, but they bill the pension and payroll accounts, where nobody evaluates them.*

**Candidate 2 — at equal money, preventing an exit beats reversing one. COLLAPSES AS WRITTEN.** "At equal money" is unsupported: the corpus gives Article 6 as **EUR 750 a month** and Article 5 as pension plus compensation indexed to the first step of the scale pro-rated by hours, and **nowhere states the euro value of the latter** (`fix_austria_portugal` D1.1). The two instruments also address different pools (retired ≤5 years and willing, in shortage groups, versus everyone reaching pensionable age while serving), on different terms, with different administrative ceilings — Article 5 ran under an annual quota of 200 places while Article 6 was an open entitlement. A ratio between a 200-place scheme and an uncapped one is not a behavioural elasticity; it is, in part, the government's own prior about the two margins, expressed as budget. And the mechanism offered, switching costs, is a redescription (§2.1). The comparison is still worth reporting — it is the only within-system, within-decree, within-month comparison anywhere — but as an illustration that governments price the two margins very differently and get what they price, not as a verdict on which margin works.

**Candidate 3 — the reserve measures retention failure. COLLAPSES, and should be replaced.** The never-served share (§3.4) makes it factually wrong for a large part of every figure; the Dutch more-applicants-than-places anomaly (§2.5) contradicts it in the best-measured system; and Moyer's decline is asserted over the corpus's own comparability warning (§1.8). The replacement in §4.2 is stronger, is not available in any published source, and is directly actionable.

**Candidate 4 — returners re-enter the hazard rate that produced them. SURVIVES ONLY IF RE-BASED ON HORIZON.** Three percentage points will not carry it (§1.7). Age will (§4.4).

**Candidate 5 — small yield, small displacement. COLLAPSES.** Its load-bearing evidence measures interest in a 45-day increment, not interest in returning (§1.6), and the underlying logic fails because the two quantities have different denominators (§4.3). Reformulated as a denominator argument it becomes one of the paper's sharpest claims.

**Candidate 6 — measurement capacity shapes the instrument set. COLLAPSES.** The memo refutes it itself with England and keeps it anyway. The leverage account (§3.2) explains more, survives England, and delivers the same recommendation with a better reason.

**Missing — the strongest proposition available.** Return is relational and employer-local; it decays with time since exit and with distance from the last employer, and the instruments that work are the ones that exploit an existing relationship rather than address a market (§3.5). It is supported by five systems on four continents, it explains conversion, displacement and sorting with a single mechanism, it converts England's failure and NSW's success from a number-contrast into an argument, and it tells a ministry what to do and when. A paper built only on the memo's six propositions tells policymakers that everything they have tried is marginal and unmeasured. That is a true finding and an unpublishable one. This is the proposition that makes the rest of the memo's scepticism worth printing.
