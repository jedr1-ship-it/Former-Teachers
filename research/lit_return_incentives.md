## Identification of the Excel's "International: USA," row (2025 paper, quality 4)

The row with country field truncated to "International: USA," and the comment "Very solid literature review restricted to causal or quasi-experimental evidence. The model is quite simplistic but illustrative and the lessons are very country dependent, however.." is **almost certainly**:

> **Fleck, L., M. Somers, T. Stolp, W. Groot, F. van Merode and R. de Vries (2025)**, "The Effectiveness of Interventions to Increase Employment in Education and Healthcare: A Systematic Literature Review", *De Economist*, Vol. 173, pp. 1–45 (published online 2024; journal volume 2025), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10645-024-09440-8

Matching evidence: (i) it explicitly "exclude[s] qualitative and non-causal quantitative studies", retaining only RCTs and quasi-experimental designs (DiD, RD, matching); (ii) its country coverage is exactly a truncated list beginning with the USA — 72 of the included studies are US, 4 Norway, 2 Chile, 2 Korea, 1 each Denmark, France, Netherlands, Sweden, Uruguay — consistent with an Excel cell that began "International: USA, …" and was cut off; (iii) it is by the same Maastricht/ROA team (Somers, Fleck, Groot, van Merode) as the Dutch "hidden reserve" paper in the adjacent Excel row, so the team plausibly reviewed both together. **Caveat**: the published article is empirically organised (interventions grouped into financial incentives, working conditions, human capital); my reading of the article page found no formal stylised model, so the team's reference to a "simplistic but illustrative model" could not be independently confirmed from the version I accessed [UNVERIFIED — check the working-paper version or ask the team].

**Its lessons for re-entry policy:**
- Financial incentives dominate the evaluated literature and are broadly effective for recruitment/retention: conditional wage premia for hard-to-staff schools show consistent positive effects (the Norwegian Falch studies show a 5.7 percentage-point increase in entry rates from a ~10% wage premium, as reported in the review); one-time bonuses targeted at effective teachers work in 6 of 9 studies; pay-for-performance shows mixed results (group bonuses null/negative).
- Pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement reduced retirements by 4–10 percentage points — directly relevant to keeping the near-retirement reserve in post.
- Working-conditions evidence is thinner but directional: a curriculum reform that raised workload increased turnover by ~4.3%; reduced testing had little effect overall.
- Human-capital measures (induction, mentoring) show mixed/delayed effects (one study: +11.5 pp retention by year three).
- **Country dependence**: with 72 of ~86 studies from the US, the review's own geography is the strongest argument that "what works" is context-bound; almost nothing in the causal literature tests *re-entry* incentives specifically.

A complementary synthesis is Sims et al. (2026, *British Educational Research Journal*): quasi-experimental evaluations consistently find financial incentives effective for retention (citing Benhenda & Sims 2022; Bueno & Sass 2024; Feng & Sass 2018), while only one quasi-experimental study exists on professional development as a recruitment/retention lever (Allen & Sims 2017), and supportive school leadership emerges as the key non-pay correlate.

## A. Key claims with citations and statistics

### A1. The inactive reserve is large relative to the shortage
- **Netherlands**: The "hidden reserve" (people with a teaching qualification not working in education) is roughly **62,000 teachers — about four times the shortage**, which stood at **13,600 FTE at end-2023**; the authors call this conservative because registers only capture post-2000 graduates (Somers et al., 2024; DutchNews, 2024). Closing the shortage requires re-activating only a fraction of the reserve.
- **England**: about **250,000 former teachers of working age** are not teaching; roughly **14,500 return each year** (Worth and Sharp, 2018, NFER).
- **United States (Michigan)**: about **61,000 Michigan-certified teachers were not teaching in the state's public schools in 2017/18** (REL Midwest, 2021).

### A2. Stated preferences of the inactive reserve: workload/hours/autonomy vs pay — the answer differs by country
- **Netherlands**: inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs; the levers they say would bring them back are **lower work pressure, more control over working hours, more autonomy — with salary one lever among several, not the dominant one** (Somers et al., 2024).
- **United States (Michigan)**: the opposite ordering — **higher salary is the most frequently cited motivator to return (>35% of respondents put it in their top three)**, and nearly one-third cite wanting a higher salary among top reasons for not teaching; financial items (retirees keeping retirement benefits, better benefits, loan forgiveness) dominate the incentive list (REL Midwest, 2021). Note the 30% response rate / self-selection caveat recorded by the team.
- **Australia**: two-decade HILDA panel evidence shows **workload, stress and work-life balance — not salary — are the strongest drivers of exit**, and leavers' job satisfaction *rises* (≈0.13–0.19 SD within a few years) even though wages do not meaningfully improve (De Nobile/HILDA-based study, 2025, *Asia Pacific Journal of Education*). Implication: money alone will not reverse a decision that was not about money.
- **England (returners)**: NFER's returner typology finds "career breakers" are most committed to returning and are **particularly attracted by part-time work, which schools rarely advertise to new hires**; fewer than a third of pilot returners had taught in the previous decade, so confidence-building classroom experience is a second binding constraint (Worth and Sharp, 2018).
- **OECD context (TALIS 2024, published 2025)**: **19% of teachers report high stress**; administrative burden is the leading stress source in several systems (e.g. **69% in Australia** cite excessive administrative work); ~**20% of under-30 teachers across the OECD intend to leave within five years** (OECD, 2025). These are the same conditions the inactive reserve says kept them out.

### A3. Causal evidence on financial incentives (transferable, with caution, to re-entry)
- **England**: early-career maths/physics retention payments of £2,000/yr after tax (~8% of gross salary) made eligible teachers **23% less likely to leave state-funded teaching** in eligible years (Sims and Benhenda, 2022, CEPEO WP 22-04, UCL).
- **United States (North Carolina)**: an annual **$1,800 bonus** for certified maths/science/special-education teachers in high-poverty or low-performing secondary schools **reduced target-group turnover by ~17%**, with the strongest response among experienced teachers — but implementation was plagued by teachers not knowing they qualified (Clotfelter et al., 2008, *Journal of Public Economics*).
- **United States (Florida)**: the Critical Teacher Shortage Program's **loan forgiveness cut attrition of middle/high-school maths and science teachers by 10.4% and 8.9%** respectively (12.3% for special education when fully funded; no significant effect when funding was cut), and a **one-time bonus reduced exit by up to ~32% in the short run** (Feng and Sass, 2018, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*).
- **Norway (via Fleck et al., 2025)**: a ~10% wage premium in shortage regions raised **entry rates by 5.7 percentage points** (Falch studies as summarised in the review).
- **Design warning on deferred compensation**: Illinois teachers offered the chance to buy extra pension benefits were **willing to pay only ~20 cents per dollar of present-value pension wealth** — they value current wages roughly five-to-one over pension wealth — casting doubt on back-loaded compensation as a tool to attract or re-attract staff (Fitzpatrick, 2015, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*).
- **Transferability caveat**: virtually all of this evidence concerns *retention of incumbents* or *entry of new teachers*; no quasi-experimental study directly prices *re-entry* of former teachers. The elasticities above are the best available upper bound, since leavers have already revealed a preference against current conditions.

### A4. Barriers: licensure friction, pension penalties, inflexible roles
- **Pension rules as a re-entry tax (US)**: North Carolina's 1999–2009 return-to-work policy — full salary plus pension, i.e. up to ~50% more income than a typical full-time teacher — generated substantial take-up and **high-need schools disproportionately hired the returning retirees**; before and after the window, retirees were capped at part-time work at half salary (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024, *Education Policy Analysis Archives*). This is the strongest US evidence that relaxing pension penalties re-activates retirees where they are most needed.
- **Georgia (restrictiveness kills take-up)**: HB 385 requires 30 years of service, a one-year wait, and limits jobs by both subject and region — the most restrictive combination among US states — yielding only **~350 full-time rehired retirees per year, under 1% of the workforce**, against a shortage of ~5,300 teachers; districts report the one-year wait pushes retirees to private schools or out of the labour force (Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts, 2025; Capitol Beat, 2025).
- **Canada (Ontario)**: retired teachers may work only up to **50 days/year** without pension suspension — a cap raised to 95 days during shortages (1990; temporarily in the pandemic) and repeatedly moved as a shortage valve (Ontario College of Teachers; per Excel, cap history [UNVERIFIED beyond Excel]).
- **Licensure/re-accreditation friction**: England's dedicated advisory service (RTTA), New Zealand's rule letting returners teach **20 half-days of relief while renewing certification** without a full Teacher Education Refresh, and NSW's streamlined re-accreditation within Teachers Re-Engage all treat licensure friction as a first-order barrier; NSW packaged re-accreditation help, approval-to-teach and background checks into one contact (NSW Government, 2024; NZ Ministry of Education Education Workforce pages [both per official pages listed in Excel; NSW figures verified below]).
- **Inflexible roles**: NFER identifies the scarcity of advertised part-time posts as a core barrier for the largest returner group (Worth and Sharp, 2018); the England pilot evaluation lists **limited part-time availability and mistimed recruitment relative to the hiring cycle** among the three main barriers to placement (NFER/DfE, 2018).

### A5. The NFER value-for-money critique of England's Return to Teaching pilot — and design lessons
- Funnel: **5,729 registered interest; 354 returners actually supported (Jan–Sep 2016); only 27 (8% of those supported) were in permanent EBacc teaching posts in state secondary schools by November 2016**; estimated **cost per additional returner: £37,400** — leading NFER to conclude the pilot was "unlikely to represent good value for money" (NFER, 2018, *Evaluation of the Return to Teaching pilot programme*, for DfE).
- Three stated barriers: timing (few posts available when returners were ready), unmet continuing support needs, and limited part-time work.
- **Design lessons**: (i) interest is cheap, conversion is the constraint — fund the conversion stage (classroom refreshers, school placements), not just marketing; (ii) synchronise support with the school hiring cycle; (iii) build part-time/flexible vacancies into the offer, since the most committed returner segment wants them; (iv) measure additionality — many of the 27 might have returned anyway, which is what drives the £37,400 figure.
- Contrast: NSW's Teachers Re-Engage used the employer's own records to contact leavers directly and offered an "easy route" (casual/temporary placements): **>1,500 contacted, >260 interested, 145 placed across >600 schools, ~60% returning from retirement** (NSW Government media release, 2024; EducationHQ, 2024) — a far cheaper funnel design, though with no published cost or additionality analysis and no reporting after May 2024.

### A6. Retiree-specific motivators: purpose, reduced hours, mentoring/tutoring roles rather than full classroom return
- **Austria**: qualitative study (nine interviews, Upper Austria 2024–25) finds return decisions are rooted in teachers' biographies — health, gender, class, the "teacherly self" — not primarily pay; returnees are paid at beginner scale on separate contracts and take-up is on the order of dozens nationwide; the paper asks whether retirees are "an untapped reserve or emergency response" and points to pension/service-crediting rules as the fixable barrier (Wieser et al. [author names not confirmed — article page blocked; title verified], 2025, *European Journal of Teacher Education*, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02619768.2025.2531930). Context: **45% of >550 surveyed Austrian school leaders report an acute shortage**.
- **Portugal**: a €750/month pension top-up under Decree-Law 51/2024 attracted, per the team's Excel citing Público/Diário de Notícias, only **~80 applicants for ~200 openings, ~63 returning** [uptake figures per Excel; decree and €750 supplement independently verified via Diário da República and union summaries — uptake numbers UNVERIFIED in sources I could open]. Unions attributed low uptake to working conditions and accumulated fatigue, not pay — retirees were offered "the same conditions as active staff".
- **United States (tutoring rather than classroom)**: AARP Foundation Experience Corps — volunteers 50+, many retired educators, tutoring struggling early readers — is the rare model rigorously shown to benefit both sides: an RCT with **883 students in 23 schools found reading gains of ES 0.13 (passage comprehension) and 0.16 (teacher-rated reading skills)**, stronger with ≥35 sessions (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009); the **Baltimore Experience Corps Trial (N=702)** found cognitive/physical health benefits for the older volunteers themselves (Carlson et al./BECT). Modest stipends (up to ~$278/month) and structured, part-time, purposeful roles are the design signature.
- **United States (mentoring new teachers)**: the Aurora, Colorado *Retired Mentors for New Teachers* RCT (2013/14–2014/15) found **year-1 math achievement significantly higher (≈12% of a year's expected growth; ~one month of instruction)** for students of mentored teachers, reading positive but not significant (ES 0.07 year 2), **no significant effect on teacher evaluation ratings or two-year retention** (11 program vs 5 control leavers), at a local cost of ~$171 per student; more mentoring hours correlated (non-causally) with higher retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017, REL 2017-225).
- **China**: the Silver Age Teachers Action Plan (MOE since 2018; expanded 2023 by ten government bodies) targets **~120,000 retired teachers over three years** for rural, poverty-lifted, ethnic-minority and border counties; recruits (generally ≤65) keep full pensions plus an allowance (Excel: 20,000 yuan/year jointly funded centrally/locally — allowance existence confirmed, exact amount [UNVERIFIED in English-language sources opened]); since 2018 ~20,000 retirees recruited; 2025 round targets 7,000 (english.www.gov.cn, 2023; MOE, 2025).
- **Funding mechanics (US, anecdotal)**: because rehired retirees keep pension benefits, positions need not fund benefits, freeing modest savings some states channel into resident/apprentice stipends with retirees as mentors (Next Education Workforce-linked practice, per WestEd correspondence in Excel) — **no published studies; anecdotal reports of generational gaps and dated curricular knowledge** [UNVERIFIED — expert email, as flagged in Excel].

## C. Caveats and limitations

1. **No direct causal evidence on re-entry incentives.** The causal literature (Fleck et al., 2025; Sims et al., 2026) is about retention and entry; applying those elasticities to people who already left assumes symmetric responses that stated-preference evidence contradicts (Australia HILDA; Netherlands).
2. **US dominance of the causal base** (72 of ~86 studies in Fleck et al.) limits transfer to European statutory-civil-service systems, where pension and licensure institutions differ fundamentally.
3. **Stated vs revealed preferences.** Survey respondents may cheap-talk pay (Michigan) or conditions (Netherlands); the Michigan survey had ~30% response and self-selection (team's own note). Portugal's low uptake is the rare revealed-preference test — and it contradicts pay-first designs.
4. **Program evidence is largely uncontrolled.** NSW, China, Portugal, Austria and the Bahamas publish no counterfactuals; China's figures are government-reported; NSW stopped reporting after May 2024; Bahamas is announcement-stage (July 2026).
5. **Additionality drives cost-effectiveness.** The £37,400/returner NFER figure reflects deadweight (returners who would have come back anyway); China's targeting of high-friction rural posts is the design that minimises deadweight, as the Excel itself notes.
6. **Mentoring nulls matter.** The one RCT on retiree mentors (Aurora) shows no retention effect for mentees and mixed achievement effects — retiree mentoring should be justified on instructional support and retiree engagement, not on mentee retention claims.
7. **Dated evidence on exit motives**: the classic US hazard-model finding that newborns, not pay, drive female exit (Stinebrickner-type analysis; Excel "2002 Paper") rests on 1970s–80s cohorts; parental-leave changes since then limit its current applicability (team's own note — concur).

## D. Design principles emerging for Chapter 3 (evidence-weighted)

1. Remove the pension penalty before adding bonuses (NC RTW; Fitzpatrick's 20-cents-on-the-dollar result implies retirees discount deferred/complex money — pay current, simple, salient cash).
2. Minimise eligibility screens and waiting periods (Georgia audit vs New Mexico/Michigan).
3. Sell reduced/flexible hours and redesigned roles, not full classroom return (NFER; Portugal unions; Austria; Experience Corps).
4. Use employer records for direct, personalised outreach with a one-stop re-licensure route (NSW funnel).
5. Target incentives where friction is highest to cut deadweight (China's rural targeting; Florida's shortage-subject conditioning).
6. Time support to the hiring cycle and fund conversion, not registration (NFER VfM critique).
