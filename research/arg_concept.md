# What a former teacher is, and what they actually bring back

*Argument memo for the re-entry policy paper. Angle: interrogate the category "former teachers" and the claim that they matter for what they carry back into schools. Every claim below is traceable to the verified research files; arithmetic of my own is flagged as such. Nothing is drawn from memory or the web. Where the corrected renderings in `TEAM_INPUT.md`, the `fix_*.md` memos and `ch5_synthesis.md` supersede an older file, the corrected version is used.*

*Relation to the sibling memo `arg_mechanism.md`: that memo asks where the pathway breaks. This one asks who is on it and what they are carrying. Where the two touch — decay of returnability, the casual-work absorption channel, the seniority premium — this memo cites rather than re-argues.*

---

## 0. The question the draft never asked

The rejected draft answered "who are former teachers?" with a table of three groups — retirees, people out of the labour market, job switchers — sourced to five different studies, and then asserted, in a single sentence, that these people "hold full qualifications, classroom craft and… precisely the accumulated experience and subject knowledge that systems struggle to replace". Both moves are unexamined. The table classifies people by where they currently are, which is the one property that has no bearing on whether they will come back or what they would be worth if they did. And the assertion about craft is offered as a premise when it is in fact the paper's most contestable claim, resting on an evidence base that never once observes a returner teaching a class.

This memo does three things. It asks whether "former teachers" is a category that can carry analytical weight, or a container whose contents are set by each country's registers and pension law. It asks what precisely the asset is, and finds that three incompatible theories of it are in circulation — one of them advanced by the OECD's own flagship — of which only the thinnest is evidenced. And it asks what the reserve *is*, arguing that its highest-value use to a government is not as a source of labour but as the only instrument that prices the defects of the job, in a currency validated by a decision the respondents actually made.

The conclusion is uncomfortable for the paper's premise in one specific way, which is worth stating at the outset rather than burying: the craft argument is strongest for the population whose availability is weakest, and the availability argument is strongest for the population whose craft is thinnest. That bind is not a caveat. It is the finding, and it reorganises what the paper should recommend.

## 1. "Former teachers" is a regulatory category, not a behavioural population — and it contains people who never taught

**Claim.** The category earns its keep for exactly one purpose and fails at every other. It is the correct object for licensure, re-certification and pension re-employment law, because those rules operate on qualification status. It is the wrong object for any argument about motivation, convertibility or what a person brings back, because the measured category systematically includes people who have no teaching experience at all.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Registers count qualifications, not careers, and the qualification is what the state issued. England's headline pool is the clearest case: the Department for Education's 2018 stocks comprise 260,000 qualified teachers of working age who were out of service *and had previously been in service*, plus a further 106,000 holders of qualified teacher status **who have never been in service** (Worth, 2023, citing DfE). Twenty-nine per cent of the pool the paper would otherwise describe as "former teachers" has never taught (own arithmetic, 106/366). The Netherlands wrote the same conflation into law: Article 1 of the *Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders primair onderwijs* defines a *herintreder* as a teacher "who has interrupted, **or has not begun**, a career in primary education" (Staatscourant 2017, No. 62002). A country's flagship returner subsidy was therefore payable for appointing someone who had never returned to anything.

Michigan, which links certificates to employment records, lets us see how large this contamination is inside a single register. Of 61,252 certified teachers not teaching in 2017/18, **70% had not taught in a Michigan public school at any point across the previous five years**, and among survey respondents who had *never* taught, 53% said they had been unable to obtain a full-time position (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, p. 4-5 and Figure 1). A substantial share of the "reserve" is not a population of leavers but a population of unsuccessful entrants — people the system trained and then declined to hire. Meanwhile 44-49% of the non-teaching group held expired certificates against 6% of active teachers (ibid., Table 1), so the qualification that defines the category has, for nearly half of it, already lapsed.

Even where the register is clean, the category's *composition* is set by national institutions rather than by anything about the people in it. Across 19 OECD systems, an average of 51% of departing fully qualified teachers resigned rather than retired, but over 80% resigned in Denmark, England (UK) and Estonia, while retirements exceed half of departures in France, the French Community of Belgium, Greece, Ireland and Israel (OECD, 2025, *Education at a Glance 2025*, Chapter D8, p. 494, Table D8.4). A "former teacher" in Ireland is, as a modal matter, a pensioner; in Estonia she is a working-age resigner. Nothing follows about individuals; everything follows about whether an instrument designed in one system addresses a population that exists in the other.

**What strains against this.** The category is not empty. Every member holds, or held, a completed initial teacher education — the one property no other recruitment pool possesses, and the property on which the entire regulatory apparatus this paper describes actually operates. Abatement rules, waiting periods, re-certification waivers and pension caps are written about qualification-holders, not about motivations, and Chapter 3's regulatory section is therefore correctly organised around the category even though Chapter 2's behavioural analysis cannot be. There is also a defensible reason for registers to count never-served qualifiers: from a pure supply standpoint they are convertible people. But that is a *supply* argument, and it is precisely the argument the team has ruled out as the paper's premise. Under the premise the team has chosen — that former teachers matter for what they bring back — the never-served are not merely a measurement nuisance; they are non-members, and every headline reserve figure that includes them overstates the population the paper is about.

**Policy implication.** The paper should say once, plainly, that "former teachers" is a regulatory category and use it as such in the chapter on rules, while arguing everywhere else in terms of the distinct populations set out in Section 2. Operationally, the recommendation is cheap and follows the OECD's own diagnosis of the data gap (OECD, 2024, p. 51): registers should separate qualified-and-served from qualified-and-never-served, and should carry last-taught date and certificate status, because those three fields convert an uninterpretable stock into a usable one. England, the Netherlands, Michigan and Australia already hold every one of them.

## 2. What actually divides the population is the state of the tie, crossed with the pension position

**Claim.** The dimension that predicts behaviour at every stage is not current labour-market status, reason for leaving in the survey sense, or time out taken alone. It is the **state of the occupational tie at exit** — whether the career was completed, suspended, rejected, or never formed — and this axis must be crossed with a second, institutionally determined one: **the pension position**, which decides at what price and under whose rules a return is even legal.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Four states, and they behave differently at every observable stage.

A *completed* career — the retiree — leaves a tie intact but the terms of engagement transformed. Return is possible and, in the corpus, common, but it is capped in hours and duration by design and by preference: Ontario permits 50 days a year (95 in shortage episodes) before pension suspension (OTPP, 2026; Hammer and Alphonso, 2010; Alphonso, 2024); the great majority of New Mexico's 2,792 working retirees sit in the low-hours, low-earnings tiers even after all salary and hour limits were removed (NMERB, 2023); in the Japanese prefecture studied in depth, only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools now seek full-time re-employment, while retirees make up roughly 60% of the part-time adjunct pool (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203). Portugal's Article 5 returners had an average age of about 68 (`cases_retiree_return` §3). This is a population that returns in fractions of a post.

A *suspended* career — the career breaker, the parental leaver — never severed the tie, and this is the segment that converts. NFER's returner typology found career breakers to be the smallest of five types at 11% of registrants, well behind idealists at 35%, yet they **returned at about double the rate of the other groups** and wanted part-time posts that schools rarely advertise (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 10-12, 89-92; Worth and Sharp, 2018). This is the modern descendant of Stinebrickner's (2002) life-event leavers, of whom about two-thirds left the labour force entirely rather than switching employer, with a newborn the dominant exit factor.

A *rejected* career is the hard case, and the corpus is unusually clear that it is hard. Australian panel evidence shows leavers' job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations above stayers' one year after exit and 0.19 higher after three, while weekly wages fall 6.2% at one year and 9.4% at three and hourly wages remain lower across the post-exit decade (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, pp. 9-11, Table 4, Figure 2). Dutch inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their present jobs **more favourably than active teachers rate theirs** (Somers et al., 2024). These people did not fall out of teaching; they bought their way out and consider the purchase sound.

A *never-formed* tie is Section 1's contamination, and it belongs in supply arithmetic, not in this paper's population.

The second axis is institutional and it cuts across the first. Austria's law excludes from prior-service crediting any period that already generates a pension, so a returning former civil-servant teacher draws a full pension plus the teacher **starting salary**, while a former contract teacher, whose service is credited, essentially recovers pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Question 19, p. 18). Two people with identical careers, identical craft and identical willingness face offers that differ by most of a salary scale, because of the employment status they held decades earlier. England's abatement applies only to final-salary Age or Premature benefits and not at all to career-average accruals, so the constraint is confined to a shrinking cohort and dissolves as career-average membership matures (Teachers' Pensions, n.d.). The pension position is not a detail of implementation; it is a second dimension of the category, and it is the one the education ministry does not control.

**What strains against this.** The four states are not clean partitions, and the corpus contains a case that blurs the most important boundary. Portugal's Article 6 supplement was taken by roughly 2,200 teachers who had reached retirement conditions and chose to stay (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 6; RTP, 2026): these are people whose careers were institutionally *completable* but whose tie was not yet completed, and union commentary attributes the weak uptake of the parallel returner route to "accumulated fatigue" and an uninviting school environment (SÁBADO, 2024) — that is, to rejection motives inside a retirement-margin population. Nor is the taxonomy directly observable: no register records why someone left. What registers do record is a serviceable proxy — resigned versus retired, plus last-taught date — which is why the EAG 2025 split (OECD, 2025, Table D8.4) is the most useful single diagnostic in the corpus.

**Policy implication.** Chapter 2 should be organised on this axis rather than on destination. The practical instruction to a ministry is that its first diagnostic question is not "how large is our reserve" but "what proportion of our exits are completions, suspensions and rejections", because the answer determines which instrument is even relevant: retirement-margin systems need pension-rule engineering, suspension-heavy systems need part-time posts and a re-certification on-ramp, and rejection-heavy systems need the job changed before any offer will read as credible. That ordering is a position, not a contingency: it says that instrument choice is determined by exit composition, and that exit composition is already published for 19 OECD systems.

## 3. The bind: craft and availability are inversely distributed across the category

**Claim.** Within the category, the two things the paper wants from former teachers — deep classroom craft and substantial available time — are negatively correlated by construction. The population that has the craft can offer a fraction of a post for a few years; the population that can offer a career has, in a significant set of systems, less experience than the average serving teacher.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Take the retirement margin first. Its craft is genuine and measurable: North Carolina's 2,694 documented returnees had **31 years of average experience** (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024), and Aurora's retired mentors averaged 22 years in the district's high-need schools (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). But its availability is structurally small, as Section 2 sets out — capped by pension law in Ontario, self-limited to low hours in New Mexico and Japan, and, in the audited Austrian case, amounting to 1,062 returners across six school years against a workforce of roughly 123,000 (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24-25), with intakes peaking in 2021/22-2022/23 and falling thereafter even though the shortage did not go away. Germany is the same shape at larger scale: 457,000 retired teachers in 2021, thirteen of sixteen Länder actively recruiting them, and just over 5,000 pensioners teaching nationwide — under 1% of a workforce of some 800,000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023).

Now the resignation margin, which is where career-length availability lives. Across the seven OECD countries with seniority data, an average of one in three resigning teachers had **fewer than five years' experience**, ranging from 16% in Denmark to 58% in Austria and 68% in Israel (OECD, 2025, p. 495). Two-thirds of resigners clearing a five-year bar is a real fact and the draft was right to cite it, but it is worth being precise about what it establishes: five years is the *eligibility threshold* Aurora set for its mentors, whose actual roster averaged twenty-two. A pool that clears "experienced" does not thereby clear "master". And in the two systems where early-career leavers dominate, the resignation reserve is composed principally of people who left before the craft the paper is invoking had been formed.

Layer on the decay documented in `arg_mechanism` §4 and the bind tightens: 70% of Michigan's non-teaching certified had not taught in five years, fewer than a third of England's supported returners had taught in the previous decade (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018), and eventual return rates fell from roughly one-third of leavers in the NLSY79 cohort to about one-fifth for those exiting between 2000 and 2019 (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022). The people with the most intact craft are the ones least recently in a classroom.

**What strains against this.** Two things. First, the correlation is not a law: England's returner inflow has historically skewed mid-career and female — *Teachers Matter* recorded returners as about 25% of the full-time inflow and more than half of the part-time inflow into UK maintained schools in 2000/01, with roughly 12% under 30 and 40% over 45 (OECD, 2005, p. 68) — which describes a substantial band of people with both accumulated experience and years of career left. Second, the retirement margin's small hours are not necessarily a defect: a person who will do two days a week for four years supplies more teaching than one who takes a permanent post and leaves after eighteen months, and English data give reason to worry about the latter (Section 6).

**Policy implication.** The bind explains why the honest re-entry proposition is a portfolio and not a programme. Systems should stop asking the retirement margin for classroom careers and the resignation margin for veteran craft, and should instead match role to population: structured, time-bounded, high-value roles for the craft-rich and time-poor, and genuine part-time career posts with a re-certification on-ramp for the time-rich and craft-thin. This is also the cleanest justification for the mentoring and tutoring strand — not that it works better in the abstract, but that it is the only role design that fits the availability profile of the people who have the asset.

## 4. Three rival theories of the asset are in circulation, and only the thinnest of them is evidenced

**Claim.** "What former teachers bring back" is not one proposition but three mutually inconsistent ones, held by different authorities, and the paper cannot advance without choosing. The team's premise and *Teachers Matter* say they bring back what they took out. *Education Policy Outlook 2024* says they bring back what they picked up elsewhere. The evidence supports neither directly, and supports a third, unglamorous account: what they bring is a valid licence, immediate deployability without consuming a training place, and institutional fluency.

**Mechanism and evidence.** The first theory — craft carried out and carried back — is the paper's premise and has a distinguished lineage: OECD (2005) treats former teachers as "a potentially important source of recruits" and builds its recommendations on maintaining contact with them (pp. 68, 88-89). Its evidence is examined in Section 5 and is weaker than its pedigree.

The second theory is the OECD's current one, and it points in the opposite direction. *Education Policy Outlook 2024* frames re-entry as aligning "with a broader vision of teaching as a dynamic profession", notes that "not all types of departures should be viewed as problematic", and holds that returners bring "diverse career experiences, which can enhance teaching quality and drive innovation" (OECD, 2024, p. 50), returning at p. 68 to "the new potential skills they bring back". This is a claim about value accumulated *outside* teaching. It is advanced as a proposition rather than as a finding, and the corpus contains no study — causal, quasi-experimental or descriptive — that tests whether outside-sector experience improves a returner's teaching. It also sits awkwardly with the destination evidence: more than half of US leavers remain in education broadly defined and fewer than a quarter take jobs outside it (Brummet et al., 2024), and in Australia 22.1% of leavers hold non-teaching jobs inside school education within a year of exit with a further 21.8% in wider education, health care and social assistance (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, Table 2). The modal leaver does not acquire experience in a different world; she acquires it in an adjacent room.

The third theory is the one the evidence actually carries, and it has three components that can each be named and checked. A **valid or restorable licence**, which is why re-certification friction is a first-order barrier rather than an administrative footnote and why 44-49% expired certificates in Michigan is a substantive finding rather than a data-quality note. **Immediate deployability without a training place** — a returner consumes no initial teacher education seat, which matters precisely when pipelines are the binding constraint, as in England's year to November 2023, when newly qualified entrants fell to 17,462, the lowest since 2010, against 43,522 leavers (Schools Week, 2024, reporting DfE data). And **institutional fluency**: Aurora's report identifies its mentors as "well versed in the district's expectations" and singles out their having no role in the mentee's evaluation as a trust advantage over in-building senior colleagues (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017, pp. 2-3). That combination — knowing the system from inside while standing outside its hierarchy — is something neither a new graduate nor a serving head of department can offer, and it is a mechanism rather than a sentiment.

**What strains against this.** The third theory is thin in the sense that matters to a policymaker: it justifies former teachers as a *convenient* supply, which is close to the shortage-remedy framing the team has rejected. If the paper takes only what is evidenced, it risks arriving at "they are cheap to deploy" — an accurate but deflating answer to "why do they merit their own analysis". There is also indirect support for the first theory that should not be dismissed: the tutoring meta-analysis finds effects largest for teacher and paraprofessional tutors and systematically smaller for volunteer and parent tutors, with the gap driven chiefly by reading programmes (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024, pooled effect 0.288 SD). That gradient is real evidence that pedagogical training carries value into a tutoring role. What it cannot do is locate retired educators on the gradient, since no meta-analysis isolates them as a tutor class.

**Policy implication.** The paper should adopt the third theory as its evidenced base, present the first as a plausible mechanism with one strong and one null test, and treat the second — the OECD's own — as an untested hypothesis worth naming as such. A policy paper that quietly reconciles two contradictory theories of its own subject is doing the thing the lead author objected to. Naming the contradiction, and saying which one the evidence can bear weight on, is the alternative.

## 5. The trial evidence points away from the craft argument: structure beats judgement, and dosage binds

**Claim.** The best-evidenced deployments of former teachers succeed in inverse proportion to how much they rely on the returner's accumulated judgement, and the effects that exist appear only above dosage thresholds that the available population is structurally unlikely to reach.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Set the three trials against each other on the dimension that matters — how much discretion the role gives the former teacher.

At the low-discretion end sits **Number Rockets**, and it is the corpus's strongest result. A scripted Tier-2 small-group mathematics intervention, roughly 40 minutes, three times a week for seventeen weeks, was replicated at scale with 994 at-risk grade 1 students in 76 schools across four urban districts, each in a different state of the REL Southwest region; the 86 tutors were recruited from local pools of **retired and substitute teachers** and received one day of initial training plus two two-hour follow-ups; the effect was +0.34 on the TEMA-3 (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012). What makes this a genuine transfer result is the comparison it was designed to make: the original efficacy trials used the researchers' graduate students (Fuchs et al., 2005), and the replication asked whether a realistic workforce could reproduce them. It could. But notice what was transferred. The protocol was scripted and the training was three days' equivalent in total; the retirees were not exercising twenty years of professional judgement, they were delivering a script reliably. The result establishes that former teachers are an excellent delivery workforce. It does not establish that their craft was the active ingredient — and the honest reading is that the intervention was designed so that it would not have to be.

In the middle sits **Experience Corps**, and here the paper must be careful, because the case is routinely mis-recruited to this argument. Volunteers are aged 50+ with a minimum of a high-school diploma and at least twenty hours of training; **being a former teacher is not an eligibility requirement**, and the share of volunteers who are retired educators is undocumented (`cases_mentoring` §4). The randomised trial enrolled 883 grade 1-3 students in 23 schools with an analytic sample of 881 (430 treatment, 451 control) and found effects of 0.13 on passage comprehension and 0.16 on teacher-rated grade-specific reading skills, with gains concentrated among students receiving at least 35 sessions (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; WWC, 2010). This is evidence about **older volunteers**, not about former teachers, and citing it as evidence for the craft argument is a category error. Its real contribution to this paper lies elsewhere: the Johns Hopkins trials showing physical, cognitive and — in a neuroimaging subsample of 111 — cortical and hippocampal volume benefits to the volunteers themselves (Fried et al., 2004; Carlson et al., 2015) make it the one case in the corpus where the older participant is a beneficiary rather than an input, which is a different and genuinely useful argument for retiree engagement.

At the high-discretion end sits **Aurora**, and it is a null. The design was as favourable to the craft hypothesis as any in the literature: recently retired master educators, screened by interview for a documented record of excellence, averaging twenty-two years in the district's high-need schools, paid USD 41 an hour, meeting mentees one-to-one weekly with caseloads of three to seven, explicitly excluded from evaluation. The year-1 mathematics effect of 0.064 was significant at .05 but did not survive the report's own Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment; reading effects were 0.014 and 0.066, never significant; teacher evaluation ratings showed no effect; and retention, if anything, went the wrong way, with eleven programme against five control teachers leaving the district (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017). The What Works Clearinghouse, reviewing in August 2019 under Standards 4.0, rated the study as meeting standards *without reservations* and characterised all three reviewed domains — reading, mathematics and teacher retention — as indeterminate, with no statistically significant positive findings (WWC, 2019, Study #88756). The strongest available test of craft transfer is a high-internal-validity null.

The pattern is therefore inverse to the premise: **structure high, effect high; structure low, effect absent.** A mechanism explains why. Delivering a well-designed protocol to a child requires classroom presence, pacing, behaviour management and reliability — capabilities a career of teaching produces abundantly. Transferring tacit expertise to another adult requires that the expertise be articulated, which is a different capability that practising a craft does not automatically confer, and which the induction literature suggests is hard to produce even in dedicated programmes: the largest US randomised trial of comprehensive induction found no achievement impacts in years one to three from one year of the programme and **no impacts on teacher retention after any of three follow-up years**, against a largely positive correlational literature (Glazerman et al., 2010; contrast Ingersoll and Strong, 2011, whose fifteen mostly non-experimental studies are broadly positive).

The best causal evidence that craft does transfer between teachers is Jackson and Bruegmann (2009): a one-standard-deviation improvement in the quality of a teacher's peers raises her students' test-score growth by 0.02 SD in reading and 0.04 SD in mathematics — about a third of the own-teacher effect — with spillovers strongest for less-experienced teachers, and historical peer quality accounting for roughly a fifth of the own-teacher effect. This is the paper's best footing, and it is worth reading closely, because it describes a mode of transfer that retiree-mentoring programmes do not reproduce. What it measures is colleagues working in the same building over years: ambient, continuous, embedded. An external mentor visiting for an hour a week is not that treatment. The corpus's own dosage findings say the same thing three independent times — Experience Corps effects concentrated at 35 or more sessions, Nickow et al. finding effects largest for programmes running at least three days a week and during school rather than after it, and Aurora's exploratory analysis associating each additional ten hours of mentoring with a doubling of the odds of a mentee staying in the district (non-causal, programme group only, p = .004).

And here the bind of Section 3 returns with teeth. Dosage is exactly what the craft-rich population cannot supply. The people with twenty years of practice are the people capped at 50 days in Ontario, sitting in the low-hours tiers in New Mexico, declining full-time re-employment in Japan, and returning in falling numbers in Austria. **The population that holds the asset is systematically unable to reach the dose at which the evidence says the asset registers.**

**What strains against this.** Number Rockets was somewhat *less* effective than the graduate-student original (`cases_mentoring` §3), which is at least consistent with a story in which tutor quality matters and retired and substitute teachers are not the top of the distribution — though the two trials differ in far more than tutor type, and the comparison cannot bear weight. Aurora is one district, two years, 77 teachers; it is underpowered for anything but a large effect, and its cost of about USD 171 per student per year with an exploratory return-on-investment calculation suggesting the annual cost could be repaid many times over means it cannot be called a failed idea, only an unproven one. And the case against unstructured roles is partly an artefact of what gets evaluated: scripted interventions are easy to trial and relational ones are not, so the corpus may be measuring evaluability rather than efficacy.

**Policy implication.** Three things follow, and they are unusually concrete. Governments should buy structure, not seniority: the return on a retired teacher is realised through a designed protocol with a specified dose, and a programme that recruits retirees for token hours into undefined roles should not expect measurable effects and, on this evidence, will not get them. Mentoring by returners should be justified on instructional support and on the retiree's own engagement — where the Experience Corps health evidence lets a second ministry's budget into the room — and never on mentee retention, which the only randomised test found no effect on. And the untested design with the best theoretical warrant is the one nobody has trialled: not the visiting mentor but the co-working returner, part-time and embedded alongside novices in the same building, which is the arrangement Jackson and Bruegmann actually measured.

## 6. Against a new graduate: two assets nobody prices, and two costs nobody counts

**Claim.** The cost comparison the corpus invites — England's £37,400 per additional returner against £33,300-46,900 per newly trained teacher employed after a year — is not a comparison of like objects, and both sides of it omit the terms that matter most. The returner's decisive advantages are that she consumes no training place and that she is the only supply channel documented to move experience toward disadvantaged schools voluntarily. Her decisive costs are an induction nobody provides and a next generation nobody counts.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Start with what the returner brings that a graduate cannot. She occupies no initial teacher education seat. That is invisible when training places are abundant and decisive when they are not, which is the current English condition: newly qualified entrants fell to 17,462 in the year to November 2023, the lowest since 2010, while 43,522 left (Schools Week, 2024). A pound spent on a returner and a pound spent on a trainee do not draw on the same production constraint, and pricing them against each other as though they did — which the evaluation's own comparison invites — flatters the trainee in exactly the systems that cannot produce one.

The second asset is distributional and it is the strongest single answer to "why former teachers, per se". North Carolina's returners flowed to schools with more economically disadvantaged and more Black students than average, **with no policy requiring it** (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Set that beside what novices do: across the OECD, novice teachers are 19% of the workforce overall but 22% in schools with high shares of disadvantaged students (OECD, 2019), so the ordinary operation of the labour market concentrates *inexperience* where difficulty is greatest. Returners, in the one large episode we can observe, ran the other way. If the paper wants a claim about former teachers that is about them rather than about headcount, this is it: they are the only channel in the corpus that has been observed moving experience toward the schools that have least of it, unprompted.

Now the costs. The seniority premium — a returner on a credited scale costs a school more than a novice for the same timetable, with Georgia's rehired retirees averaging USD 78,487 and Ontario's retired supply teachers paid CAD 432.69 a day against CAD 227.36 for new teachers — is argued fully in `arg_mechanism` §5 and is not re-argued here. Two other costs are conceptual and unpriced anywhere.

The first is induction. *Teachers Matter* recommended twenty years ago that "different forms of induction programmes could be of considerable assistance to returning teachers, and not just to beginning teachers" (OECD, 2005, p. 121), and no system in the corpus has been shown to do it. The implicit assumption is that craft substitutes for induction. The English linked data say it does not: of the 2011 returner cohort, 32% were out of service four years later plus 2% retired, against 29% of the 2011 newly qualified cohort, and departmental analysis put the odds of a returner leaving after their first year back at 60% higher than average (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 57). The one system that can measure whether returners survive finds that they survive slightly worse than beginners — which means the returner's craft is being spent without being supported, and that a cost comparison stated per hire is systematically the wrong denominator. Per expected year of service, the returner in a permanent post may well be the worse buy.

The second unpriced cost is the next generation, and it is the one place where re-entry policy can consume its own supply. In Ontario, one board's retirees took 2,861 supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates, the province's ten largest boards were spending CAD 108.3 million a year on retired supply teachers, and roughly 12,774 newly certified teachers were competing for about 6,500 annual openings (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010) — the investigation that led the re-employment cap to be *cut* from 95 days to 50 in 2012, and that the Ontario Teachers' Federation invoked in refusing a further extension in March 2024 on the ground that "the Pension Plan is… not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024; OCEOTA, 2024). Chinese coverage of the Silver Age plan reports the same failure mode in an institutional setting that shares nothing with Ontario — localities using retirees to postpone hiring new teachers (R.I.S.E. Magazine, 2025, secondary source). Two independent systems, one mechanism: a novice who does not get the supply days does not accumulate the experience that would have made her the veteran of 2045. A system that staffs today with yesterday's teachers can depress the stock of tomorrow's.

**What strains against this.** The crowding-out mechanism requires a slack market, and most of the systems in this paper have the opposite problem; where vacancies exceed candidates, the returner and the novice are not substitutes. The equity finding is a single episode in a single state under an unusually generous rule and has no counterfactual: the authors could not observe vacancies and explicitly could not establish where those posts would otherwise have been filled (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). And the retention comparison is partly a category error of its own — a retiree recruited as a defined-term buffer is *supposed* to leave, and Austria's tapering returns after the crisis peak are a valve working as intended (Rechnungshof, 2025), so the excess attrition is a genuine cost only for returners recruited as permanent replacements.

**Policy implication.** Extend induction to returners, as the OECD recommended in 2005, and stop pricing re-entry per hire. The reportable unit should be survival in post at one and three years, which every register country can already compute — England demonstrably can, since it produced the 60%-higher-odds finding. Where a re-employment rule is used as a shortage valve, it should carry an explicit new-graduate guardrail, because Ontario shows the dial moving in both directions and shows why.

## 7. Selection into return runs against shortage, and this is structural rather than a programme failure

**Claim.** The former teachers most likely to return are those with the weakest options outside teaching, which means the reserve is systematically thinnest in exactly the subjects where shortages are deepest. Returner programmes targeted at shortage subjects are therefore fishing in the emptiest part of the pool, and their poor conversion is a property of the population, not only of their design.

**Mechanism and evidence.** *Teachers Matter* states the mechanism in one line and the corpus has not used it: relative salaries influence the decision to return, and "returning rates are generally higher among those teaching subjects that provide the fewest opportunities for employment elsewhere" (OECD, 2005, p. 70). The same volume observes that science and high-credential leavers were "less likely to return to teaching once they had left" (pp. 176-177). The logic is straightforward and it is the mirror image of exit selection: the outside option that pulled a physics teacher out is the same outside option that keeps her out.

England ran the experiment twice and got the predicted answer both times. The 2015-16 pilot was restricted to EBacc subjects and placed 27 people in permanent state secondary posts out of 5,729 registrations (Buchanan et al., 2018). Its adviser-based successor, restricted to mathematics, physics and modern foreign languages, recorded across 2019/20 and 2020/21 twenty-five and twenty-four returners in mathematics, twenty-nine and twenty-seven in modern foreign languages, and **eight and fifteen in physics — twenty-three physics returners in two years from more than 111 physics leads given an adviser** (UK Parliament, 2022; Walker, 2022). Physics, the hardest shortage subject, is also the subject with the best alternative labour market and, accordingly, the thinnest returner yield. `arg_mechanism` §1 attributes England's collapse to vacancy match and timing; this is the complementary compositional explanation, and the two are additive rather than rival.

The other side of the same coin is where returners *are* abundant: Michigan's non-teaching certified stock is 57% certified for elementary grades against listed endorsement-area shortages led by career and technical education, special education and elementary (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021), and the corpus's clearest documented returner flows — NSW's 145 casual and temporary placements, Portugal's 63 — are into general staffing rather than shortage specialisms.

**What strains against this.** The selection story predicts that money should work better in shortage subjects precisely because it must overcome a larger outside option, and the retention literature is consistent with that: England's £2,000 early-career payments for mathematics and physics made eligible teachers 23% less likely to leave (Sims and Benhenda, 2022), Florida's loan forgiveness cut attrition of middle and high-school mathematics and science teachers by 10.4% and 8.9% (Feng and Sass, 2018), and a Norwegian wage premium of about 10% raised entry rates by 5.7 percentage points (Falch, as reported in Fleck et al., 2025). None of these prices re-entry, and the caveat carried from `lit_return_incentives` §C1 must stand: no quasi-experimental study anywhere prices a re-entry incentive, and elasticities estimated on people who have not yet left cannot be silently borrowed for people who have already revealed a preference against current conditions. It also remains possible that shortage-subject returners are scarce for the mundane reason that shortage-subject teachers are scarce in the stock to begin with, which the corpus cannot separate from the outside-option mechanism.

**Policy implication.** Systems should stop expecting the reserve to solve subject shortages and should target it where it is actually dense — general primary staffing, relief and structured support roles — while pursuing shortage subjects through the retention and entry margins where the causal evidence exists. Where a system does want shortage-subject returners, the offer must be sized against the outside option that is keeping them away, not against the teaching salary they left, which is the design error England's two attempts share.

## 8. The reserve is not a stock, a queue or a labour supply: it is the most precise instrument a system has for pricing the defects of the job

**Claim.** The reserve's value to a government is primarily diagnostic. What former teachers state as the price of return is the best available measurement of what is wrong with teaching as designed, because it is expressed as a price rather than a grievance, given by people with no stake in the answer, and validated against a decision they actually made and paid for.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Consider first what the reserve is not.

It is not a stock in any comparable sense, because the number is manufactured by the definition. The Netherlands produces two reserves in the same country in the same period: just over 62,000 on the ROA/CBS basis, counting only those who qualified between 2000 and 2021 (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024), and, on DUO's stricter basis of at least two years out with retirees and agency workers excluded, 34,000 in primary and 64,000 in secondary and vocational — equal to 22% of all qualified primary teachers but 43% of all qualified secondary and vocational teachers (DUO, 2026). Same country, same year, different denominators, and a factor-of-two difference in the headline share. Comparing reserve sizes across countries is comparing register designs.

Nor is it a queue. A queue implies people waiting for a door to open. Dutch inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs (Somers et al., 2024); Australian leavers' job satisfaction is 0.13 SD higher one year out and 0.19 SD higher at three, bought at 6.2% and 9.4% of weekly wages (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025); English leavers took roughly a 10% wage cut in exchange for shorter hours and higher satisfaction (Worth, Bamford and Durbin, 2015); and very few American leavers move to jobs paying more than teaching did (Scafidi, Sjoquist and Stinebrickner, 2006). These are people who paid to leave and consider it money well spent.

And it is not a labour supply of the size the arithmetic suggests, which the team has already ruled the paper must say plainly. Over half of Australia's non-deployed registered teachers say they would consider returning "under the right circumstances" while **5% are actively looking** (AITSL, 2025) — a ten-to-one ratio that the programme funnels then reproduce, and that `arg_mechanism` §8 reads as the gap between professional identity and labour supply.

What remains is the instrument, and it has three properties that no alternative measurement possesses. It is expressed as a **price**: "what would it take" is a more disciplined question than "what is wrong", and it forces respondents to rank rather than complain. It is given by people with **no stake in the employer**: a serving teacher answering a satisfaction survey is describing the job she must return to on Monday, whereas a leaver is describing a job she has already left, and the two response biases run in opposite directions. And it is **validated against a revealed choice**, because we know these people left and, in three systems, we know what they paid to do it.

The readings the instrument produces are coherent within systems and divergent across them in exactly the way exit behaviour diverges, which is the evidence that it is reading something real. Dutch inactive teachers name lower work pressure, more control over working hours and more autonomy ahead of salary — matching the Dutch exit pattern. Michigan's certificate-holders name salary first, with a third putting higher pay among their top three reasons for not teaching and a salary increase the most frequently selected return incentive (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021) — matching US exit evidence, where each USD 1,000 of cost-of-living-adjusted salary is associated with 0.34 percentage points lower turnover and predicted turnover falls by roughly 40% moving from USD 50,000 to USD 70,000 (Tan et al., 2026). Australian leavers name workload and work-life balance — matching HILDA. Two independent measurement modes, asked of different populations at different moments, agree within each system and disagree between them. That is what a working instrument looks like.

There is a further asymmetry worth exploiting rather than apologising for. Leavers are a selected group: their tolerance was lowest, so the price they name is an **upper bound** on what would have retained the median serving teacher. A serving-teacher satisfaction survey, answered by those who stayed, gives a **lower bound**. TALIS gives systems the lower bound — 19% of teachers reporting high stress, teachers stressed by student intimidation or by maintaining discipline twice as likely to wish to leave within five years, and 17% of teachers not near retirement intending to leave within five years (OECD, 2025, *Results from TALIS 2024*, pp. 232-234) — and no comparable instrument gives them the upper bound. Having both brackets a target that neither provides alone.

**What strains against this.** The instrument's best-known reading is its weakest sample: Michigan surveyed 59,433 and received 17,551 responses, a 30% rate, with an analytic sample of 9,842 that the report itself states "is not representative of Michigan certified teachers who do not teach", with unweighted percentages to be "interpreted with caution" (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021, Box 2). Stated preferences may be cheap talk, and the corpus has no costly-signal measurement anywhere (`arg_mechanism` §8). The reading is retrospective — it prices the job as it was when the respondent left, which for more than half of Australia's non-deployed registrants was three or more years earlier (AITSL, 2025). And the selection that gives the instrument its upper bound also limits it: if leavers are systematically the least tolerant, their price may be unmeetable rather than merely expensive.

**Policy implication.** This inverts the natural ordering of recommendations, and the inversion is the point. The first instrument a system should build is not a return programme but a standing leaver-and-reserve survey, fielded on items **directly comparable to the serving-teacher survey it already runs** — which is precisely the design that made the Dutch study informative, since it asked inactive teachers to rate their current jobs on the same dimensions active teachers rate theirs, and found the inactive scoring higher. The OECD has already stated the gap institutionally: "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically" (OECD, 2024, p. 51). Extending a TALIS-comparable module to leavers is a smaller ask than any programme in this paper and would do more.

And then the uncomfortable corollary the paper should be willing to state. If the reserve's principal value is diagnostic, the honest ordering of a government's spending puts retention redesign first, re-entry second, and the measurement before both. Portugal ran that comparison inadvertently within a single decree: 63 accepted returners under Article 5 against roughly 2,200 teachers who used the Article 6 supplement to postpone retirement (`fix_austria_portugal` §D1). Pension enhancements rewarding delayed retirement cut retirements by 4 to 10 percentage points (Fleck et al., 2025). At equal money, preventing an exit beats reversing one. Saying so does not demote this paper's subject; it explains what the subject is for.

## 9. What the evidence cannot settle, and what would settle it

Five questions bear directly on this memo's angle and cannot be answered from the corpus. Each has a test that costs less than the programmes the paper describes.

**Does a returner teach as well as the alternative hire?** Nothing in the corpus links a returner to a student outcome. The North Carolina authors state explicitly that they could not, having no vacancy data and no counterfactual (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). This is a hole directly beneath the paper's premise and must be named as one. It is also eminently fillable: England already links returner records to subsequent service, since that is how the 60%-higher-attrition finding was produced, and the same linkage supports value-added comparison of returner and newly qualified cohorts.

**Is the asset craft, or is it reliable delivery?** The Number Rockets and Aurora results are consistent with either story. The decisive test is cheap and nobody has run it: randomise retired teachers between a scripted protocol and a discretionary role with matched hours, and see which produces the effect. That single trial would settle the central question of this memo.

**Where do retired educators sit on the tutor-quality gradient?** The tutoring meta-analysis distinguishes teacher, paraprofessional, volunteer and parent tutors but not retired educators (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan, 2024), so the paper's inference that retirees sit near the teacher end rests on one trial. A subgroup analysis of existing trials would resolve it without new data collection.

**Does the outside experience the OECD credits returners with actually improve teaching?** No study in the corpus tests it, in either direction. Registers that record a returner's intervening sector — the Dutch and US linked-administrative data both do — could compare returners from within education against returners from outside it.

**Is re-entry institutionally harder than entry for someone who never taught?** Sixteen of twenty-eight systems have introduced structured pathways tailored to second-career teachers, while twelve provide no alternative routes at all (OECD, 2025, Box D8.1, p. 489) — and the OECD is explicit that this concerns second-career *entrants*, not returners. Against that, the corpus's re-entry routes are overwhelmingly temporary: New Zealand's refresh waiver was repeatedly extended before being mainstreamed, Ireland's career-break substitution suspension has been renewed annually since at least 2021/22, and most US instruments carry sunsets (`ch5_synthesis` Lesson 8). Systems have institutionalised the door in for people who never taught while leaving the door back as a renewable emergency measure for people who already hold the qualification. Whether the returner with a lapsed certificate in fact faces a heavier requirement than an alternative-certification entrant in the same jurisdiction is unresolved; every regulator holds both sets of requirements and could answer it in an afternoon.

## CANDIDATE PROPOSITIONS FOR THE PAPER

1. **"Former teachers" is a regulatory category, not a behavioural population, and the paper should use it as one** — it is the right object for licensure and pension rules, which operate on qualification status, but it is the wrong object for any argument about who returns or what they carry, because 106,000 of England's 366,000-strong pool have never taught, the Netherlands wrote never-having-started into the legal definition of a returner, and 70% of Michigan's certified non-teachers had not taught in five years.

2. **The category divides on the state of the occupational tie — completed, suspended, rejected or never formed — crossed with a pension position the education ministry does not control** — career breakers returned at double the rate of every other type in England, resignation-margin leavers in Australia are 0.13 to 0.19 SD happier having paid 6 to 9% of their wages to leave, and two Austrians with identical careers face offers differing by most of a salary scale according to the employment status they held decades earlier.

3. **Craft and availability are inversely distributed across the population, and this bind, not context-dependence, is why re-entry is a modest margin** — the retirement margin holds the 22-to-31-year careers and can offer 50 days a year, low-hours tiers and falling intakes, while in the systems where career-length availability lives, between a third and two-thirds of resigners left with under five years of experience.

4. **The evidence for what former teachers bring back is strongest exactly where their accumulated judgement matters least** — retired and substitute teachers delivering a scripted tutoring protocol reproduced an effect of +0.34 at scale, hand-picked master educators averaging twenty-two years produced a WWC-verified null in reading, mathematics and mentee retention, and the best causal evidence for craft transfer between teachers describes embedded co-working rather than the weekly mentoring visit that programmes actually buy.

5. **The reserve's highest-value use is diagnostic: what leavers demand as the price of return is the most precise measurement a system has of what is wrong with the job, because it is stated as a price by people with no stake in the answer and validated against a decision they paid for** — which makes a leaver survey comparable to the serving-teacher survey the cheapest instrument in this paper, and puts retention redesign ahead of re-entry in any honest ordering of spending.

6. *(reserve proposition, if a sixth is wanted)* **Selection into return runs against shortage** — returning is likeliest where outside options are worst, which is why England recorded twenty-three physics returners in two years from more than 111 advised physics leads, and why the reserve should be targeted where it is dense rather than where the vacancies are.
