# The mechanics of return: where the pathway actually breaks

*Argument memo for the re-entry policy paper. Angle: build a causal account of how a former teacher does or does not end up back in a classroom, and locate the break. All claims are traceable to the verified research files; own arithmetic is flagged as such. Nothing here is drawn from memory or the web.*

---

## 0. The question the draft never asked

Every programme in the corpus that measures itself publishes a shrinking sequence of numbers, and the paper has so far reported those sequences as if they were the same event happening in five places. They are not. England registered 5,729 people and put 27 into the posts it wanted to fill. New South Wales contacted more than 1,500 and placed 145. Portugal opened 200 places and 63 people took them. The Netherlands budgeted for 500 subsidised returners and cut the ceiling twice because the money was not being spent. Austria wrote to retirees in every Land and audited 1,062 returns over six years against a workforce of about 123,000. Read as a list, these look like one phenomenon — "interest exceeds conversion" — and the policy conclusion writes itself: fund conversion. Read as processes, they are four or five different failures occurring at different points, for reasons that are institutionally specific and that partly contradict one another.

Getting a former teacher back in front of a class requires, in sequence: that the person knows return is possible; that they are willing on the terms offered; that they are legally and administratively eligible (licence, checks, pension rules, waiting periods); that they apply to something specific; that a post exists which matches their subject, level, location and preferred hours at the moment they are ready; that an employer chooses them over the alternative candidate; and that they stay long enough for the appointment to be worth making. Seven conditions, each with its own institutional owner: the ministry owns awareness, the individual owns willingness, the regulator owns eligibility, the school owns the vacancy and the hiring decision, and the pension fund quietly owns several of the others. The corpus lets us say, for most of the measured systems, which of these conditions bound — and the answer differs by system in a way that explains far more than any comparison of conversion percentages.

A preliminary warning that the paper must carry: **the published funnels have incommensurable denominators.** England's base is self-selected registrations after a national advertising campaign; NSW's is a cold list drawn from the employer's own payroll records; Portugal's is an administrative quota; Georgia's is what employers did with a statute; the Netherlands' is grants claimed by school boards. Dividing outputs by these different objects and comparing the quotients — which the rejected draft did — produces numbers that cannot be interpreted. The comparison that *is* legitimate is qualitative and structural: which stage absorbed the loss, and what feature of the system put it there.

---

## 1. The collapse is not one phenomenon; the design chooses which stage bears the loss

**Claim.** The five measured funnels break at four different stages, and in each case the breaking stage is a predictable consequence of a design decision that was taken before anyone expressed interest.

**Mechanism and evidence.** In **England's 2015-16 pilot**, awareness and willingness were not the constraint: registrations of interest ran at 5,729 against a target of about 3,000, and the evaluators list "demand vastly exceeded expectations" among the positive findings (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 10-12). Nor was the middle of the funnel wasted: 426 were recruited, 412 started training, 354 were supported. The loss is concentrated at vacancy match and hiring: 27 of the 354 were in permanent EBacc posts in state secondary schools by November 2016, and 62 in any state-funded post at all (ibid., pp. 11-12). The evaluation names the reasons — support delivered after the hiring cycle, and a lack of practical teaching opportunities and part-time posts in some schools (ibid., p. 91). The successor Returners Engagement Programme required schools to tie funded places to actual vacancies (NCTL, 2017), which is an admission that the pilot had built supply into a market whose demand it had not checked.

In **New South Wales**, the loss is at the front. Of more than 1,500 recent leavers contacted from departmental records, more than 260 expressed interest — about one in six — but 145 of those were placed, a conversion from interest to post of roughly 56% (NSW Government, 2024a; NSW Department of Education, 2024a, p. 14). NSW did not solve a hard problem; it declined to pose one. Its returners went into casual and temporary work across more than 600 schools, a market with no fixed hiring calendar and effectively unlimited absorptive capacity, and the department bundled accreditation, approval to teach and the working-with-children check into a single contact.

In **Portugal**, the loss is at willingness, and it is unambiguous because the programme could not fill its own quota: against a government target of 200 returners, 79 to 80 retirees applied and 63 were accepted (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5; DN, 2024; Renascença, 2024b). Applications were fewer than places. No amount of conversion support fixes that.

In the **Netherlands**, the loss sits at the employer's door, and the evidence is a contradiction inside the same country. The *Regeling tegemoetkoming herintreders* offered school boards EUR 2,500 for each returner appointed for at least six months; the budget provided for 500, take-up was just over 100 in 2018 and nearly 200 cumulatively by late 2019, and the 2019 extension explicitly *lowered* the annual ceilings to 120 and 100 "because the subsidy was not exhausted" (Staatscourant 2017, No. 62002; Staatscourant 2019, No. 28510; Kamerstukken II 2019/20, 35 300 VIII, nr. 15). Yet the evaluation of the regional schemes that succeeded it reports several regions receiving more applications from side-entrants and returners than there were places, and finds tailored support to returners among the least-realised of all funded activities (De Jong et al., 2022, pp. 37, 60). Interest exceeded places while the money to create places went unclaimed. That is not a conversion problem; it is a statement that EUR 2,500 does not buy what schools actually lack.

In **Georgia**, uniquely, we can see the hiring decision from the employer's side, because the statute ordered an audit: 45% of surveyed school systems said the law's provisions had blocked a hire they would otherwise have made, and 635 retirees worked full-time under it across three fiscal years — about 350 in any year, under 1% of a 125,000 workforce, against roughly 6,000 vacant or not-fully-certified positions (Georgia DOAA, 2025). Here the binding stage is eligibility: thirty years of creditable service, a one-year wait, and caps by subject and region.

**What strains against this.** The stages are not cleanly separable in practice, and two of them interact: England's supported returners may have failed at the hiring stage partly because eligibility had been drawn so wide that many were poor candidates — the pilot admitted people who had qualified but never taught, and fewer than a third of those supported had taught in the previous decade (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). "Suitability of some participants" is one of the evaluation's own explanations for the poor value for money (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 91). A stage-attribution exercise is therefore an argument about the *dominant* constraint, not a decomposition into independent factors.

**Policy implication.** Any government proposing a re-entry instrument should be required to state, in advance, which stage it believes binds and on what evidence — and the only systems that can answer are the ones that publish a stage-level funnel. NSW's contacts-interest-placements sequence is the template, and it exists in a media release rather than an evaluation. The generalisable recommendation is not "fund conversion"; it is "publish the funnel, then fund the stage".

## 2. Conversion is decided at the entrance, not in the middle — which is where the money goes

**Claim.** Everything that determines whether an expressed interest becomes an appointment — who is admitted to the pool, and what kind of post is on offer — is fixed before the programme spends anything, and the spending is concentrated on a stage with a low marginal product.

**Mechanism and evidence.** England ran the experiment twice with different technology and got roughly the same conversion. The 2015-16 pilot bought subject-knowledge and refresher training through grant-funded school partnerships: £586,900 in grants, an average £1,425 per participant who started training, £17,800 per supported returner employed, and £37,400 per *additional* returner in a permanent EBacc secondary post once deadweight is assumed at half (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 56-58). Its successor, the Return to Teaching Advisory service, bought brokerage: an adviser, application and interview help, and signposting to vacancies. Over 2019-21 more than 600 former teachers in maths, physics and MFL were given an adviser and about 128 returns were recorded, at roughly £5,400 per recorded returner (UK Parliament, 2022; Walker, 2022; the per-head figure is own arithmetic from the published cost and return counts, and additionality is unknown). Set those against the pilot's 62 of 354 into any state post: the cheap advisory arm converted at about one in five, the expensive training arm at not quite one in five. The refresher training, which absorbed the money, did not move the outcome. What returners lacked was not capability but access to a post.

The point sharpens with the pilot's own counterfactual. The evaluation reports that around 4% of *non-supported* registrants returned over the same period (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 11-12). Applied to the roughly 5,300 registrants who received nothing, that is on the order of 210 people returning unaided, against 62 among the 354 who were supported (own arithmetic from the published figures). The evaluators rightly warn that the 18%-versus-4% gap overstates programme impact, because supported participants were more certain of returning to begin with. But the absolute comparison points the other way and is worth stating plainly: the untouched pool produced several times more returners than the funded one. A registration list is itself a policy asset; England spent its budget on 6% of that list.

Now compare entry conditions rather than services. NSW's cold list of teachers who had resigned within the previous five years produced 145 placements from 1,500 contacts — 9.7% of everyone contacted, most of whom had never asked to be. England's self-selected volunteers produced 62 state-school posts from 5,729 registrations — 1.1% (both figures own arithmetic from NSW Government, 2024a and Buchanan et al., 2018). A cold call beat a national advertising campaign by roughly nine to one per head of the pool. The difference is not effort or money; it is that NSW selected on recency and offered work that existed, while England selected on enthusiasm and offered a permanent secondary post in a shortage subject at a moment when few were vacant. The pilot's own finding that national advertising played a small role relative to online search (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 10-12) is the same result seen from the awareness end: campaigns recruit volume at the cold end of the distribution.

**What strains against this.** England's advisory service has no independent evaluation and no additionality estimate; its returns are self-recorded by the department, which also notes that returns in other subjects and unreported returns are not captured (UK Parliament, 2022). It is possible that the adviser service simply logged people who were returning anyway, in which case its apparent efficiency is an artefact. Equally, the pilot's training may have had value the placement statistics cannot see — confidence, subject currency — which would show up in later returns outside the measurement window. And NSW's 145 placements are casual and temporary, which is a lower bar than a permanent post; the comparison rewards NSW partly for having asked less.

**Policy implication.** Re-entry money should be spent in the order the funnel breaks, and on present evidence the first two pounds should go to selecting the pool and securing the post, not to retraining. Concretely: tie funded places to identified vacancies before recruitment opens (the REP correction), draw the pool from recent leavers on the employer's own records, and treat refresher training as a service purchased for people who already have an offer — which is exactly the sequencing New Zealand arrived at when it made the Teacher Education Refresh requirement contingent on *not* having a job offer (Teaching Council, 2026).

## 3. Return runs through the door the teacher left by

**Claim.** Re-entry is a relational transaction, not a market one: the strongest predictor of a return in the evidence is a surviving tie to a specific school or employer, and systems that possess such ties convert far better than systems that broadcast.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Three independent kinds of evidence converge. North Carolina's return-to-work window produced the largest documented re-entry episode in the corpus — about 3,500 returners, with just over 35% of the previously retirement-eligible returning to full-time work by 2009 — and **54% of them returned to the school they had taught in before retiring** (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Austria's qualitative study finds that keeping professional contact with the former school is what converts availability into an actual return, and that the decision is a biographical event rather than a labour-supply response (Resch, 2025, abstract-verified). NSW's funnel exists at all because the department could write to people it employed, using its own staffing records, and could package their re-accreditation itself (NSW Government, 2024a). Vorarlberg's small contribution is the most elegant version: re-entry information is sent out together with the retirement paperwork, at the moment the tie is still warm (APA/ORF, 2023). None of this is new advice — *Teachers Matter* recommended "maintaining contact with former teachers to keep them informed of educational developments and job opportunities" twenty years ago (OECD, 2005, pp. 88-89) — but it has been treated as a communications tip rather than as the mechanism it is.

Why would a relationship matter so much? Because it resolves, at zero cost, three of the seven conditions at once. It supplies awareness precisely when it is actionable; it substitutes for the screening that makes a school prefer a known quantity; and it makes the vacancy visible to the one person whose subject, level and location already fit, since they fitted before.

**What strains against this.** Contact is necessary and manifestly not sufficient, and Germany is the disproof of the naive version. Thirteen of sixteen Länder actively recruit retired teachers, Hesse alone wrote to about 7,000 pensioners in one school year, and just over 5,000 pensioners were teaching nationwide — under 1% of a workforce of some 800,000 (Deutsches Schulportal, 2023); Hesse's own handbook attributes roughly 550 filled posts to a package that combines retiree recruitment with service extensions and part-time top-ups, not to the letters alone (Hessisches Ministerium, 2024, p. 8). Vienna's 300 letters and Salzburg's contacting of every retiree up to age 67 belong to the same pattern of near-universal outreach and small yields (APA/ORF, 2023). A mailing is not a relationship. The distinction the paper needs is between *outreach*, which is a ministry broadcasting to a list, and *attachment*, which is a school holding a tie to a named person — and only the second has evidence behind it.

**Policy implication.** The operational unit of re-entry policy is the school, not the system. The cheapest instrument available to any government is an exit protocol: when a teacher resigns or retires, the school records the reason, the subject, the preferred future hours and consent to be contacted, and the school — not the ministry — holds and uses that record. Nothing in the corpus costs less or has better odds. The corresponding warning is that national campaigns should be sized as awareness-raising for the cold end of the pool, and evaluated accordingly, rather than as the recruitment channel.

## 4. The reserve that responds is a flow, not a stock — and returnability decays fast

**Claim.** The convertible population is not the accumulated stock of qualified non-teachers but the recent flow of leavers; the probability of return declines sharply with time out of the classroom, which is why stock-based arithmetic has repeatedly over-promised.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Three things decay together: the licence, the network and the professional identity. Michigan makes the first visible — of roughly 61,000 certified teachers not teaching, 70% had not taught in a public school at any point over the previous five years and 44-49% held expired certificates, against 6% of active teachers (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021). The longitudinal US literature makes the second and third visible in the aggregate: eventual return fell from about one-third of leavers in the NLSY79 cohort, and a quarter to a half in earlier cohort studies, to about one-fifth for those exiting between 2000 and 2019 (Grissom and Reininger, 2012; Moyer, 2022). NFER's typology says the same thing in one line: career breakers — the group whose exit was explicitly temporary and recent — returned at about double the rate of the other four types (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018).

Policymakers have quietly acted on this without saying so. Portugal restricted Article 5 to those retired five years or less; NSW contacted those who had resigned within five years; Hesse targeted the "recently retired"; DUO defines the Dutch silent reserve as those out for at least two years, and reports it shrinking by 17-18% over a decade (DUO, 2026). And where a system relies on the stock, it depletes: Austria's audited returns peaked in 2021/22 and 2022/23 and then fell, with the Court of Audit stressing the temporary character of the measure (Rechnungshof, 2025, pp. 24-25), even though the shortage did not go away. Japan shows the terminal state of a reserve made of retirees: retired teachers make up roughly 60% of part-time adjunct hires in the prefecture studied in depth, but only about 35% of age-based retirees in elementary schools now seek full-time re-employment (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024, pp. 201, 203).

The arithmetic consequence is severe. The Dutch claim that reactivating 22% of a 62,000-strong hidden reserve would close the shortage (Somers, Groot and van Merode, 2024) is a statement about a stock, and the country's own activation instrument was sized at 500 people — 1.6% of the 31,000 primary-education reserve its own explanatory memorandum cited. The gap between the arithmetic and the ambition is the clearest available signal that the officials writing the scheme did not believe the stock was convertible.

**What strains against this.** Two things. First, natural return from the stock is large and continuous where it is measured: about 17,000 teachers return to English state schools each year, roughly one-third of all new entrants (DfE, 2023), and re-entrants make up 37-49% of new hires in the United States (Sutcher, Darling-Hammond and Carver-Thomas, 2016). The stock is not inert; it is being drawn on constantly without programmes. Second, decay is confounded with selection: the people who return quickly may be those who always intended to, so the falling hazard may reflect who is left rather than deterioration in those who remain.

**Policy implication.** This inverts the standard framing. Where re-entry programmes could have a marginal product is not in *starting* return — the market already does that at scale — but in *steering* it toward shortage subjects, hard-to-staff schools and the moments when the flow is warm. North Carolina's returners went disproportionately to high-need schools with no mandate requiring it (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024); China conditions its allowance on service in poverty-lifted, minority and border counties (MOE et al., 2023); Portugal narrowed its supplement to shortage zones in 2026 after conceding the blanket version "in many cases was not justified" (RTP, 2026). Georgia is the counter-example that clarifies the rule: it tried to steer by prohibition — restricting eligibility to the three highest-need subject areas in each region — and the audit found the screens blocked hires rather than redirecting them (Georgia DOAA, 2025). Steering by incentive works; steering by prohibition suppresses.

## 5. The salary schedule is a two-sided trap, and only a third-party payer escapes it

**Claim.** The single most under-analysed barrier in the corpus is not licensure but the experience-based salary schedule, which makes a returner more expensive than the alternative hire; every system that has solved the school's cost problem has done so by making the returner poorer, and the one system that produced mass return did so by having a third party — the pension fund — pay the difference.

**Mechanism and evidence.** On a seniority scale, a returner with twenty years of credited service costs the school far more than a newly qualified teacher for the same timetable. Georgia's audit puts a number on it: rehired retirees earned an average of USD 78,487 in FY2023-24, reflecting placement high on the state schedule, with employers also required to pay both employer and employee contributions to the retirement system (Georgia DOAA, 2025). Ontario's supply market shows the same arithmetic in daily rates — retired teachers were paid CAD 432.69 a day against CAD 227.36 for new teachers, and the province's ten largest boards spent CAD 108.3 million a year on retired supply teachers while roughly 12,774 newly certified teachers chased about 6,500 annual openings (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). That investigation is why the re-employment cap was *cut* from 95 days to 50 in 2012.

Systems that want returners to be affordable therefore reset them to the bottom of the scale — and destroy willingness in the process. Austria excludes from prior-service crediting any period that already generates a pension, so a returning former civil-servant teacher draws the full pension plus the teacher *starting* salary, while a former contract teacher, whose service is credited, essentially recovers pre-retirement pay (BMBWF, 2023, answer to Question 19, p. 18). The penalty thus falls precisely on the largest and most senior part of the retiring cohort. Portugal does the same thing by design: Article 5 returners receive their pension plus compensation indexed to the *first step* of the career scale, pro-rated by teaching hours (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5). Both systems then recorded weak take-up, and Portugal's could not fill its quota.

North Carolina is the exception that proves the mechanism. It did not ask the school to pay more or the teacher to accept less: it let retirees draw full salary *and* their pension concurrently — up to roughly 50% more income than a regular full-time teacher — with the pension annuity being, as the authors argue, a cost the state was paying anyway (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). The school got a teacher at schedule cost; the teacher got a premium; the pension fund financed the gap. That is why North Carolina moved a third of its retirement-eligible cohort while Portugal moved 63 people, and it is a far better explanation than any claim about national attitudes to teaching. Note too what happened when the window closed in 2010: returns dropped to zero. This was a price response, not a change of heart.

**What strains against this.** Permissiveness alone does not reproduce North Carolina's result. New Mexico removed salary and hour limits entirely, and 154 retirees used the new route in its first year out of roughly 48,000 potentially eligible, with the great majority of the 2,792 working retirees sitting in the low-hours and low-earnings tiers (NMERB, 2023; NM HED, 2022). Removing a penalty is not the same as paying a premium, and the denominators again differ — North Carolina's 35% is measured against the recent flow of retirement-eligible teachers, New Mexico's against a whole retiree stock. Fitzpatrick's (2015) finding that teachers value current wages roughly five to one over pension wealth reinforces the direction: what moves people is salient current cash, not adjustments to deferred entitlements. And the equity objection is real and documented: Ontario's federation refused a fourth extension of the raised cap in 2024 with the argument that the pension plan "is not a tool for addressing labour market challenges" (Alphonso, 2024; OCEOTA, 2024).

**Policy implication.** Re-entry finance should be designed as a question of *who pays the seniority premium*. Three answers exist: the school (which then declines to hire), the returner (who then declines to come), or a third party. Only the third has produced scale. That makes the pension system — not the education ministry — the decisive institution in re-entry policy, and it puts the equity guardrail in the same place: the same lever that unlocks return also displaces new graduates, which is why the Ontario dial has moved in both directions.

## 6. The market that absorbs returners is the casual one, for reasons of price and calendar — and that is uncomfortable

**Claim.** Returners are systematically absorbed into relief, casual and substitute work and systematically rejected by permanent appointment processes; this is the clearest regularity in the corpus, and it sits awkwardly with the paper's premise that former teachers matter for the craft and mentoring capacity they bring back.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Two features of permanent hiring exclude returners almost mechanically. The first is price, as above. The second is the calendar: permanent posts are filled in an annual cycle, and a returner's readiness is not synchronised with it. England's evaluation names timing — support delivered after the hiring cycle — as a principal cause of failure (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 91), and its successor tied funded places to actual vacancies (NCTL, 2017). Portugal's applications ran in the first days of October 2024 and its first placements began in January 2025, after the annual placement rounds, with candidates ranked in a central pool by their professional graduation mark at retirement and allocated where shortages were (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Art. 5; Renascença, 2024b) — an entry route that is late, impersonal and geographically indifferent, which is close to a design specification for deterring a 68-year-old.

Casual and relief work has neither problem. It is priced by the day, has no cycle, and is transacted by a principal who needs someone on Tuesday. So that is where returners go. NSW placed its 145 into casual and temporary roles (NSW Government, 2024a). New Zealand's on-ramp is relief teaching — twenty half-days a year without a current practising certificate, plus a refresh waiver triggered by a relief job offer (Education and Training Act 2020, Sch. 3; Ministry of Education, 2026a; Teaching Council, 2026). The Bahamas built its reservist scheme on the existing supply-teacher platform, formalising a reliance on retirees that the union says was already there (ZNS Bahamas, 2026a; Munnings, 2026). Japan's retirees are about 60% of adjunct hires (Sakuma and Shimazaki, 2024). Ireland's annual suspension of career-break restrictions exists to release those teachers into substitute work (Department of Education and Youth, 2025a). Ontario's whole regulatory apparatus is about the casual market. And Ontario shows that when returners do compete there, they win: at one board, retirees took 2,861 supply assignments against 1,638 for new graduates (Hammer and Alphonso, 2010). Principals prefer the known, experienced, immediately deployable person for short, urgent work — and prefer the cheaper, calendar-synchronised novice for the permanent post.

**What strains against this.** The casual channel is exactly the one that wastes what the paper says former teachers bring. It offers no continuity with a class, no induction, no mentoring role and no development function; *Teachers Matter* recommended induction for returners precisely because returning is not the same as never having left (OECD, 2005, p. 121). It is also the channel most exposed to the crowding-out objection that closed it in Ontario. If the paper's case for former teachers rests on craft, curriculum knowledge and mentoring capacity, then the empirically available route delivers the people while stripping the value — and the routes designed to capture the value are the ones with the best outcome evidence but almost no policy uptake: structured tutoring by retired and substitute teachers reproduced an effect of +0.34 on a mathematics assessment at scale (Gersten et al., 2015; Rolfhus et al., 2012), and Experience Corps produced reading gains of 0.13-0.16 alongside documented health benefits for the older volunteers themselves (Morrow-Howell et al., 2009; WWC, 2010). The one randomised trial of retirees as mentors to new teachers is a qualified null under WWC standards, with no significant effects on reading, mathematics or teacher retention (DeCesare, McClelland and Randel, 2017; WWC, 2019) — so the mentoring case must be argued on instructional support, not on retaining the mentees.

**Policy implication.** A system that wants former teachers for what they carry should stop trying to insert them into the permanent appointment process, where they lose on price and timing, and should instead design the roles where their comparative advantage is real and the transaction is short: structured tutoring, subject-specialist support, supervised relief attached to a single school rather than a pool. That is also the honest way to reconcile the paper's premise with the evidence, rather than pretending the classroom-return route is working.

## 7. The funnel does not end at appointment: returners leak faster than novices

**Claim.** Retention in post is a stage of the pathway, not an afterthought, and it is the stage that turns a favourable cost comparison into an unfavourable one.

**Mechanism and evidence.** England is the only system in the corpus with the linked data to see this, and the result is unfavourable: of the 2011 returner cohort, 32% were out of service four years later, plus 2% retired, against 29% of the 2011 newly-qualified cohort, and departmental analysis found the odds of a returner leaving after their first year back to be 60% higher than average (Buchanan et al., 2018, p. 57). The mechanism is not mysterious. Some returners re-enter through the least supported door (casual work); some return to precisely the conditions they left; and none receive induction, because induction is reserved for beginners.

This wrecks the cost comparison the evaluation itself invites. NFER set £37,400 per additional returner against £33,300-46,900 per newly trained teacher employed after a year (Buchanan et al., 2018, pp. 56-58), and the rejected draft repeated the pair of numbers as if they settled something. They do not, because the two denominators buy different quantities of teaching: the trainee is a longer-lived asset in the sector at the moment of purchase. Costed per expected year of service, the returner in a permanent classroom post is plausibly the worse buy — an uncomfortable conclusion, but one the paper is better off stating than leaving for a reviewer to state.

**What strains against this.** The comparison cuts the other way on other dimensions the cost figures ignore. The returner arrives with classroom craft the trainee does not have, needs no training place, and — in North Carolina — flowed to schools with more economically disadvantaged and more Black students than average without any policy requiring it (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). Some of the excess attrition is by design and not a failure: retirees returning for a defined emergency, career breakers bridging a gap, and Austria's returns tapering after the crisis are all consistent with a valve working as intended (Rechnungshof, 2025). The paper should distinguish a returner recruited as a permanent replacement, for whom the retention gap is a real cost, from one recruited as a buffer, for whom it is the point.

**Policy implication.** Two things follow. Induction must be extended to returners, as *Teachers Matter* recommended in 2005 (OECD, 2005, p. 121) and as no system in the corpus has been shown to do. And every re-entry instrument should report survival in post at one and three years, not placements — otherwise a programme is scored on the moment of appointment, which is the moment its problems begin.

## 8. Why interest so vastly exceeds conversion

The first six sections explain the mechanics. This one addresses the motive, because the mechanics alone would not produce a ten-to-one gap.

**Claim.** Expressed interest and appointment are not two points on the same scale. Interest is an expression of professional identity and costs nothing; appointment is a labour-market transaction that requires the person to give back precisely what they left to obtain. Systems have been reading the first as a forecast of the second, and it never was one.

**Mechanism and evidence.** Start with the cleanest measurement of the gap. In Australia, more than half of registered teachers not working in schools said they would consider returning "under the right circumstances", while 5% were actively looking (AITSL, 2025). That ten-to-one ratio between conditional willingness and revealed search is roughly the ratio the programme funnels then reproduce. It is not that the surveys are wrong; it is that "would consider under the right circumstances" is a true statement about identity and a null statement about supply.

Then ask what the right circumstances would have to beat. This is where the corpus is at its most decisive and where the paper has so far been at its most timid. The reserve is not a queue of people waiting for a door to open; it is composed of people who opened a door and walked through it, and who are, on their own accounts, better off. Australian panel evidence shows leavers' job satisfaction 0.13 standard deviations higher one year after exit and 0.19 higher after three years, while weekly wages fall 6.2% at one year and 9.4% at three and hourly wages remain lower for a decade (Cuervo and Vera-Toscano, 2025, pp. 9-11). They paid for the exit and consider it worth the price. Dutch inactive teachers rate several working conditions in their current jobs more favourably than active teachers rate theirs, and say what would bring them back is lower work pressure, more control over hours and more autonomy — not primarily salary (Somers et al., 2024). US linked administrative and tax data show more than half of leavers remaining in education broadly defined, about a fifth leaving the labour force, and mean earnings still slightly below pre-exit levels eight years later (Brummet et al., 2024). In the classic cohort evidence, about two-thirds of exiting female teachers left the labour force altogether rather than switching employer (Stinebrickner, 2002). None of these are the profiles of people who were poached and might be poached back.

This is why Portugal is the corpus's best natural experiment, and why the paper should build a section on it rather than a bullet. One decree, one year, one government, one population of pension-eligible teachers, two margins. Article 5 offered a return: re-enter, be ranked in a central pool, be placed where the shortage is, on a fixed-term contract at first-step-indexed pay pro-rated by hours. Article 6 offered continuation: stay where you are, keep doing what you are doing, receive EUR 750 a month. Sixty-three people took the first; about 2,200 took the second (Decreto-Lei n.º 51/2024, Arts. 5-6; DN, 2024; RTP, 2026). The asymmetry is not evidence that returners rejected EUR 750 — they were never offered it — but something more useful: at the same moment, in the same system, an offer that required no transition was thirty-five times more effective than one that required a full re-entry. The friction being priced is the transition itself, not the classroom.

Two further readings support the identity account. NFER's returner typology found the largest segment to be "idealists" (35%), people responding to a vocational call, while the segment that actually converted at double the rate was "career breakers" (11%), people whose exit had always been temporary and who wanted part-time work (Buchanan et al., 2018; Worth and Sharp, 2018). A campaign that recruits idealists harvests attachment; a scheme that targets career breakers harvests intention. And the Austrian interview evidence describes return as a biographical event shaped by individual, family, school and system factors rather than as a response to pay (Resch, 2025; Resch, 2025b). These people are not deciding whether to accept a wage; they are deciding whether to resume being a particular kind of person.

**What strains against this.** The stated-preference evidence is not uniform, and the paper should print the split rather than smooth it. Michigan's certificate-holders lead with money: a third selected wanting a higher salary among their top three reasons for not teaching, and a salary increase was the most frequently chosen incentive to return (Lindsay, Gnedko-Berry and Wan, 2021) — on a 30% response rate from an explicitly non-representative sample. And money does move people when it is large enough and current enough: North Carolina's roughly 50% income premium moved a third of the eligible cohort. The honest formulation is therefore not "money does not work" but that the money must be large enough to compensate a transition the person has already priced and rejected once — which is why a EUR 750 supplement retains and a pension-plus-salary stack returns.

**What it would take to settle it.** The corpus cannot currently distinguish cheap talk from unmet demand, because almost every system measures willingness with a costless signal — a registration, a survey tick, a reply to a letter. The test is straightforward and no one has run it: measure interest with a costly signal (an application to a named post with a named start date), and report the conversion from *that* denominator. NSW comes closest by starting from a cold list, which at least removes self-selection. Until then, registration counts should be reported as marketing metrics, not as estimates of supply, and the paper should say so.

## 9. What the evidence cannot settle, and what would settle it

Four questions matter for the mechanism and cannot be answered from the corpus as it stands.

**Does removing licensure friction move returns?** It is the most commonly recommended fix and the least evidenced. New Zealand ran the closest thing to a clean natural experiment — a refresh waiver for returners with a relief job offer, introduced, repeatedly extended, and finally mainstreamed into the registration policy, alongside government-paid certification fees to 2028 — and published no uptake figures and no evaluation whatever (Ministry of Education, 2026a; Teaching Council, 2026). Michigan quantifies the size of the friction (44-49% of the non-teaching certified hold expired certificates) but not its causal weight. To settle it: publish counts of re-certifications and subsequent appointments before and after a waiver, which every regulator already holds.

**Does the returner teach as well as the alternative hire?** No study anywhere in the corpus links returners to student outcomes; the North Carolina authors state explicitly that they could not, having no vacancy data and no counterfactual (Jarrold-Grapes and Mahler, 2024). This is a serious hole beneath the paper's own premise that former teachers bring something back, and it should be named. The available outcome evidence concerns retirees in *tutoring* roles, not returners in classrooms.

**What is the additionality of any of this?** Exactly one estimate exists, and it is an assumption rather than a measurement: NFER's £37,400 rests on supposing half the 27 would have returned anyway (Buchanan et al., 2018). NSW published no counterfactual and stopped reporting after May 2024, disappearing from the department's next annual report entirely (NSW Department of Education, 2025). The Dutch subsidy lapsed without evaluation. To settle it: stagger the rollout of any brokerage or subsidy across regions and compare, or match participants to the register — England's own 60%-higher-attrition finding proves the linked data exist.

**Which stage do the unmeasured systems break at?** France, Ireland, Louisiana, Virginia, Michigan, New Zealand and China publish enabling rules and no counts (cases_other_legislation; cases_us_legislation; ch5_synthesis §C6, Lesson 12). The OECD has already made the point institutionally: "many education systems may lack accurate data on the number and characteristics of potential returnees and their motivations, which could limit their ability to capitalise on this opportunity strategically" (OECD, 2024, p. 51). The recommendation that follows from the whole mechanism argument is a reporting standard, not a programme: contacted, eligible, applied, offered, appointed, still in post at one and three years — six numbers, all of which every system already generates and almost none of which any system publishes.

## CANDIDATE PROPOSITIONS FOR THE PAPER

1. **The funnel does not collapse in one place: each system's design decides which stage bears the loss** — England lost its returners at vacancy match, NSW at willingness, Portugal at application, the Netherlands at the school's absorptive capacity, Georgia at statutory eligibility — so the useful comparison is not conversion rates but stages, and no government should fund an instrument without first naming the stage it believes binds.

2. **Conversion is determined at the entrance to the funnel, by who is admitted and what post is on offer, while the money is spent in the middle** — England's expensive refresher-training arm and its cheap advisory arm converted at roughly the same rate, and a cold list of recent leavers in New South Wales outperformed a national campaign's self-selected volunteers by an order of magnitude per head of the pool.

3. **Re-entry is a relational transaction, not a market one** — 54% of North Carolina's returners went back to the school they had left, Austrian returners are converted by continuing contact with their former school, and NSW's funnel existed only because the employer held the records — so the operational unit of re-entry policy is the school and its exit protocol, not the ministry and its campaign, while Germany's 7,000 letters for under 1% of the workforce show that broadcasting is not the same as attachment.

4. **The seniority salary schedule, not licensure, is the decisive barrier, and only a third-party payer escapes it** — a returner costs a school more than a novice, so systems that make the school pay get no hires and systems that reset returners to the starting salary (Austria's civil servants, Portugal's first-step index) get no takers; North Carolina alone produced mass return, by letting the pension fund finance the premium, and returns fell to zero the moment that window closed.

5. **Expressed interest measures professional identity, not labour supply, because the reserve is made of people who chose to leave and are better off for it** — more than half of Australia's non-deployed registrants would consider returning while 5% are actually looking, leavers' job satisfaction rises while their wages fall, and Portugal's single decree moved about 2,200 teachers to postpone retirement against 63 who came back, which is the cleanest available demonstration that what re-entry policy is really pricing is the transition itself.
